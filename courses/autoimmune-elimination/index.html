Lesson 1

Welcome & How This Works

WhenPrep Day 1

Step 1 of 13

Welcome! We’re so glad you’re here, and we’re excited for the journey you’re starting today and for the results it’s going to bring you.

Over the next several weeks you’ll calm your system, give your body the space it needs to heal, and discover exactly which foods work for you and which ones don’t.

How it’s going to go

  • Prep Week — you’ll learn what drives inflammation, clear out your kitchen and stock up, so Day 1 feels easy.
  • Elimination (30+ days) — you’ll eat only Yes foods and log how you feel while your body calms down.
  • Reintroduction — you’ll bring foods back one at a time to find your exact triggers.
  • Personalize — you’ll build the way of eating that’s right for your body.

The more you stick to it, the better your results

This protocol works because it’s clean. Every “just this once” makes it harder for your body to settle and harder to read your results. The more fully you follow it, the clearer your answers and the bigger the change you’ll feel.

This isn’t forever

This is not meant to be a permanent diet. It’s a reset that lets your system calm down and heal. Afterward you’ll still want to eat better than you may have been before, but you’ll do it with something most people never have: a clear map of your own triggers. That knowledge helps keep your symptoms from flaring in the future and lets your body run the way it was designed to.

Let’s get started.

This diet focuses on non-processed, natural and organic food sources — foods that would have been accessible to humans living off the land and farming their own food. Clean grass-fed, pasture-raised meats, wild fish, vegetables, fruit and berries.

It is our most focused protocol. It starts from the Anti-Inflammatory Diet and goes one step further: for a set period of time we also remove the foods that are most likely to provoke the immune system or irritate the gut in people with autoimmune conditions or stubborn symptoms — nightshades, eggs, nuts, seeds, seed spices, legumes, erythritol, chocolate and coffee. Then we bring them back one at a time to learn exactly which ones your body does and doesn’t tolerate.

Changing your diet can feel overwhelming. The goal is not perfection — it is to drastically reduce the load on your body and eat foods that nourish and restore balance to your digestive, nervous and immune systems.

Why an elimination diet?

Food reactions are rarely obvious. Many don’t show up as hives or an upset stomach within minutes — they show up as joint pain the next day, brain fog, fatigue, a skin flare, poor sleep or bloating two days later. When you eat a dozen different foods a day, it is impossible to tell which one is responsible.

An elimination diet solves that problem the same way a good experiment does: remove the variables, let the body calm down and establish a clean baseline, then test one variable at a time. Lab tests for food sensitivities can be a helpful clue, but a careful elimination and reintroduction remains the most direct way to see how your body responds to a food.

What the research shows Small clinical studies of this style of protocol (often called the Autoimmune Protocol, or AIP) have reported meaningful results. In one study of adults with active inflammatory bowel disease, most participants reached clinical remission by week 6 and maintained it while following the diet. In a study of women with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, quality-of-life scores improved substantially and the inflammatory marker CRP dropped over 10 weeks. These studies were small and uncontrolled, so they show promise rather than proof — which is exactly why the reintroduction phase and your own tracking matter so much.
Konijeti et al., Inflamm Bowel Dis 2017; Abbott et al., Cureus 2019

This protocol happens in four phases, in a set order. Each phase has its own job, and skipping ahead is the most common reason people don’t get clear answers about their triggers.

  • Prepare — set yourself up so Day 1 isn’t overwhelming.
  • Eliminate — remove every likely trigger at once so your body can calm down and you can see your true baseline.
  • Reintroduce — test foods one at a time to learn exactly which ones affect you.
  • Personalize — turn what you learned into the way you eat long term.

Plan on about a week to prepare, at least 30 days of elimination, then several weeks to a few months of reintroduction, since each food takes 3–5 days to test. Now let’s walk through each phase and exactly what you’ll do in it.

Food reactions aren’t always obvious. Use this guide to know what to notice, then score it honestly.

What to watch for

Digestion — bloating, gas, belching, reflux or heartburn, nausea, cramping, constipation, diarrhea or urgency, changes in your bowel movements

Joints & muscles — pain, stiffness (especially in the morning), swelling, aches, weakness

Energy & brain — fatigue, an energy crash after meals, brain fog, poor focus, trouble finding words, dizziness

Mood & sleep — anxiety, irritability, low mood, trouble falling or staying asleep, waking unrefreshed

Skin — rashes, eczema, acne, itching, hives, flushing, psoriasis flares

Head, sinus & mouth — headaches or migraines, congestion, sneezing, post-nasal drip, itchy mouth or throat, mouth sores, ringing ears

Heart & fluid — racing heart after eating, puffiness, water retention (rings or socks feel tight)

Cravings & appetite — strong cravings, never feeling full

Timing

  • Some reactions show up within minutes. Many take 24–72 hours. That’s why you score every day, not just after meals.

Adjustment symptoms in the first week

  • Headaches, tiredness, irritability and cravings around Days 2–7 are usually your body coming off sugar and caffeine, not a food reaction. They typically ease by Days 7–10. Drink water with a pinch of sea salt, eat enough protein and rest.

Add your own condition’s symptoms

  • Thyroid (Hashimoto’s): cold hands and feet, hair shedding, dry skin, puffiness
  • Rheumatoid arthritis: minutes of morning stiffness, number of swollen joints
  • Psoriasis or eczema: size of the flare, itch level
  • IBD or IBS: number of bowel movements, urgency
  • Lupus: joint pain, rash, fatigue  ·  MS: fatigue, numbness or tingling

How to score

  • 1 = no symptoms  ·  10 = the worst you can imagine.
  • Score at the same times each day and compare against your own scores, not how you think you should feel.
  • Write down anything unusual — a new symptom counts too.
Get medical help for blood in your stool, unexplained weight loss, or a fever. Call emergency services for swelling of the lips, tongue or throat, or trouble breathing.

Lesson 2

What Drives Inflammation

WhenPrep Days 1–2

Step 1 of 15

Below you’ll go through the 12 biggest drivers of inflammation one at a time: lifestyle and environment first, then foods and ingredients, then the gut. For each one you’ll learn why it matters and what you can do about it.

Food is only one piece. Inflammation is the sum of everything the body has to defend against or clean up. Lowering the total load is what gives the immune system room to calm down.

Lifestyle & environment
🌫

Stress

Stress is one of the most underplayed causes of inflammation in the body. We all know it’s bad for us, but few of us really understand the massive toll it takes.

How stress becomes inflammation

The brain and body respond to emotional stress much as they do to physical injury. They send a cascade of chemicals, immune cells and inflammatory signals as if there were damaged tissue to repair. But where is the damage? Instead of a local repair, the result is a widespread immune and inflammatory response.

  • Fight-or-flight gets stuck on. Extreme or chronic stress keeps the nervous system in a constant state of alarm, which dysregulates the immune system and hormones.
  • Cortisol stops working. Cortisol normally switches inflammation off. Under long-term stress, immune cells become resistant to it — so inflammation keeps running even though cortisol is high.
  • The gut becomes leaky. Stress hormones activate immune cells in the gut wall that loosen the tight junctions. In one study, simply preparing for a public speech measurably increased intestinal permeability.
  • Stress creates dysbiosis. Stress changes gut motility, mucus and acid, shifting the balance of gut bacteria.
  • Sleep suffers. Poor sleep on its own raises inflammatory markers such as IL-6 and CRP, compounding the effect.

Stress and autoimmune disease

  • In reviews of autoimmune patients, up to 80% report an unusually stressful emotional event before their disease began.
  • A study of more than 100,000 people found those diagnosed with stress-related disorders (such as PTSD) were significantly more likely to develop an autoimmune disease later.
  • Adverse childhood experiences (abuse, neglect, household dysfunction) are linked to higher rates of autoimmune disease decades later.

What you can do

I cannot emphasize enough how paramount it is that we deal with emotional stress, trauma, negative patterns and memories, and learn to regulate our nervous systems. If your nervous and immune systems are dysregulated, it is unlikely you will get well with any other efforts.

  • Do the mindset, meditation and emotional work in this program — it is part of your protocol, not an extra
  • Practice slow breathing before meals and at bedtime to shift into “rest and digest”
  • Protect your sleep: consistent bedtime, dark room, morning light
  • Track your daily stress level — a “food reaction” may actually be a hard day
What the research shows
  • People under chronic stress developed resistance to cortisol, failed to turn off inflammation, and were more likely to get sick when exposed to a cold virus (Cohen et al., PNAS 2012).
  • Acute psychological stress increased intestinal permeability in healthy volunteers, driven by the stress hormone CRH (Vanuytsel et al., Gut 2014).
  • Stress-related disorders were associated with a higher risk of 41 autoimmune diseases (Song et al., JAMA 2018).
  • Childhood adversity increased the risk of autoimmune disease hospitalization in adulthood (Dube et al., Psychosom Med 2009).

Also: Stojanovich & Marisavljevich, Autoimmun Rev 2008

Food Additives

Most additives are used to increase flavor, desirability and shelf life — not nutrition. Many have never been tested the way you might assume.

How many are there?

  • More than 10,000 substances are allowed in US food (10,787 as of 2011).
  • That’s only what the FDA knows about. Companies can declare their own ingredient “generally recognized as safe” (GRAS) without telling the FDA. Researchers estimated about 1,000 additives may be in our food this way with no FDA record.
  • Only about 1 in 5 additives directly added to food has the feeding studies needed to estimate a safe dose.
  • Additives are studied one at a time — almost never in the combinations we actually eat them.

Why they drive inflammation

  • Emulsifiers thin the gut’s protective mucus. Carboxymethylcellulose and polysorbate 80 (used in ice cream, dressings and sauces) let bacteria reach the gut lining, causing low-grade inflammation, colitis and metabolic syndrome in mice. A human trial found carboxymethylcellulose changed gut bacteria and reduced beneficial compounds.
  • Carrageenan (a thickener in nut milks, deli meat and coconut products) triggers inflammation in cell and animal studies.
  • Artificial dyes increased hyperactivity in children in a controlled trial. Red Dye No. 3 lost its US food approval in 2025 after being linked to cancer in animals.
  • Titanium dioxide (a whitener in candy and frosting) was banned in the EU in 2022 over concerns it may damage DNA. It is still allowed in the US.
  • Preservatives and flavor enhancers such as nitrites, BHA/BHT and MSG can provoke reactions in sensitive people.

What you can do

  • Avoid chain restaurant foods and processed foods — this alone drastically decreases your intake
  • Choose foods with one ingredient, or a short list of words you recognize
  • Watch for: gums, “natural flavors,” carrageenan, polysorbate, cellulose, colors with numbers, maltodextrin
What the research shows
  • Emulsifiers promoted colitis and metabolic syndrome by disrupting the gut mucus layer and microbiota in mice (Chassaing et al., Nature 2015).
  • A randomized controlled feeding trial found carboxymethylcellulose altered the human gut microbiota (Chassaing et al., Gastroenterology 2022).
  • Artificial food colors and a preservative increased hyperactivity in 3- and 8/9-year-olds (McCann et al., Lancet 2007).

Substance counts: Neltner et al., Compr Rev Food Sci Food Saf 2011; Neltner et al., Reprod Toxicol 2013

Pesticides / Herbicides

These are products designed to poison and kill unwanted plants and pests — in other words, to kill life. It is not a mystery that they can affect our own cells and the trillions of microbes living in us.

How much are we exposed to?

  • The US uses roughly 1 billion pounds of pesticides a year.
  • Glyphosate (Roundup), the most widely used herbicide in the world, was found in the urine of about 80% of Americans tested by the CDC.
  • It is sprayed on many non-organic grains (wheat, oats) and legumes right before harvest to dry them out, which leaves more residue on the food.

Why they drive inflammation

  • They harm gut bacteria. Glyphosate works by blocking an enzyme pathway plants use — and many bacteria use the same pathway. About 54% of core human gut bacteria species are potentially sensitive to it. Glyphosate has been patented as an antimicrobial.
  • They bind minerals. Glyphosate was first patented as a chelator, a chemical that grabs onto minerals like zinc, manganese and magnesium.
  • Some share an origin with war chemicals. Organophosphate insecticides came out of the same WWII-era chemistry as nerve gases. Agent Orange, used in Vietnam, was made from two herbicides, one of which (2,4-D) is still sprayed today.
  • They disrupt hormones and create oxidative stress, adding to the body’s overall burden.

What you can do

Buying organic is more important than many assume, and buying locally grown from a regenerative farm that rotates small crops and animals is ideal if you are able.

  • If organic everything feels overwhelming and expensive, start with the Dirty Dozen list (the most-sprayed produce) and buy those organic
  • Find local farmers who sell grass-fed, organic meat
  • Wash produce well; peel non-organic produce where you can
  • Filter your water
What the research shows
  • Switching to an organic diet for just 6 days cut urinary glyphosate by about 70% and overall pesticide levels by about 60% (Hyland et al., Environ Res 2019).
  • Bioinformatic analysis found 54% of core human gut bacterial species carry the glyphosate-sensitive form of the target enzyme (Leino et al., J Hazard Mater 2020).

Also: US Patents 3,160,632 (1964) and 7,771,736 (2010); EPA Pesticides Industry Sales & Usage report; CDC NHANES

Plastics / Forever Chemicals (PFAS)

Our modern world has done wonders for convenience, but plastics and “forever chemicals” have taken a toll on the human body. The bombardment becomes too much for it to process, disrupting our hormones, cell communication and overall health.

Plastic in the brain

  • A 2025 study of autopsied brains found far more microplastic in the brain than in the liver or kidney — 7 to 30 times more.
  • Brain levels rose about 50% between 2016 and 2024. The 2024 average worked out to roughly a plastic spoon’s worth of plastic.
  • Brains from people with dementia contained several times more plastic than normal brains.
  • Most of it was polyethylene — the plastic in bags, wraps and bottles.

Plastic in the heart and blood

  • Microplastics have been found in human blood, placenta and artery plaque.
  • People with microplastics in their neck-artery plaque had a much higher risk of heart attack, stroke or death over the next three years.

Where it comes from

  • Heating food in plastic. Microwaving plastic baby-food containers released over 2 billion nanoplastic and 4 million microplastic particles per square centimeter in some cases.
  • Bottled water — about 240,000 plastic particles per liter, mostly nanoplastics.
  • Plastic tea bags — billions of particles released into a single cup.
  • Food packaging, non-stick pans, take-out containers, synthetic clothing dust

Forever chemicals (PFAS)

  • PFAS are found in the blood of nearly all Americans (about 97%).
  • They’re called “forever” because they barely break down — in the body or the environment.
  • Sources: non-stick cookware, stain- and water-resistant fabrics, grease-proof food wrappers, some tap water.
  • Plastics also release hormone disruptors like BPA and phthalates, which mimic or block estrogen and testosterone.

What you can do

  • Never heat food in plastic — use glass or ceramic
  • Store food in glass or stainless steel
  • Drink filtered water from glass or stainless steel, not plastic bottles
  • Swap non-stick pans for cast iron, stainless steel or ceramic
  • Use loose-leaf tea or paper tea bags
What the research shows
  • Brain samples from 2024 had a median of about 4,900 µg of micro- and nanoplastic per gram — roughly 0.5% by weight — and dementia brains far more (Nihart et al., Nature Medicine 2025). Some scientists caution the measurement method may overestimate amounts.
  • Microplastics in carotid plaque were linked to a 4.5-fold higher risk of heart attack, stroke or death (Marfella et al., N Engl J Med 2024).
  • Microwaving plastic containers released billions of nanoplastics per cm², and those particles killed up to 75% of cultured kidney cells (Hussain et al., Environ Sci Technol 2023).

Also: Qian et al., PNAS 2024 (bottled water); Hernández et al., Environ Sci Technol 2019 (tea bags); CDC NHANES (PFAS)

Heavy Metals

Heavy metals are natural metals our bodies don’t handle well. They embed in our tissues, disrupting cell function and hormone signaling. Due to industrial mining, coal burning and food processing, they are in our environment more than ever.

Why they drive inflammation

  • They impersonate minerals. Lead takes the place of calcium, cadmium takes the place of zinc — jamming the enzymes and cells that depend on those minerals.
  • They create oxidative stress and use up glutathione, the body’s master antioxidant and detoxifier.
  • They can trigger autoimmunity. Mercury and other metals induce autoimmune responses in animal studies and are linked to autoimmunity in people.
  • They disrupt hormones, including thyroid function.

Common sources

  • Large predatory fish like tuna, swordfish, shark and king mackerel (mercury)
  • Drinking water — lead, arsenic or cadmium from pollution or old plumbing. Do not assume your water is clean: treated city water, filtered water and even mountain spring water can all be contaminated.
  • Rice, especially brown rice — absorbs arsenic from soil and water
  • Processed foods — trace cadmium or lead from production or packaging
  • Cigarette smoke — cadmium and lead that accumulate over time
  • Also: some protein powders, dark chocolate, old paint, some imported spices and cookware, and silver (amalgam) dental fillings

What you can do

  • Get a high-quality water filtration system — an under-the-sink system can cost as little as $150
  • Choose small, wild fish (salmon, sardines) and low-mercury canned tuna brands like Safe Catch and Wild Planet
  • Support your liver and glutathione (see Lesson 9)
  • Remineralize: adequate zinc, selenium, magnesium and calcium help the body push metals out
What the research shows
  • Mercury and other heavy metals are well documented to induce autoimmunity in genetically susceptible animals and are associated with autoimmune markers in people (Pollard et al., Biochim Biophys Acta 2019).

Seed Oils

Industrial seed oils — canola, soybean, corn, cottonseed, sunflower, safflower, grapeseed — were barely part of the human diet 100 years ago. Today they’re in nearly every processed and restaurant food.

How they’re made

  • Extracted with chemical solvents such as hexane
  • Exposed to high heat during refining, which alters their chemical structure and makes them oxidized and rancid
  • Bleached and deodorized to hide the rancid smell
  • Then often reheated again and again in restaurant fryers

Why they drive inflammation

  • Toxic aldehydes. Heating these oils creates aldehydes such as 4-HNE that damage cells, including brain cells. 4-HNE is found at elevated levels in brains affected by Alzheimer’s disease.
  • Too much omega-6. Seed oils are very high in linoleic acid. In large amounts, omega-6s promote oxidative stress and chronic inflammation and crowd out anti-inflammatory omega-3s.
  • They build up in your body. The linoleic acid in Americans’ body fat has more than doubled over the past 50 years.
  • They stay a long time. Fats stored in body fat turn over slowly — it can take anywhere from months to several years to replace them.

Seed oils and the brain

  • Mice prone to Alzheimer’s fed a canola-oil-rich diet had worse memory and more amyloid plaque than mice on a regular diet.
  • When researchers recovered data from a large 1960s trial that replaced saturated fat with corn oil, cholesterol went down — but risk of death went up, especially in people over 65.

What you can do

  • Cook with avocado oil, coconut oil, extra virgin olive oil, lard, beef tallow or ghee
  • Check labels on chips, dressings, mayo, roasted nuts and “healthy” snacks
  • Eat out less, or ask restaurants to cook in olive oil or butter
  • Raise omega-3s with wild fish and fish oil
What the research shows
  • A canola-enriched diet worsened memory and synaptic integrity and increased amyloid plaque in Alzheimer’s-model mice (Lauretti & Praticò, Sci Rep 2017).
  • In recovered Minnesota Coronary Experiment data, replacing saturated fat with linoleic-acid-rich corn oil lowered cholesterol but did not lower deaths; each drop in cholesterol was associated with higher death risk (Ramsden et al., BMJ 2016).
  • Linoleic acid in US adipose tissue rose 136% over half a century (Guyenet & Carlson, Adv Nutr 2015).
Honest noteSome large observational studies have found the opposite — higher polyunsaturated fat intake with lower Alzheimer’s risk. Those studies didn’t separate fresh oils from heated, oxidized ones, which is where most of the harm appears. The direct link in humans hasn’t been proven either way, but the mechanisms and animal data are strong enough to make avoiding them a sensible choice.

Sugar

Sugar is one of the fastest ways to turn up inflammation. The average American eats around 17 teaspoons of added sugar a day — far more than the body was designed to handle.

Why it drives inflammation

  • Inflammatory messengers. Sugar increases production of pro-inflammatory molecules such as cytokines. In young healthy men, even moderate amounts of sugary drinks raised the inflammatory marker CRP within weeks.
  • Blood sugar spikes and insulin resistance. High intake, especially of refined sugar, elevates blood sugar, which leads to insulin resistance and activates inflammatory pathways.
  • AGEs. Excess sugar sticks to proteins and fats, forming advanced glycation end products (AGEs) that promote inflammation and damage tissues over time.
  • Fatty liver. Fructose is processed almost entirely by the liver, where excess is turned into fat.
  • Feeds the wrong microbes. Sugar fuels yeast and harmful bacteria, contributing to dysbiosis.

Linked conditions

  • Heart disease
  • Type 2 diabetes
  • Arthritis
  • Fatty liver disease
  • Gout (fructose raises uric acid)

What you can do

  • Cut refined sugar completely during this protocol
  • Keep even natural sugars to a minimum — small portions of high-sugar fruit
  • Eat protein and fat with fruit to slow the blood sugar rise
  • Read labels: sugar hides under dozens of names, like cane juice, dextrose and syrup
What the research shows
  • Consuming 40–80 g/day of fructose or sucrose from sweetened drinks for 3 weeks raised hs-CRP in healthy young men (Aeberli et al., Am J Clin Nutr 2011).

Dairy

Dairy can cause inflammation for many different reasons, and most of them come down to how modern milk is produced. Today’s grocery-store milk is very different from milk our ancestors drank.

The proteins

  • Casein and whey are proteins many people have difficulty digesting. Milk is one of the most common food allergens.
  • Changed casein (A1). Most modern cows carry a genetic mutation that produces A1 beta-casein instead of the original A2. A1 breaks down into a peptide called BCM-7, which may trigger inflammation in the gut and elsewhere. In a controlled trial, A1 milk caused more digestive symptoms and higher inflammatory markers than A2 milk.
  • Opioid-like effects. Casein breaks down into casomorphins, which act on opioid receptors — part of why cheese can feel addictive.
  • Cross-reactivity with gluten. Casein looks similar enough to gluten that, in lab testing, antibodies against gluten also reacted to it.

Lactose

  • About two-thirds of adults worldwide lose much of their ability to digest lactose after childhood.
  • Without the enzyme lactase, lactose ferments in the gut, causing bloating, gas and inflammation.

Processing

  • Pasteurization uses heat to kill harmful bacteria, but it also destroys the natural enzymes (such as lipase and phosphatase) and beneficial bacteria in raw milk.
  • Ultra-pasteurization uses even higher heat, further changing the proteins.
  • Additives such as carrageenan, gums and powdered milk solids are common in dairy products.

How the cows are raised

  • Grain instead of grass. Most dairy cows are fed corn and soy instead of grass. This lowers anti-inflammatory omega-3s and CLA in the milk and raises omega-6. Organic, pasture-based milk has about 62% more omega-3 than conventional milk.
  • Hormones. Modern cows are milked while pregnant, so milk carries the cow’s estrogen and progesterone. In one study, men’s estrogen rose and testosterone fell within hours of drinking milk.
  • Growth hormone. Synthetic growth hormone (rBGH/rBST), banned in Canada and Europe but still allowed in the US, raises IGF-1, a growth factor in milk.
  • Antibiotics and pesticides. Residues from feed and treatment can end up in milk.

Other effects

  • Dairy spikes insulin far more than its sugar content would suggest
  • Linked to acne and congestion in many people

What you can do

  • Remove all dairy during this protocol: milk, cheese, yogurt, kefir, ice cream, sour cream, whey and casein
  • Use coconut milk, coconut cream and coconut yogurt instead
  • Ghee (clarified butter) has most of the milk proteins removed and is on the Yes list
What the research shows
  • A1 beta-casein milk caused more digestive discomfort, higher inflammatory markers and worse scores on a thinking-speed test than A2-only milk in a double-blind crossover trial (Jianqin et al., Nutr J 2016).
  • Organic milk contained 62% more omega-3 and a healthier omega-6:omega-3 ratio than conventional milk (Benbrook et al., PLoS One 2013).
  • Drinking commercial milk from pregnant cows raised serum estrogens and lowered testosterone in men (Maruyama et al., Pediatr Int 2010).
  • Lactose malabsorption affects about 68% of the world’s population (Storhaug et al., Lancet Gastroenterol Hepatol 2017).
Honest noteRaw milk keeps its enzymes, but in a small trial it did not reduce symptoms for people with lactose intolerance compared with pasteurized milk (Mummah et al., Ann Fam Med 2014). And in people without a dairy sensitivity, studies generally don’t show dairy raising inflammation. That’s exactly why we remove it and test.

Gluten

Gluten is a group of proteins found in wheat, barley and rye. It can cause inflammation in anyone, not only people with celiac disease or gluten sensitivity.

Why it drives inflammation

  • It opens the gut lining. Gluten triggers the release of zonulin, a protein that loosens the tight junctions between gut cells — “leaky gut.” This lets undigested food and toxins into the bloodstream, provoking an immune response. Lab studies show this happens in gut tissue from people without celiac disease too.
  • It activates the immune system directly. Wheat contains proteins called amylase-trypsin inhibitors (ATIs) that switch on the innate immune system, separate from gluten itself.
  • It’s hard to digest. Gluten resists our digestive enzymes, leaving large protein fragments in the gut.
  • It can be addictive. Partly digested gluten forms opioid-like peptides (gluten exorphins) that act on the same receptors as morphine. This may help explain why bread and pasta cravings are so strong.
  • Wheat lectin. Wheat germ agglutinin binds to the gut lining and can affect its function.
  • Glyphosate residue. Non-organic wheat is often sprayed right before harvest.
  • Blood sugar. Wheat starch raises blood sugar quickly.

Gluten and autoimmunity

  • Celiac disease is an autoimmune disease triggered by gluten.
  • People with one autoimmune disease are more likely to have celiac disease or gluten sensitivity.
  • Non-celiac gluten sensitivity can cause gut symptoms, joint pain, fatigue, brain fog and headaches.

What you can do

  • Remove all wheat, barley, rye and spelt
  • Watch for hidden gluten in sauces, soy sauce, soups, seasonings, beer and supplements
  • Use cassava, coconut, tigernut and arrowroot flours instead
What the research shows
  • Gliadin increased zonulin release and intestinal permeability in tissue from both celiac and non-celiac people (Drago et al., Scand J Gastroenterol 2006; Hollon et al., Nutrients 2015).
  • Wheat amylase-trypsin inhibitors activated innate immunity through the TLR4 receptor (Junker et al., J Exp Med 2012).
  • Gluten digestion produces opioid peptides (gluten exorphins) with morphine-like activity (Zioudrou et al., J Biol Chem 1979).

Also: Fasano, Physiol Rev 2011

Grains

Even gluten-free grains — corn, rice, oats, millet, quinoa — can be a problem while the gut is healing.

Why they drive inflammation

  • Lectins and phytic acid can irritate the gut lining, increase intestinal permeability and bind minerals like zinc, iron and magnesium.
  • Cross-reactivity. The immune system may mistake proteins in non-gluten grains for gluten because their structure is similar. In lab testing, antibodies against gluten reacted with proteins from corn, rice and oats. For someone with gluten sensitivity or celiac disease, this can mean digestive discomfort, inflammation or joint pain even without eating gluten.
  • Blood sugar. Most grains are high in carbohydrates and raise blood sugar quickly, especially refined grains. Over time this drives insulin resistance, which triggers inflammatory pathways.
  • Contamination. Oats are often contaminated with wheat. Corn and grains can carry mold toxins (mycotoxins).
  • Heavy metals and spray. Rice absorbs arsenic. Most US corn is genetically modified to tolerate glyphosate.

What you can do

  • Remove all grains during this protocol
  • Replace them with sweet potato, squash, cassava, plantain, cauliflower rice and root vegetables
What the research shows
  • Antibodies against gliadin cross-reacted with several non-gluten foods, including corn, rice and oats, in lab testing (Vojdani & Tarash, Food Nutr Sci 2013). This has not been confirmed in clinical studies.

🥲

Artificial Sweeteners

Artificial sweeteners were sold as a healthy swap for sugar. The research tells a different story — especially for the gut.

Where they came from

  • Sucralose (Splenda) was discovered during research into chlorinated sugars as possible insecticide ingredients.
  • Saccharin came out of coal-tar chemistry.
  • Aspartame was discovered while developing an ulcer drug.

Why they drive inflammation

  • They damage gut bacteria. In rats given Splenda for 12 weeks, beneficial gut bacteria fell by up to half. Killing the good microbes lets harmful bacteria grow, leading to dysbiosis.
  • They change blood sugar through the gut. In a human trial, sucralose and saccharin altered the gut microbiome and worsened blood sugar responses. Moving those people’s gut bacteria into mice moved the effect too.
  • They can raise insulin. They don’t spike blood sugar directly, but some research shows they raise insulin, our fat-storage hormone. Chronic use may push the body toward insulin resistance.
  • Inflammatory signals. Some studies show increased pro-inflammatory cytokines.
  • Breakdown products. A compound in and made from sucralose, sucralose-6-acetate, damaged DNA and made human gut tissue leakier in lab testing.
  • Cancer questions. The World Health Organization’s cancer agency classified aspartame as “possibly carcinogenic” in 2023.

What you can do

  • Avoid sucralose, aspartame, saccharin and acesulfame-K
  • Check “sugar-free” and “diet” products, gum, protein powders, electrolyte mixes and medications
  • During this protocol, erythritol is out too. Pure stevia, allulose and monk fruit are fine, and raw honey and pure maple syrup are fine in moderation (see Lesson 4)
What the research shows
  • Splenda reduced beneficial gut bacteria by up to about 50% in rats over 12 weeks (Abou-Donia et al., J Toxicol Environ Health A 2008).
  • Non-nutritive sweeteners altered the human microbiome and glucose tolerance in a randomized trial (Suez et al., Cell 2022; Suez et al., Nature 2014).
  • Sucralose-6-acetate was genotoxic and impaired gut barrier integrity in human intestinal tissue (Schiffman et al., J Toxicol Environ Health B 2023).
  • Sucralose raised insulin levels after a glucose load in people with obesity (Pepino et al., Diabetes Care 2013).
Inside the gut
🦠

Gut Dysbiosis

Your gut is home to around 1,000 to 1,500 species of bacteria — trillions of cells that do work your body can’t do on its own. When this community gets out of balance, it’s called dysbiosis.

What healthy gut bacteria do

  • Break down food
  • Make vitamins such as B vitamins and vitamin K
  • Help produce signaling molecules such as serotonin and dopamine, plus other enzymes and gases important to well-being
  • Train the immune system: they stimulate T-cells and regulatory T-cells, which control inflammation and prevent autoimmune responses
  • Influence antibodies such as IgA, which protects the gut’s surfaces

How dysbiosis drives inflammation

  • Toxins. When harmful bacteria overgrow, they produce toxins that damage the gut lining and increase intestinal permeability (“leaky gut”).
  • Leaks into the blood. Undigested food particles and bacteria slip into the bloodstream, triggering an immune response and systemic inflammation.
  • An overactive immune system. Dysbiosis promotes the release of inflammatory cytokines throughout the body.
  • Linked to autoimmune diseases, allergies and metabolic disorders.

What causes dysbiosis

  • Medications — even simple ones like ibuprofen and Tylenol. Most regular NSAID users show damage to the small intestine.
  • Antibiotics — some species are still missing months later
  • Acid blockers (PPIs)
  • Steroids such as prednisone
  • Stress, gluten, heavy metals, pesticides, herbicides, food additives, GMOs, seed oils and artificial sweeteners
  • Low-fiber, high-sugar diets and alcohol

What you can do

  • Remove the irritants — that’s exactly what this protocol does
  • Eat a wide variety of colorful vegetables for fiber and polyphenols
  • Add fermented foods and a probiotic (unless you have upper-gut symptoms like SIBO)
  • Use bone broth, collagen and gut-healing nutrients (see Lesson 9)
  • Talk to your doctor about medications you take regularly
What the research shows
  • 71% of long-term NSAID users had small-bowel injury on capsule endoscopy, compared with 10% of non-users (Graham et al., Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol 2005).
  • After a short course of antibiotics, most gut species recovered within 6 months, but several common species were still missing (Palleja et al., Nat Microbiol 2018).
  • Proton pump inhibitor use was associated with lower gut microbial diversity (Imhann et al., Gut 2016).
Key takeawayIf your nervous and immune systems are dysregulated, it is unlikely you will get well with any other efforts. Food, environment and stress all count toward the same load.

Terms you’ll see

Click a term to expand it.

Intestinal permeability (“leaky gut”)
The gut lining is a single layer of cells held together by “tight junctions.” When those loosen, partially digested food, bacteria and their toxins slip into the bloodstream, and the immune system responds.
Lectins
Carbohydrate-binding proteins plants use as a defense. Some resist digestion and can bind to the gut lining.
Saponins & glycoalkaloids
Soap-like plant defense compounds that can disrupt cell membranes, including the membranes of gut cells.
Phytates (phytic acid)
Bind minerals such as zinc, iron and magnesium, reducing how much you absorb.
Enzyme (protease) inhibitors
Block the enzymes that digest protein, leaving larger fragments for the immune system to see.
Cross-reactivity
When the immune system mistakes one protein for another that looks similar.

Nightshades are plants in the Solanaceae family. They are delicious and nutritious for many people, but they are one of the most common triggers for people with autoimmune disease, especially joint pain and gut symptoms.

Why they are sometimes a problem

  • Glycoalkaloids. Nightshades make natural pesticides — solanine and chaconine in potatoes, tomatine in tomatoes. In laboratory and animal studies, the potato glycoalkaloids disrupted the gut barrier and worsened intestinal inflammation in mice prone to inflammatory bowel disease, at levels comparable to what people eat. Concentrations are highest in potato skins, green or sprouting potatoes and unripe tomatoes.
  • Capsaicin and related compounds. The “heat” in peppers comes from capsaicin. In human intestinal cell studies, paprika and cayenne extracts increased the permeability of the gut lining.
  • Lectins and saponins. Tomato lectin resists digestion and binds to the intestinal lining. On its own this hasn’t been shown to cause damage in healthy animals, but in a gut that is already inflamed or leaky, anything that sticks to the lining is a candidate irritant.
  • Joint pain. Many people with arthritis report that nightshades worsen their joint pain. Controlled studies are lacking, so this is one to test for yourself — and it’s one of the most common “aha” moments during reintroduction.
What the research shows Potato glycoalkaloids increased gut permeability and aggravated inflammatory bowel disease in two different mouse models. Paprika and cayenne increased permeability of human intestinal cell layers in the lab.
Patel et al., Inflamm Bowel Dis 2002; Iablokov et al., Dig Dis Sci 2010; Jensen-Jarolim et al., J Nutr 1998; Kilpatrick et al., FEBS Lett 1985

Avoid

  • Tomatoes & tomatillos
  • White potatoes (all non-sweet potatoes)
  • Peppers of any kind — bell, jalapeño, chili, banana, pimento
  • Capsicums
  • Eggplant
  • Goji berries
  • Ground cherries / cape gooseberries
  • Garden huckleberries
  • Pepinos & tamarillos
  • Ashwagandha
  • Nightshade spices: cayenne, chili powder, chili flakes, paprika, red pepper, red pepper flakes, curry powder, garam masala, Chinese five-spice

Good to know

  • Sweet potatoes and yams are NOT nightshades — enjoy them.
  • Black pepper is not a nightshade — it’s allowed.
  • Common blueberries and wild huckleberries are not nightshades. The berries to watch for are goji, cape gooseberry / ground cherry and garden huckleberry.
  • Ashwagandha is a nightshade — and it shows up in many adrenal, sleep and stress supplements. Check your labels.

Eggs are packed with nutrition, so it can be surprising to see them on the elimination list. The issue is almost entirely in the white, which is why yolks are usually the first thing reintroduced.

Why they are sometimes a problem

  • Egg white proteins are among the most common food allergens. Ovomucoid, ovalbumin, ovotransferrin and lysozyme are the main culprits. Ovomucoid is especially stubborn — it survives cooking and digestion largely intact.
  • Built-in enzyme inhibitors. Ovomucoid and ovoinhibitor block trypsin, one of the enzymes you use to break down protein. Less complete digestion means larger protein fragments reaching the gut lining — exactly what an overactive immune system reacts to.
  • Lysozyme. This egg-white enzyme resists digestion, can be absorbed intact, and binds bacterial cell-wall material. One proposed (not yet proven) concern is that it can carry bacterial fragments across a leaky gut lining.
  • Frequency. Many people eat eggs nearly every day, sometimes several times a day. Foods eaten that often are more likely to become sensitivities when the gut barrier is compromised.
What the research shows The major egg allergens are concentrated in the white; ovomucoid is heat-stable and a trypsin inhibitor. Many people sensitive to whole eggs tolerate yolks.
Mine & Yang, J Agric Food Chem 2008
Hidden sources Mayonnaise and aioli, baked goods, meatballs and meatloaf binders, breaded or battered foods, some “paleo” snacks and bars, Caesar dressing, some pasta, and many supplements made with egg-derived lysozyme. Use gelatin or cassava/coconut flour as a binder instead.

Nuts, Seeds, Spices, Legumes, Sweeteners & More

WhenPrep Day 4

Step 1 of 18

Why they are sometimes a problem

  • They are a plant’s babies, and they are well defended. Nuts and seeds are designed to pass through an animal intact and still sprout. To do that they carry lectins, phytic acid and enzyme inhibitors.
  • Phytic acid binds minerals. It reduces absorption of zinc, iron, magnesium and calcium — the very minerals your gut lining and immune system need to heal.
  • High in omega-6. Sunflower seeds, pumpkin seeds, walnuts, pecans, pine nuts and Brazil nuts are rich in linoleic acid. In moderation that’s fine, but large amounts push the omega-6 to omega-3 balance toward inflammation.
  • Common allergens. Tree nuts and sesame are among the most common food allergens, and there is considerable cross-reactivity between different tree nuts.
  • Easy to overeat. A handful becomes three. Nut flours and butters concentrate all of the above, and almond flour baking can quickly add up to several cups of almonds.
What the research shows Phytic acid is a well-documented inhibitor of mineral absorption. Soaking, sprouting and roasting reduce it, which is a useful tool when you reintroduce.
Gupta et al., J Food Sci Technol 2015

Avoid

  • Almonds, Brazil nuts, chestnuts, hazelnuts, macadamias, pecans, pine nuts, pistachios, walnuts
  • Pumpkin, sesame, sunflower seeds
  • Nut & seed flours, butters, milks
  • Tahini, sesame oil, nut oils

Good to know

  • Coconut is a Yes — it is a drupe, not a tree nut.
  • Tigernuts are a Yes — they are a small root vegetable, not a nut.
  • Cassava, tigernut, coconut and arrowroot replace nut flours.

Spices that come from a seed carry the same defensive compounds as other seeds, just in smaller amounts. Because they’re used in so many dishes, they add up and they make it hard to tell what you reacted to.

Why they are sometimes a problem

  • Mustard, sesame and celery seed are recognized food allergens in many countries.
  • Blends hide them. Curry powder, garam masala, five-spice, taco and steak seasonings usually combine seed spices with nightshades.

Why they are sometimes a problem

  • The highest-lectin food group. Kidney beans contain phytohaemagglutinin, a lectin potent enough that a handful of undercooked beans can cause vomiting and diarrhea. Proper soaking and cooking destroys most of it — but not always all.
  • Peanut lectin gets into the blood. In a study published in The Lancet, intact peanut lectin was detected in people’s bloodstream within an hour of eating peanuts. That is direct evidence that some dietary lectins cross the gut barrier.
  • Saponins. Legumes, especially soy and chickpeas, are rich in saponins, which increased the permeability of intestinal tissue in laboratory studies.
  • Gas-producing fibers. Legumes are high in fermentable sugars (galacto-oligosaccharides) that feed gut bacteria. In a healthy gut that’s fine; with dysbiosis or SIBO it often means bloating, gas and pain.
  • Soy is a top allergen, is usually genetically modified and heavily sprayed in the US, and is processed into dozens of hidden ingredients. Even naturally fermented soy (gluten-free soy sauce, natto, miso) is out during elimination.

Wang et al., Lancet 1998; Johnson et al., J Nutr 1986; Vasconcelos & Oliveira, Toxicon 2004

Black beans, black-eyed peas, chickpeas / garbanzo beans, fava beans, kidney beans, lima beans, mung beans, navy beans, pinto beans, white beans, lentils, peanuts & peanut butter, snow peas, sugar snap peas, soybeans, edamame, tofu, soymilk, fermented soy (soy sauce, miso, natto). Refined sugar and artificial sweeteners are already off the table. On this protocol, erythritol goes too. Raw honey and pure maple syrup are allowed in moderation, and stevia, allulose and monk fruit are allowed.

Honey and maple syrup: in moderation

  • They’re whole-food sweeteners, but they’re still sugar. Blood sugar stability is key for healing, and every spike raises insulin and inflammatory signaling.
  • Use small amounts — a teaspoon in a dressing or a drizzle on pancakes, not every day and not by the spoonful.
  • Choose raw, local honey and 100% pure maple syrup (not “pancake syrup,” which is corn syrup).

Why erythritol is out

  • Erythritol upsets the gut. As a sugar alcohol it commonly causes gas, bloating and loose stools in sensitive people.
  • Erythritol and clotting. A 2023 study linked higher blood erythritol to greater risk of heart attack and stroke and showed it made platelets more reactive.

Why stevia, allulose and monk fruit are okay

  • They add sweetness with little to no effect on blood sugar or insulin.
  • Stevia and monk fruit come from plants and are used in tiny amounts.
  • Allulose is a rare sugar the body barely uses for energy, so it doesn’t spike blood sugar.

Use them wisely

  • Read the label. Many “stevia” and “monk fruit” products are mostly erythritol by weight. Choose pure stevia, pure monk fruit, or blends without erythritol or added sugar.
  • Start small with allulose. In larger amounts it can cause gas or bloating.
  • Keep sweet foods occasional. Sweet taste keeps cravings alive, even without sugar.
  • Count honey and maple syrup as sugar. Keep them to small amounts, like your fruit.
What the research shows Higher circulating erythritol was associated with major cardiovascular events, and erythritol enhanced platelet reactivity in lab and human feeding studies.
Witkowski et al., Nat Med 2023

Dark chocolate

Cocoa is a seed, so it carries seed compounds. It is also a stimulant (caffeine and theobromine), is high in oxalates and is one of the richest dietary sources of nickel, a common sensitivity. Most chocolate also contains sugar and often soy lecithin. Out for now; it’s a popular reintroduction later.

Coffee

Coffee is also a seed. Caffeine raises cortisol and adrenaline, which works against calming a stressed nervous and immune system, and it can disrupt sleep — one of your most powerful healing tools. It stimulates acid and gut motility, aggravating reflux and loose stools for some. One laboratory study found antibodies against gluten cross-reacted with proteins in instant coffee; that hasn’t been confirmed in people, but it’s another reason to test it on its own. Wean down over a week before you start to avoid withdrawal headaches. Green, black and herbal teas are a Yes.

🥩

Protein intake

Many people don’t eat enough protein, but too much can also raise blood sugar — excess protein the body can’t use is converted to glucose. A general rule is 0.5 × your ideal body weight (in pounds) in grams of protein per day, a little more if you are active or young. 6 oz of meat is about 50 grams. Electrolytes like sodium, potassium and magnesium regulate fluid balance, muscle function and nerve signaling, maintain blood pressure, and let nutrients into cells and waste out. If you feel weak and tired, hydrating with salt and electrolytes is a good place to start. (Choose an electrolyte mix with no sugar or erythritol during elimination — stevia or monk fruit is fine.)

Support Your Healing: Key Nutrients

WhenElimination Days 8–14

Step 1 of 8

Food comes first, but a healing body needs some nutrients in amounts that are hard to get from food alone. These six areas support the immune system, calm inflammation and repair the gut lining.

First, an important note on choosing supplements. Then we’ll go through each nutrient.

Before you buy Check every supplement for the eliminated foods: ashwagandha, capsicum/cayenne, black pepper extract (piperine / BioPerine), soy, egg-derived lysozyme, sugar or erythritol, and potato or corn starch fillers.
☀

Vitamin D3 with K2

D3 and K2 work together to support bone health, immune regulation and cardiovascular function. D3 enhances calcium absorption, while K2 directs calcium to the bones and away from the arteries. D3 also modulates immune function by regulating T-cells and reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines, helping to prevent autoimmune disease. K2 reduces inflammation by activating Matrix Gla Protein (MGP), which prevents calcification-related inflammation. Together they support heart health, brain health, metabolic balance and insulin sensitivity.

🐟

Fish Oil / Omega-3

Omega-3s from fish oil, krill oil and cod liver oil are rich in EPA and DHA, which compete with pro-inflammatory omega-6 fats in cell membranes. EPA and DHA are precursors to specialized pro-resolving mediators (SPMs) like resolvins and protectins, which actively resolve inflammation and promote tissue repair. Krill oil adds phospholipid-bound omega-3s for better absorption, plus astaxanthin, a potent antioxidant. Brand matters: poor processing can make the oil rancid, and the fish source matters for mercury.

💊

B Vitamins

B vitamins are essential for immune regulation and inflammation control. Choose methylated forms such as methylfolate and methylcobalamin — the active forms — since many people have trouble converting inactive forms due to genetic variants such as MTHFR. Methylfolate drives the methylation cycle, essential for DNA synthesis, immune cell growth and regulating inflammatory cytokines. Methylcobalamin lowers homocysteine, a pro-inflammatory marker, and supports red blood cells and the nervous system. B6 supports lymphocyte growth and cytokine production, and B vitamins are cofactors in ATP production, giving immune cells the energy to work properly.

🌿

Liver Support

Supporting the liver is essential for immune regulation and reducing inflammation. Milk thistle (silymarin) protects liver cells, boosts glutathione and reduces oxidative stress. Dandelion root and burdock root promote bile flow, helping eliminate toxins that can overstimulate the immune system. N-acetylcysteine (NAC) raises glutathione to neutralize free radicals. Turmeric (curcumin) and schisandra berry have strong anti-inflammatory effects, modulating cytokines and calming excessive immune responses. Zinc and selenium are cofactors for liver detox enzymes and regulate immune function. (Many turmeric supplements include black pepper extract — choose one without it during elimination.)

🪨

Magnesium

Magnesium is involved in over 300 enzymatic reactions. It lowers pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-6 and TNF-α, supports antioxidant activity and helps prevent excessive immune activation. Good forms include glycinate (highly absorbable and calming), malate (cellular energy and muscle recovery), threonate (designed to reach the brain, for neuroinflammation) and taurate (cardiovascular support — blood pressure, heart rhythm and circulation — plus calming the nervous system and improving insulin sensitivity).

🌱

Gut Healing

Key nutrients help repair the intestinal barrier, calm inflammation, support mucus production and nourish the cells lining the digestive tract. L-glutamine is a primary fuel for intestinal cells and may strengthen tight junctions. Collagen and gelatin provide glycine, proline and hydroxyproline for connective tissue repair. Zinc — especially zinc carnosine — supports tissue healing, immune regulation and the protective barrier. Vitamin A regulates mucosal immunity and supports regeneration of the gut lining.

Polyphenol-rich foods such as berries, green tea, pomegranate and colorful vegetables further protect the gut lining and feed beneficial microbes. Bone broth, well-cooked vegetables, adequate protein and hydration provide easily digestible nutrients while reducing digestive strain. Removing irritants — which is exactly what this protocol does — creates the environment where these nutrients work best.

Hidden Sources & Label Reading

Eliminated foods hide in packaged foods, restaurant meals and supplements. A single slip won’t ruin your progress, but repeated hidden exposures can keep symptoms simmering and make your reintroduction results confusing.

Where eliminated foods hide

Watch forOften hidden in
“Spices,” “seasoning,” “natural flavors”Can include paprika, chili or seed spices. Jerky, meat sticks, rotisserie chicken, sausages, deli meats, broths, dressings.
Paprika / paprika extractUsed for color in salmon, bacon, chips, sauces, “smoked” flavors.
Potato starch / modified food starchShredded cheese substitutes, gluten-free baked goods, gravies, medications and supplement capsules.
Ashwagandha, capsicum, cayenneAdrenal, stress, sleep, metabolism and “thermogenic” supplements.
Egg / albumin / lysozymeMayonnaise, baked goods, meatballs, battered foods, some supplements.
Soy lecithin, soybean oilChocolate, supplements, “avocado oil” mayo blends, restaurant cooking oil.
Sugar, cane juice, erythritol (including in “stevia” and “monk fruit” blends)Bacon, smoked salmon, sausages, deli meats, coconut yogurt, broths, protein powders, electrolyte mixes.
Sunflower / safflower oilChips, “paleo” snacks, roasted vegetables at restaurants, dried fruit.
Tomato, chiliWorcestershire, BBQ, ketchup, salsa, taco seasoning, many soups.
The simple rule If a label lists an ingredient you don’t recognize or can’t verify, leave it on the shelf for now. The fewer ingredients, the better.

Eating out

  • Order simple: grilled or roasted protein, plain steamed or roasted vegetables, a salad with olive oil and lemon.
  • Ask for no seasoning except salt, cooked in olive oil (no butter or vegetable oil).
  • Ask that no peppers, tomatoes, cheese or croutons be added.
  • Bring your own coconut aminos or dressing if you like.

Simple Meal Ideas

You don’t need fancy recipes to do this well. These are simple ideas for every meal, all made from Yes foods.

Use them on busy days, or as a starting point when you’re not sure what to make. Full recipes are in the Recipe Collection in the Toolkit.

Breakfast

  • Smoothie with berries, greens and coconut cream (add a scoop or two of electrolytes for flavor and sweetness)
  • Coconut yogurt (no added sugar or guar gum) with berries, coconut flakes and a sprinkle of tigernuts
  • Collagen protein drink (e.g., Vital Proteins collagen @ Costco)
  • Nitrate-free ham or bacon with avocado and fruit
  • Leftovers! Last night’s dinner makes a great breakfast
  • See also: breakfast skillets and hash in the recipe section

Lunch

  • “Sandwiches” wrapped in a large lettuce leaf or a coconut wrap
  • Chicken salad made with coconut yogurt or mashed avocado, celery and apple
  • Thai lettuce wraps with coconut aminos, lime, ginger, garlic and cilantro
  • Tuna or salmon (low-mercury, wild caught)
  • Deli sandwich with high-quality deli meat (Kirkland ham, roast beef or turkey @ Costco — check for sugar and chili) on lettuce
  • Roasted vegetables: Brussels sprouts, asparagus, zucchini, cauliflower, carrots, onion, parsnips, turnips, mushrooms, yams
  • Steamed broccoli or cauliflower with ghee and salt
  • Suja green drink @ Costco and Walmart (check that the flavor has no added sweetener)
  • Easy green salad meal: leftover rotisserie chicken or chopped deli meat on mixed organic greens with kalamata olives, artichoke, cucumber, carrots, cauliflower, mushrooms, sprouts and avocado. Dress with half a lemon, olive oil and salt.
  • Cobb salad without cheese or eggs
  • Coleslaw — use stevia, allulose or monk fruit for sweetness
  • Guacamole (no tomato or jalapeño) with cucumber slices as chips
  • Raw vegetables with ranch made from coconut yogurt, dill, garlic, onion powder and lemon

Dinner

  • Taco salad without cheese or chips — season with garlic, onion powder, oregano, turmeric and salt
  • Tacos on lettuce or cassava tortillas
  • Hamburger or salmon patty on lettuce
  • “Nomato” sauce (carrot, beet and pumpkin simmered with garlic, onion and basil, then blended) on zucchini noodles or spaghetti squash
  • Rotisserie chicken (check the seasoning, or roast your own)
  • Grilled chicken: lemon, garlic, herb, or teriyaki-style with coconut aminos, ginger and garlic, sweetened with stevia or allulose
  • Steak
  • Roast (smoked roasts and briskets are amazing — time to get a smoker! Use a salt, garlic and herb rub)
  • Stir-fry with coconut aminos on cauliflower rice
  • Roasted salmon with lemon and dill
  • Chicken noodle soup with zoodles (zucchini noodles)
  • Vegetable soup with hamburger (no tomato)
  • Stew with carrots, parsnips, turnips and sweet potato (no white potato or tomato)
  • Stuffed zucchini boats or acorn squash with ground meat and cauliflower rice
  • Coconut curry with turmeric, ginger, garlic and lemongrass (no curry powder or paste) on cauliflower rice
  • Meatloaf made with coconut or cassava flour and gelatin as the binder
  • Tom kha gai — coconut, lemongrass, lime leaf, ginger and mushroom soup (skip the chili)
Adapted for elimination These ideas come from the Anti-Inflammatory meal list with eggs, peppers, tomatoes, chili, cheese, nuts, sweeteners and soy sauce removed or swapped. Egg salad, stuffed bell peppers, Penang curry, tortilla soup, BBQ sauce and pea pods are back on the menu after you’ve tested those foods.
