Breast Cancer Beyond Genetics · Part Ten

Screening, Honestly

You have been told mammograms save lives. You have probably not been told what else they do.

Both halves belong to you.

Mammography Uses Ionizing Radiation

Modeling study

X-rays damage DNA. The dose from a single mammogram is small, but it repeats — and it accumulates in the tissue you are trying to protect.

A modeling study estimated that among 100,000 women screened annually from ages 40 to 74, the radiation itself would cause roughly 125 additional breast cancers and 16 deaths. The estimate ran higher for women receiving above-average doses or additional diagnostic views.

It is a model rather than a count, because a radiation-caused cancer looks identical to any other. That is precisely why the number rarely comes up.

Risk rises with younger age at first exposure, annual rather than every-other-year screening, larger breasts requiring extra views, repeated diagnostic workups, and individual sensitivity to radiation.

On The Flying Comparison

You may hear a mammogram compared to a plane ride. The doses are not equivalent.

They are also distributed differently. Cosmic radiation is diffuse across your whole body. Mammography aims X-rays directly through breast tissue.

Screening Can Find Cancer That Would Never Have Hurt You

Systematic review · 2023

This is called overdiagnosis, and it is not the same as a false positive.

A false positive means the scan looked suspicious and nothing was there. Overdiagnosis means something was there — a real abnormality meeting the diagnostic criteria for cancer — that would never have caused symptoms or shortened your life.

A 2023 systematic review estimated that about 12.6% of breast cancers found through screening were overdiagnosed. Other analyses have ranged from under 5% to around 30%, depending on definitions and statistical adjustments.

The problem is that an overdiagnosed cancer is indistinguishable from a dangerous one at the moment of diagnosis. So it gets treated. Surgery, radiation, sometimes years of endocrine therapy — for something that was never going to hurt her.

Roughly one in eight women diagnosed through screening is treated for a cancer that would never have harmed her. Medicine cannot yet tell which one she is.

DCIS

Ductal carcinoma in situ is abnormal cells contained inside a milk duct. It has not invaded surrounding tissue. Mammography finds it through microcalcifications — tiny flecks of calcium that show up white on the film — and detection has risen enormously since screening became routine.

Some DCIS becomes invasive cancer. Some sits unchanged for decades. Some may never matter at all. Medicine cannot currently predict which is which for any individual woman.

Ask your team

If you are diagnosed with DCIS, ask for the grade of the lesion, its size and hormone-receptor status, whether active surveillance is an option for you, the absolute benefit of each proposed treatment rather than the relative benefit, and the risks of surgery, radiation, and endocrine therapy in your specific case.

The difference between relative and absolute benefit matters enormously here. “Reduces recurrence by 50%” sounds very different from “reduces your risk from 4% to 2%” — and they can be the same number.

Dense Breast Tissue

Dense tissue does two things at once, and most women only hear about the first.

It makes mammograms harder to read — dense tissue and tumors both appear white on the film, so a tumor can hide the way a snowball hides in a snowbank. And it independently raises breast cancer risk.

Many states now require that you be told your density after a mammogram. If you have dense breasts, that changes what a normal mammogram means and may change what imaging makes sense for you.

Ask your team

Ask directly. What is my breast density category, and does it change what you would recommend?

Ultrasound Finds What Mammography Misses

Randomized trial · 72,998 women

This is the part of the screening conversation most women never get, and it comes from a large randomized trial.

J-START enrolled 72,998 women aged 40 to 49 in Japan and randomly assigned them either mammography alone or mammography plus ultrasound.

77%
sensitivity with mammography alone
91%
sensitivity with ultrasound added
—
significantly more stage 0 and stage I cancers found

More early-stage cancers, found earlier, with no additional radiation. In a population where mammography performs worst — younger women, who tend to have denser tissue.

What catches cancer early is regular surveillance. Which tool you use for it is a separate question, and it is yours.

Ultrasound: The Full Picture

What it does well
  • No ionizing radiation, so no accumulating dose
  • Works well in dense tissue
  • Tells a fluid-filled cyst from a solid mass
  • Evaluates a specific lump
  • Finds cancers mammography misses
  • Guides needle biopsies
Where it falls short
  • Does not reliably detect microcalcifications
  • More dependent on the skill of the person performing it
  • Produces its own false positives
  • Not validated as a stand-alone screening strategy with mortality data
Go deeper: the microcalcification question is more interesting than it looks

Ultrasound’s main weakness is that it misses microcalcifications — which is how much DCIS first appears.

Now hold that next to the overdiagnosis number from two pages ago. If somewhere between 5% and 30% of screen-detected cancers were never going to cause harm, and a large share of those are DCIS found through microcalcifications, then a tool that misses pure-microcalcification DCIS may be filtering out some of the cancers women are treated for unnecessarily.

That is not a settled argument, and it cuts both ways — some DCIS does progress. But it means the standard framing of this limitation as a straightforward deficiency is incomplete.

What has not been tested

No trial has evaluated ultrasound alone as a primary screening strategy with mortality outcomes. That study has not been run.

Consider what a trial like that would cost, and who would pay for it. There is no product at the end of an ultrasound trial.

MRI

What it does well
  • No ionizing radiation
  • Very high sensitivity
  • Works well in dense tissue
  • Finds cancers other imaging misses
  • Standard for high genetic or family risk
Where it falls short
  • Usually requires contrast dye
  • Expensive, often not covered without documented high risk
  • More false positives, meaning more biopsies
  • Contributes to overdiagnosis as well

Thermography

Thermography reads heat patterns rather than anatomy. It is marketed as a radiation-free alternative, and it does not reliably detect early cancers. It is not an equivalent substitute for anatomical imaging.

I include it because you will encounter it, and because a book that questions mainstream screening owes you the same skepticism toward the alternatives.

Know Your Own Risk First

Almost no woman is told this exists: validated tools — the Gail model and the Tyrer-Cuzick model — estimate your individual risk based on family history, reproductive history, breast density, and prior biopsies.

That number should come before any screening decision. The right plan for a woman at 8% lifetime risk is not the right plan for one at 30%.

What to do

Ask your provider to calculate it, or use the Tyrer-Cuzick tool yourself and bring the result to your appointment.

Your Body Is Still The First Line

Imaging is not the only thing that finds breast cancer. Know how your breasts normally look and feel, and report a new lump, nipple inversion, bloody discharge, skin dimpling, localized thickening, persistent one-sided pain, or any new change in shape.

A normal scan does not close the question on a persistent physical change. If something feels wrong and imaging says nothing is there, keep pushing.

The questions to bring
  • What is my personal baseline risk?
  • Do I have dense breasts, and does that change what you recommend?
  • What is the radiation dose from this test?
  • Would adding ultrasound find things mammography would miss for me?
  • Am I a candidate for MRI?
  • If something is found, what is the chance it is DCIS?
  • What is the risk of overdiagnosis at my age?
  • What is the absolute benefit of what you are recommending?
  • Would a second radiology opinion change the plan?

What I Am Actually Saying

Not that you should skip screening.

Regular surveillance is what catches disease early. That is the part that matters, and it is worth doing.

What is not settled — and what you are rarely invited to weigh in on — is which tool, how often, and starting when. Those answers depend on your risk level, your breast density, your age, and your tolerance for both radiation and overdiagnosis.

You cannot make an informed choice with half the information. Now you have the other half.
