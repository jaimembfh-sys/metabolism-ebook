Breast Cancer Beyond Genetics · Part One

Why You Haven’t Heard This

You’ve been told to get screened. To know your family history. To report a lump.

You have probably never been told that, by most estimates, roughly one in three breast cancers is linked to things you can change.

Both are true. Only one gets repeated.

Most Breast Cancer Is Not Inherited

Nordic twin study · 50,000+ pairs

Inherited mutations like BRCA1 and BRCA2 account for about 5 to 10% of breast cancers.

The largest study of its kind followed more than 50,000 pairs of female twins across the Nordic countries from 1943 to 2010. Twins are useful here because identical twins share all their genes while fraternal twins share about half — comparing how often each type shares a diagnosis separates inherited risk from everything else. Heritability came out around 31%, though the range in that study was wide, with another 16% attributable to shared childhood environment.

Roughly two-thirds of what determines who develops breast cancer is not inherited.

The story you have been handed — that breast cancer is bad genes or bad luck, and your only power is catching it early — is not what the research says.

And none of this is your fault. You were born into an environment engineered without your consent, filled with compounds that did not exist a century ago, and nobody handed you this information while it would have mattered most. That is not a personal failure. It is something that was done to you, and to every woman reading this.

What follows is how to take some of it back.

Why You Haven’t Heard It

Research follows funding, and funding follows what can be owned.

A new drug can be patented and sold exclusively for years. That justifies spending hundreds of millions on trials. Sleep, sunlight, fasting, exercise, fiber, muscle, and removing a chemical from your kitchen cannot be patented by anyone. So they do not get studied at the same scale — not because they do not work, but because no one profits when they do.

Absence of large trials is often absence of a sponsor, not absence of an effect.

You do not have to take my word for how openly this is discussed. In 2018, Goldman Sachs analysts published a report for biotech clients titled The Genome Revolution. It asked a question directly, in writing:

“Is curing patients a sustainable business model?”

The report used a hepatitis C cure as its cautionary example: the treatment worked so well it shrank its own market, and revenue fell from $12.5 billion in 2015 toward under $4 billion by 2018.

That is not a conspiracy theory. It is an investment bank stating the obvious financial tension in a document meant for professionals rather than patients. Cures are worth less than treatments to the people selling them. Everyone in the industry knows it. Almost no one says it to you.

What Breast Cancer Is Worth

Market analysts put the global breast cancer drug market at roughly $33 billion a year, with projections between $62 and $80 billion by the mid-2030s. Targeted therapies alone generated $21.4 billion in 2024. The United States accounts for about $8.4 billion of it.

That figure is drugs only. It does not include surgery, radiation, imaging, or hospital care.

Read the industry’s own forecasts and you find something else worth noticing. Alongside rising incidence, they list growing awareness and early detection programs as a factor driving demand for therapeutics. Screening expansion appears in market analysis as a revenue driver.

None of this requires anyone to be hiding a cure. It only requires that money flows toward what can be sold.

The Uncomfortable Part About Donations

Donations do not go to drug companies. They go to universities, hospitals, and academic labs. That sounds reassuring until you understand how those institutions now make money.

Go deeper: how universities came to sell their research

In 1980, Congress passed a law letting universities patent discoveries made with public research funding and sell the licensing rights to private companies. Before that, almost none of it was ever commercialized. After it, universities built entire departments to do exactly this — technology transfer offices, staffed with lawyers and business teams, whose job is turning research into licensable property.

So a university now holds patents. It collects royalties. Departments budget around that revenue. Careers are built on research that leads somewhere commercial.

Universities and labs make money when a drug company buys the license. That is not a side effect. It is the business model.

Now put a study on broccoli sprouts into that system. No patent. No licensee. No royalties. No company willing to fund a large trial — and no one inside the university advocating for it, because there is nothing to sell at the end.

Nobody has to act in bad faith for this to shape everything. A question with an owner at the end of it gets funded. A question about sleep, or fasting, or a vegetable does not.

When you donate, you are funding an answer to a question that was narrowed before you gave.

The Research Itself Is Shakier Than You Have Been Led To Believe

You have probably assumed published medical research is basically trustworthy. The people who audit it do not.

One anesthesiologist examined 526 trials submitted to a journal and found 14% contained false data. When he obtained the individual patient records — the raw numbers behind the published summary, which most reviewers never see — the picture was worse.

One prominent researcher estimates hundreds of thousands of fabricated trials are circulating right now. Because almost none are retracted, they accumulate citations and end up shaping the guidelines your doctor follows.

Beyond outright fraud, there is slant. Systematic reviews find industry-funded studies are about 27% more likely to report favorable results and 34% more likely to reach favorable conclusions than independently funded ones.

Go deeper: how a study gets steered

No data has to be faked for that to happen. A study can be steered by who gets included and who gets excluded, what outcome is measured, how long people are followed, what the new treatment is compared against, and which findings get emphasized in the summary busy doctors actually read. A drug compared against a deliberately low dose of an older drug will look excellent. A trial stopped early, while the numbers happen to look good, will look better than one run to completion.

Your doctor is not lying to you. Your doctor is working from what was funded, published, and promoted — and that body of evidence was shaped long before it reached them.

The Pink Ribbon Has A History

Breast Cancer Awareness Month was created in 1985 by the American Cancer Society together with the pharmaceutical division of Imperial Chemical Industries — a chemical conglomerate whose products included pesticides and petrochemical derivatives. That division later became part of AstraZeneca, maker of tamoxifen.

A chemical company helped create the month, sold the treatment, and shaped a message built around screening and early detection.

Critics have noted for decades what that message leaves out: industrial pollution, chemical exposure, environmental carcinogens.

This is not a theory about a hidden cure. It is a documented conflict of interest at the origin of the entire awareness campaign — and it explains why you have heard get your mammogram a thousand times and never once heard reduce your alcohol, which is one of the clearest modifiable risks there is.

What This Guide Is Not

This is not an argument against medical care.

If you are diagnosed, surgery, chemotherapy, radiation, hormone therapy, and targeted treatments may save your life. Some of the finest people in medicine spend their careers doing exactly that work. Nothing here should keep you from treatment, or from a doctor you trust.

Treatment is not your only tool, and you were never handed the rest of them.

Your Body Is Not Passive

Your Body Is Not Passive
You are not sitting still waiting for a diagnosis.

Right now your body is repairing DNA, destroying damaged cells, retiring cells that can no longer divide safely, and running immune surveillance around the clock. Cancer does not happen because one cell went wrong. It happens when enough of those defenses are overwhelmed, over years, inside an internal environment that stopped supporting them.

That environment is built from what you eat, how you sleep, how much muscle you carry, how much insulin you circulate, what chemicals you absorb, and whether your body ever gets a break from digesting.

You cannot change your age or your genes. You can change nearly everything else — and how much of it you change is genuinely up to you.
