Breast Cancer Beyond Genetics · Part Eleven

Know Your Numbers

Every mechanism in this book is measurable. Almost none of it appears on a standard annual blood panel.

That is not because the tests are exotic or expensive. Most are cheap and widely available. They simply are not part of the routine, because the routine was designed to catch disease that has already arrived — not to measure the conditions that let it develop.

A normal annual physical can miss every single thing described in this book.

Why “Your Labs Are Normal” Can Be True And Useless

Two problems hide inside that sentence.

The first is what gets measured. A standard panel checks whether you are currently sick. It does not check insulin, it does not check how you clear estrogen, and it rarely checks vitamin D unless you ask.

The second is what “normal” means. Reference ranges are built from the population that walked into the lab — a population in which metabolic dysfunction is extremely common. Sitting inside that range means you resemble everyone else being tested. It does not mean the number is optimal.

Blood sugar is the clearest example. By the time fasting glucose rises enough to flag, insulin has usually been elevated for years — and insulin is the growth signal from Part Two. The warning light comes on long after the damage starts.

What To Ask For

What follows is organized by what it tells you, not by which panel it comes on. You can bring this list to a doctor and ask for it directly.

The Metabolic Picture

These tell you how much insulin your cells are living in — the thread running through nearly every chapter of this book.

Inflammation And Nutrient Status

hs-CRP
High-sensitivity C-reactive protein. A general measure of background inflammation — the constant repair signal from Part Two.
25(OH)D
Vitamin D. Most women are low, particularly in winter. In the VITAL trial, the benefit against advanced cancer appeared only in participants at a normal weight.
Ferritin
Iron storage. Both low and high matter — and loose iron is part of why IV vitamin C behaves the way it does in tumor cells.
RBC magnesium
Serum magnesium is a poor measure because your body defends blood levels at the expense of tissue. Red blood cell magnesium is more informative. Magnesium appears twice in Part Seven for good reason.
B12 and folate
The methylation nutrients. Depleted by acid-blocking medication and metformin.
Homocysteine
An indirect readout of whether your methylation is working. Elevated homocysteine suggests it is not.

The Hormone Picture

Here is where standard testing falls furthest short, and where the difference between tests actually matters.

What a blood test shows

A blood draw gives you a snapshot: how much estrogen and progesterone are circulating at the moment the needle went in.

That is genuinely useful information. What it cannot show you is which door your estrogen is leaving through, or whether you are neutralizing what comes out. Those are the two things Part Two identified as decisive, and a blood panel does not measure either.

What a dried urine panel shows

The DUTCH test — Dried Urine Test for Comprehensive Hormones — is the one most commonly used for this. You collect dried urine samples across a day, which also captures how hormones move over that day rather than at a single instant.

The pattern that matters most: high traffic through the damaging door, combined with poor methylation.

That combination is where estrogen-driven DNA damage accumulates — and it is modifiable, which is the entire reason to look.

Being straight with you about this test

The DUTCH test measures real biochemistry, and the research behind the estrogen routing pathway is solid.

It is not a cancer screening test. It does not detect cancer, it does not replace mammography or ultrasound, and it does not replace a risk model. A reassuring result is not a clean bill of health.

What it gives you is different and, for the purposes of this book, more useful: a picture of a process you can still change.

Your Risk Number

From Part Ten: the Tyrer-Cuzick model estimates your individual lifetime risk from family history, reproductive history, breast density, and prior biopsies.

Ask your provider to run it, or use the tool yourself and bring the result. This number should come before any screening decision, because the right plan at 8% lifetime risk is not the right plan at 30%.

Where To Start If You Only Do One Thing

Testing everything at once is expensive and unnecessary. If budget is the constraint, this is the order I would go in.

In priority order
  1. Fasting insulin. Cheapest, most informative, almost never offered.
  2. 25(OH)D. Widely available, commonly low, easily corrected.
  3. HbA1c and hs-CRP. Often already on your panel — just ask for the numbers.
  4. Tyrer-Cuzick risk score. Free, and it should precede any screening decision.
  5. A dried urine hormone panel, when you want to see the estrogen pathway itself.

Then Test Again

This is the part most people skip, and it is where the value actually lives.

Re-testing after three to six months tells you whether what you changed did anything in your body — not in a study population, not on average, in yours. It converts a set of recommendations into a feedback loop.

Test, change something, test again. That is the whole method, and it is what separates informed action from guessing.

It is also, honestly, the part that is hardest to do alone — not because the tests are complicated, but because interpreting them against your history, your medications, and your symptoms takes someone looking at the whole picture.
