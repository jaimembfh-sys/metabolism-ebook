Breast Cancer Beyond Genetics · Part Four

What You’re Absorbing

Almost none of this existed when your great-grandmother was born. Nearly all of it arrived in the same century in which breast cancer rates climbed.

Chemicals That Act Like Estrogen

Your body recognizes estrogen by its shape. A receptor sits inside the cell waiting for a molecule of the right form to arrive, and when one docks, the cell receives an instruction.

The problem is that a great many manufactured chemicals are shaped closely enough to fit. They dock. The instruction goes out. Your body never ordered it.

These are called xenoestrogens — foreign estrogens — and the Endocrine Society estimates more than 1,000 chemicals may act this way.

Go deeper: how dioxins change your estrogen processing

Some do more than imitate. Dioxins activate a receptor that increases production of CYP1B1 — the enzyme from Part Two that pushes estrogen down the damaging exit. So the exposure does not only add estrogen signal. It changes how your body processes the estrogen you already have.

You cannot avoid all of this. You can remove a surprising amount of it, and most of the changes cost nothing.

Two Things That Make This Worse Than It Sounds

Several of these store in fat and stay for years. Dioxins, PCBs, and PFAS are not flushed out in a week. They accumulate, which means today’s exposure adds to a running total rather than replacing yesterday’s.

Timing matters as much as dose. Exposure during prenatal development, puberty, pregnancy, and the menopausal transition carries more weight than the same exposure at other times, because those are the windows when breast tissue is actively developing and remodeling. Researchers call these windows of susceptibility.

Which is also why this chapter matters for your daughters and granddaughters, not only for you.

Plastic, And Why Heat Is The Multiplier

Plastic exposure is not one thing. It includes microplastic and nanoplastic particles, phthalates that make plastic flexible, bisphenols that make it rigid, and the additives blended into both.

Human studies have associated certain phthalate exposures with breast cancer, and laboratory work shows microplastics and nanoplastics affecting inflammation, oxidative stress, and the behavior of breast cancer cells.

Heat changes everything. A 2023 study found microwave heating released far more plastic particles than room-temperature storage or refrigeration — some containers shedding millions of microplastic and billions of nanoplastic particles per square centimeter within three minutes.

What to do
  • Never microwave in plastic. Move food to glass or ceramic first. This single change may be the highest-value item in this chapter.
  • No hot drinks in paper cups. The lining is plastic, and hot liquid is exactly the condition that releases it.
  • No Styrofoam, particularly for anything hot, oily, or acidic.
  • Glass or stainless steel for water. Never leave a plastic bottle in a hot car.
  • Replace scratched or cloudy plastic containers — damage accelerates shedding.
  • Store fatty and acidic foods in glass. Fat and acid pull more out of plastic than water does.
  • Do not put plastic through the dishwasher on high heat.

Aluminum cans are lined with plastic

This surprises most people. The inside of nearly every aluminum can carries an epoxy resin lining to keep the metal from reacting with the contents — and that lining has historically been made with BPA. Acidic contents and warm storage increase what migrates into the drink.

“BPA-free” often means the manufacturer substituted BPS or BPF, which are structurally similar and behave similarly in hormone assays.

Non-Stick Cookware

The slick coating on non-stick pans is PTFE, sold for decades as Teflon — and PTFE is itself a type of PFAS, the class often called forever chemicals. There are more than 14,000 identified PFAS compounds, linked to cancer, reproductive harm, and hormone disruption.

They are called forever chemicals because they resist breakdown. They persist for months or years in the body and, in the environment, may never break down at all. PFAS have been detected in breast milk and in the umbilical cord blood of newborns.

The breast cancer signal

A study of French women found a positive association between breast cancer risk and blood levels of PFOS measured before diagnosis — particularly for hormone receptor-positive tumors. Using a large US cohort, National Cancer Institute researchers found the same: a positive association between serum PFOS and hormone receptor-positive tumors.

The evidence is not uniform across every study, and the American Cancer Society describes the breast cancer link as possible rather than established. But the pattern — hormone receptor-positive specifically — is exactly what you would expect from a hormone-disrupting compound.

The labeling problem

When Consumer Reports tested non-stick pans, the PTFE-coated pan contained measurable PFOA and 15 other PFAS compounds, despite PFOA supposedly having been phased out. Ceramic-coated pans tested contained none of the 96 PFAS screened for.

“PFOA-free” is not the same as PFAS-free. Manufacturers dropped the one chemical with bad publicity and moved to a related one. California passed a law specifically to stop this labeling practice.

What to do
  • Cook on cast iron, stainless steel, glass, or ceramic. Cast iron and stainless are cheap, last decades, and have no coating to fail.
  • If you keep a non-stick pan: never preheat it empty, keep the heat moderate, use wood or silicone utensils, and never scrub it with steel wool.
  • Throw out any pan that is scratched, flaking, or worn. Damaged coating is the highest-exposure scenario.
  • Look for PTFE-free on the label, not just PFOA-free.
  • Do not reheat food in grease-resistant packaging — that coating is often PFAS too. Move it to a plate.

Your Water

Bottled water repeatedly tests higher for small plastic particles than treated tap water. So bottled is not the solution — and it comes in the container that creates the problem.

Tap water varies enormously by municipality and by well. PFAS contamination is widespread and not removed by ordinary pitcher filters.

What to do

A certified granular activated carbon or reverse osmosis filter reduces PFAS. Reverse osmosis is the more thorough of the two. If you are on a private well, have it tested. Then drink from glass or stainless steel.

Receipts

This one is small, strange, and almost entirely avoidable.

Thermal receipt paper is coated with free BPA — not bound into a polymer, just sitting on the surface, at roughly 20 milligrams per gram of paper. It transfers to skin on contact.

Now the part that matters. Hand sanitizers and lotions contain compounds that enhance skin penetration — and they can increase dermal absorption of a compound like BPA by up to 100-fold.

In a published experiment, volunteers used hand sanitizer, held a receipt, then ate French fries with their hands. Bioactive BPA in their blood rose dramatically within 90 minutes.

Of 50 receipts tested in one survey, 44% contained high BPA levels and 52% contained BPS — the replacement, which is hormonally similar. Over 70% of cashiers are women.

What to do

Decline the printed receipt; most terminals offer email. If you take one, do not handle it right after sanitizer, lotion, or sunscreen. Do not let children play with them. Wash your hands before eating with them.

Personal Care Products

The average woman applies a dozen or more products to her skin before leaving the house, and skin is an absorptive organ, not a barrier.

Antiperspirant

Aluminum salts are what make an antiperspirant different from a deodorant — they block sweat ducts. In the laboratory, aluminum enters cells, destabilizes chromosomes, interferes with DNA repair, and affects estrogen-receptor signaling in breast cancer cells. It has been measured in human breast tissue, at higher concentrations in the outer region nearest the underarm.

Go deeper: what the human studies on antiperspirant actually found

The human evidence is unsettled. A 2017 Austrian study of 209 breast cancer patients and 209 controls found women who used underarm products more than once daily starting before age 30 had nearly four times the odds of breast cancer, and aluminum levels in breast tissue tracked with reported use. But the range around that estimate was very wide, the subgroup driving it was small, and studies asking people to recall decades-old habits are vulnerable to bias.

A 2024 meta-analysis of seven studies found no overall association. The catch is that most of those studies never asked about frequency or age at first use — the very thing the 2017 study found mattered.

Where this leaves you. Average use has not been shown to raise risk. Heavy use beginning young is genuinely unstudied, and researchers reviewing this in 2023 recommended avoiding aluminum antiperspirants on precautionary grounds while noting aluminum-free deodorants have not been implicated.

Parabens, phthalates, and fragrance

Parabens are preservatives. They have been found intact in human breast tissue and can stimulate estrogen-responsive breast cells at concentrations measured in real samples.

Fragrance is the loophole. A single word on an ingredient list can legally conceal dozens of undisclosed compounds, including phthalates, because the mixture is treated as a trade secret. Lotion, shampoo, makeup, air freshener, laundry detergent, and dryer sheets all use it.

A small human study found that avoiding paraben- and phthalate-containing personal care products for 28 days lowered urinary exposure markers and reversed several cancer-associated signaling patterns in non-cancerous breast cells. Four weeks was enough to measure a change.

What to do

Aluminum-free, paraben-free deodorant — and do not apply it right after shaving or onto irritated skin. Choose fragrance-free rather than unscented, which often means a masking fragrance was added. Replace products as they run out rather than throwing everything away at once.

Your Food Supply

Produce

Several widely used pesticides are xenoestrogens — organochlorines in particular have well-established estrogen-mimicking activity. You do not have to buy everything organic to reduce most of the exposure.

Prioritize organic for thin-skinned produce you eat whole: berries, apples, grapes, peaches, cherries, spinach, kale, peppers, celery, tomatoes. Worry less about thick-skinned or peeled items: avocado, onion, citrus, banana, melon, sweet corn.

Meat and fat

Dioxins concentrate in animal fat, and human exposure is primarily through food. Conventional livestock may also carry hormone residues.

Practical version: choose leaner cuts of conventional meat, or better-raised fattier cuts. If you can only upgrade one thing, upgrade the fat — that is where these compounds accumulate.

Packaging

Grease-resistant wrappers, microwave popcorn bags, and takeout containers frequently carry PFAS coatings. Move takeout to a plate before eating and never reheat in the container.

Your Home

Flame retardants
Brominated compounds coat furniture, carpet padding, electronics, and car seats. They migrate into household dust. Vacuum with a HEPA filter and damp-dust rather than dry-dusting, which just moves it into the air.
Cleaning products
Fragrance, quaternary ammonium compounds, and undisclosed mixtures. Vinegar, baking soda, and castile soap handle most household cleaning.
Laundry
Detergent and dryer sheets deposit fragrance compounds directly onto fabric that sits against your skin for hours. Fragrance-free detergent and wool dryer balls are a one-time swap.
Air fresheners
Plug-ins and sprays are concentrated fragrance delivery. Open a window instead.
Dust
Household dust is where flame retardants, phthalates, and PFAS accumulate. Regular damp cleaning removes more exposure than most people expect.

Where To Actually Begin

That list is long, and reading it in one sitting is overwhelming by design — not mine, but the environment’s. You did not build this and you cannot fix all of it.

So start where the exposure is highest and the change is cheapest.

The five that matter most
  1. Never microwave in plastic — glass or ceramic instead.
  2. Replace scratched non-stick pans with cast iron or stainless.
  3. Filter your water and drink from glass or steel.
  4. Switch to aluminum-free, fragrance-free personal care as products run out.
  5. No hot drinks in paper or Styrofoam cups.

None of those requires a budget. Four of the five are one-time changes you make once and never think about again — which is the opposite of a diet, and the reason this category is worth your attention.

You were not asked whether you wanted these compounds in your kitchen. You do get to decide how many of them stay.
