# 8.1 >>> SAFETY GATE — READ FIRST

## SGLT2 inhibitors — absolute escalation

**Any generic name ending in "-flozin."** Also present in many combination products, so **ask the user to check the bottle** rather than rely on recall.

| Generic | Common brand |
|---|---|
| canagliflozin | Invokana |
| dapagliflozin | Farxiga / Forxiga |
| empagliflozin | Jardiance |
| ertugliflozin | Steglatro |
| bexagliflozin | Brenzavvy |

**Also in combinations** — Xigduo, Synjardy, Invokamet, Glyxambi, Qtern, Trijardy, Steglujan.

**>>> Increasingly prescribed beyond diabetes** — for heart failure and chronic kidney disease, including in people **without** diabetes. A user may be taking one and not associate it with blood sugar at all. **Ask about all medications, not just diabetes medications.**

### The mechanism, and why it's dangerous

SGLT2 inhibitors block renal glucose reabsorption, producing glucosuria. Blood glucose falls independent of insulin.

**The risk is what that removes.** In ordinary DKA, ketone accumulation is accompanied by **hyperglycemia** — and that hyperglycemia is the clinical flag that triggers investigation.

**SGLT2 inhibitors decouple the two.** Glucose continues being excreted while ketogenesis proceeds, producing **euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis** — true metabolic acidosis with normal or even low blood glucose.

**Consequence: it gets missed.** By patients checking their own glucose and concluding they're fine, and by clinicians including in emergency departments, because the presenting glucose doesn't fit.

**Contributing:** SGLT2 inhibition lowers insulin secretion and raises glucagon, both ketogenic. **Carbohydrate restriction adds a third ketogenic input to a primed system.**

**Compounding risk factors:** carbohydrate restriction or fasting, acute illness, vomiting or diarrhoea, dehydration, surgery or procedural fasting, alcohol, reduced insulin dose, strenuous fasted exercise.

**>>> A user who is low-carb AND unwell AND on one of these is in the highest-risk configuration. Sick-day guidance is a prescriber matter.**

### >>> MANDATORY COACH SEQUENCE

1. **No ketogenic or low-carbohydrate protocol.** Not modified, not gradual, not "just cut the obvious carbs." **Route first.**
2. **State the reason plainly, without alarm.** She needs to understand *why* or she'll treat it as boilerplate. Framing: *this isn't a warning against eating this way — it's a warning against doing it without your prescriber knowing, because this medication removes the signal that would tell you something's wrong.*
3. **Give her the script:** *"I'm planning to significantly reduce my carbohydrates. I understand that can increase ketoacidosis risk on this medication. How would you like to handle it?"*
4. **Give her the warning signs** — nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, unusual fatigue, confusion, deep or rapid breathing, fruity-smelling breath. **And the critical instruction: do not wait for high blood sugar. It may never come.**
5. **Same-day care if any appear**, telling the clinician **both** that she's on an SGLT2 inhibitor and that she's changed her diet. **Say explicitly that this is the combination that gets missed.**
6. **Stay useful on what's in scope.** Food quality, protein, meal structure, sleep, movement. **Do not treat the referral as the end of the conversation.**

## Other absolute stops

**Type 1 diabetes.** Low-carbohydrate eating is done in T1D but requires insulin adjustment and supervision. **No protocols. And never discuss the relationship between reduced insulin and weight loss** — Lesson 2 layer, absolute.

**Insulin or sulfonylureas** (glipizide, glyburide, glimepiride, gliclazide) → hypoglycemia risk. Prescriber first. **Never suggest a dose adjustment.**

**Pregnancy or trying to conceive.**

**Any disordered eating signal** — a restrictive framework is contraindicated. No carbohydrate targets, no ketone targets, no fasting protocols. **Escalate** per referral framework pattern 12.

**Fatty acid oxidation disorders, carnitine deficiency, pyruvate carboxylase deficiency, porphyria.**

**History of pancreatitis or active gallbladder disease.**

**Active cancer treatment** — see 8.5.

## Requires caution

**Antihypertensives** — carbohydrate restriction lowers blood pressure; dose may become excessive. **New lightheadedness on standing is a prescriber conversation, not an electrolyte problem to solve with salt.**

**Cholecystectomy or fat intolerance** — bile capacity *(Lesson 11 layer)*.

**Chronic kidney disease.**

**>>> DEFAULT:** where any of the above applies, discuss the physiology without providing a protocol, and route. **"Talk to your doctor first" is the correct answer, not a failure to help** — pair it with what specifically to raise.

# WHAT THE LESSON TEACHES

1. When insulin is low, the liver converts fatty acids into ketones — principally beta-hydroxybutyrate
2. The brain cannot use long-chain fatty acids, so ketones are how fat fuels the brain
3. Ketones produce fewer free radicals than glucose and act as **signalling molecules**
4. Ketosis provides access to a large internal fuel supply, stabilizing energy and reducing cravings
5. **Transition takes days to weeks** — "keto flu" reflects a temporary energy gap plus a sodium/fluid shift
6. Electrolytes and B vitamins (B1, B5) matter during transition
7. **Keto can fail the chronically ill** — gluconeogenic muscle breakdown and impaired T4→T3 conversion
8. **Ketones are complementary to declining estradiol in menopause**
9. The goal is metabolic flexibility, not permanent deep ketosis
10. **(New)** Why ketones exist at all; the CPT-1 gate and its carrier; ketosis vs. fat loss with the futile cycle; the under-eating failure mode; measurement; LDL; the forever question

# DEPTH BEHIND EACH CLAIM

## 8.2 Why ketones exist — tissue specificity

**[ESTABLISHED]** Long-chain fatty acids cross the blood-brain barrier poorly. Ketones do. **Ketogenesis exists to supply the brain**, not because fat generally requires conversion.

**Skeletal muscle and cardiac muscle oxidize fatty acids directly**, no intermediate step. The heart derives the majority of its ATP from fatty acid oxidation under normal conditions — **it is the most fat-dependent organ.**

**>>> COACH USE:** important precision. The published lesson correctly says the brain can't use fatty acids, which can leave a user believing fat generally needs converting. **Ketones are the workaround for one organ; everything else was already burning fat as it is.** Reframes what "being in ketosis" means.

## 8.3 BHB as a signalling molecule

**[ESTABLISHED]** **Shimazu T, et al.** *Science*. 2013;339(6116):211–214. **PMID 23223453.**

BHB is a specific endogenous inhibitor of class I HDACs. Exogenous BHB, fasting, and calorie restriction all increased global histone acetylation in mouse tissues. HDAC inhibition increased acetylation at the **Foxo3a** and **Mt2** promoters; downstream antioxidant targets include MnSOD/SOD2 and catalase. **BHB-treated mice showed substantial protection against paraquat.**

Also: BHB inhibits the **NLRP3 inflammasome** — the principal anti-inflammatory mechanism. *Verify citation.* Lysine β-hydroxybutyrylation is a separate transcriptional mechanism.

**[CONTESTED] Magnitude.** Butyrate is a considerably more potent HDAC inhibitor than BHB in side-by-side comparison. Much of the protective data is rodent.

**>>> COACH USE:** this **extends** the lesson's claim that ketones act as signalling molecules. It's the mechanism, and it's striking — ketones instruct cells to raise their own antioxidant defenses.

## 8.4 The CPT-1 gate and its carrier

**[ESTABLISHED]** Long-chain fatty acids require **CPT-1** for mitochondrial entry. Glucose and insulin drive acetyl-CoA carboxylase to produce **malonyl-CoA**, which inhibits CPT-1. Malonyl-CoA is the committed precursor for de novo lipogenesis — its presence is a storage signal.

**Consequence:** fatty acids can be abundant in the cytosol and unable to enter mitochondria. **Availability isn't the constraint; access is.**

**Primary sources:**
- McGarry JD, Mannaerts GP, Foster DW. *J Clin Invest*. 1977;60(1):265–270. **PMID 874089**
- McGarry JD, Leatherman GF, Foster DW. *J Biol Chem*. 1978;253:4128–4136
- McGarry JD, Foster DW. *J Biol Chem*. 1979;254(17):8163–8168 — the fasting increase in fat oxidation and ketogenesis is accounted for by removal of malonyl-CoA inhibition plus rising hepatic carnitine

**Same node controls hepatic ketogenesis** — the gate and ketone production are one mechanism.

**>>> THE CARRIER — new, and it links three lessons.** CPT-1 transport requires **carnitine**, which is synthesized from **lysine and methionine** in a **vitamin C–dependent** pathway *(Lesson 4 and Lesson 9 layers)*.

**So fat oxidation capacity depends on adequate protein and vitamin C**, not only on insulin. The gate can be unlocked with insufficient carrier available. **Verify the carnitine biosynthesis pathway before the coach states it as fact.**

**Medium-chain fatty acids bypass CPT-1.** In McGarry 1977, malonyl-CoA inhibited ketogenesis from oleate (C18) but **not** octanoate (C8). **The mechanistic basis for MCT behaving differently — known since 1977.**

**AMPK opens the gate.** Contraction-activated AMPK inactivates acetyl-CoA carboxylase and accelerates malonyl-CoA degradation. **Muscle contraction hits the same regulatory node as insulin, from the opposite direction** *(Lesson 17 layer)*.

**Two timescales:** allosteric regulation in minutes to hours, fully reversible. Enzyme expression and mitochondrial biogenesis over weeks *(Lesson 4 layer)*.

**>>> COACH USE — the most explanatory single mechanism in the knowledge base.** Answers why carbohydrate amount matters more than calories, why "a little bit" stalls people, why post-meal walking works, why MCT helps during transition, why protein and vitamin C matter for fat burning, and why week six differs from week one. **Use it often.**

## 8.5 >>> BHB AND CANCER — DO NOT OVERSTATE

**[CONTESTED — evidence runs both directions]**

The "ketones fight cancer" claim circulates widely. Preclinical work exists both ways. **A study of BHB in mammary tumours reported *increased* tumour growth**, described by the authors as a possible "β-OHB paradox."

**>>> COACH RULE — ABSOLUTE:**
- **Never state or imply that ketosis treats, prevents, or slows cancer**
- **Never discourage or comment on any oncological treatment**
- User with a cancer diagnosis or history: acknowledge active research interest, state plainly that the evidence is not settled and includes contrary findings, **route to oncology**
- **No ketogenic protocol during active treatment without clinician involvement**

## 8.6 Ketosis is not fat loss — and the mechanism

**[ESTABLISHED]** Nutritional ketosis indicates fat oxidation, not net fat mass reduction. **Dietary fat is oxidized preferentially over stored fat**; sufficient dietary fat permits sustained ketosis with minimal mobilization of endogenous stores.

**>>> THE MECHANISM — the triglyceride/fatty acid futile cycle.** New, and it's what makes this concrete rather than a caveat.

**Reshef L, et al.** *J Biol Chem*. 2003;278(33):30413–30416: **"As much as 60% of the total fatty acids formed by lipolysis are re-esterified in fasting humans."** Explicitly described as a **"futile cycle"** — six ATP required to activate three fatty acids for re-synthesis.

**Hanson RW, Reshef L.** *Biochimie*. 2003;85(12):1199–1205. **PMID 14739071:** up to 65% re-esterified. The pathway — **glyceroneogenesis** — is **active during fasting**, with PEPCK-C transcription *induced* in adipose tissue during fasting.

**White adipose tissue has negligible glycerol kinase**, so it cannot reuse the glycerol it releases — it must build a new backbone, from glucose when available or via glyceroneogenesis when not.

**>>> COACH USE — HIGH VALUE:** the correct answer when a user reports positive ketone readings, good energy, no cravings, and no body composition change. **Ketosis means the door is open; it says nothing about net direction.** The determinant is whether tissues need the energy — if dietary fat covers requirements, released fatty acids are repackaged.

**The practical lever is typically added fats** — those not attached to a protein source. **Subject to gating:** qualitative only, no numeric fat targets, never with a disordered eating signal present.

*(Full treatment in the Lesson 9 layer.)*

## 8.7 Carbohydrate thresholds

**[HEURISTIC — not validated]** The lesson's under-40 g and under-20 g figures are practical starting points. Individual thresholds vary substantially with insulin sensitivity, activity, muscle mass, and adaptation state.

**>>> COACH RULE:** present as starting points. **Note the ≥50 g finding** — Spaulding 1976 found carbohydrate at or above roughly 50 g prevented the T3 decline. **The threshold recommendation and the thyroid caveat are directly linked; mention them together.**

**>>> Suppress numeric targets under gating.**

## 8.8 Who keto fails, and why

**[SUPPORTED]** The lesson's "Why Keto Can Fail the Chronically Ill" section is substantively sound and unusually honest. Both named mechanisms are real: **gluconeogenic protein breakdown** under perceived famine, and **impaired hepatic T4→T3 conversion** *(Lesson 5 layer)*.

**>>> TWO ADDITIONS THE COACH SUPPLIES:**

**1. The T3 effect is broader than the chronically ill.** It occurs in essentially all carbohydrate-restricted individuals and is **carbohydrate-specific rather than calorie-driven** (Spaulding 1976: T3 ↓47% on isocaloric no-carbohydrate diet; no significant change at ≥50 g). The lesson is accurate about who is most *at risk*; it understates the scope.

**2. The most common failure mode is unintentional under-eating**, not chronic illness. Appetite suppression is a primary mechanism of low-carbohydrate eating and produces inadvertent deficit in otherwise healthy people. Connects to Lesson 3, Lesson 5 (low energy availability, sex difference), Lesson 7 (leptin as permissive signal).

**Sex difference [SUPPORTED — single study]:** in one-day fasting among obese adults, female participants showed altered cortisol rhythm — higher amplitude, earlier peak — while **none of the rhythm parameters changed in males** *(Front Nutr 2023;10:1078508)*.

**>>> THE TIMELINE IS DIAGNOSTIC** *(Lesson 5 layer)*: days 2–14 resolving = adaptation. **Onset at, or persistence past, week 4–6 = investigate. Never advise pushing through past week six.**

**>>> The correction for under-eating is increased intake.** Counterintuitive and correct. **Subject to disordered eating gating.**

## 8.9 Ketones and menopause — sourced

**[SUPPORTED — primary literature now identified]**

**Origin of the course's section:** closely tracks **Bikman's "Ketones vs. Menopause: A Natural Metabolic Solution"** (Insulin IQ), including its five-systems structure. The "as estradiol wanes, allow ketones to wax" phrasing is very likely his. **Attribute to Bikman by name; cite Brinton for the mechanism.**

**Brinton RD, Yao J, Yin F, Mack WJ, Cadenas E.** Perimenopause as a neurological transition state. *Nature Reviews Endocrinology*. 2015;11(7):393–405. **PMID 26007613.**
- **Estrogen is a master regulator of brain energy metabolism** via a network of estrogen receptors
- **During the perimenopausal transition the estrogen receptor network becomes *uncoupled* from the bioenergetic system**, and a hypometabolic state associated with neurological dysfunction can develop
- **Neuronal glucose transporter levels decline, coincident with brain hypometabolism**
- Frames perimenopause as a **"window of opportunity"**

**Ding F, Yao J, Rettberg JR, Chen S, Brinton RD.** *PLoS One*. 2013;8(11):e79977. **PMID 24244584.**
- Brain glucose transport declined at **6–9 months, just before reproductive senescence**; sustained thereafter
- **"An adaptive response in the hippocampus was a shift to transport and utilization of ketone bodies as an alternative fuel"**

**Ovariectomy and hippocampal fuel shift** (PMC3608536): declining glucose availability with **rising serum ketones**, coupled to **increased neuronal MCT2** (ketone transporter) **and SCOT** (the enzyme converting ketones to acetyl-CoA). **17β-estradiol preserved glucose metabolic capacity.** Clinical corroboration: estrogen-therapy users show higher cerebral blood flow and metabolism; non-users' decline in posterior cingulate and prefrontal cortex **closely resembled the hypometabolic profile of Alzheimer's brains.**

**Mosconi L, et al.** *Scientific Reports*. 2021;11. **Human data.** Higher cerebral blood flow and ATP in postmenopausal women interpreted as compensatory response to glucose hypometabolism and a means to increase ketone metabolism.

### >>> The white matter point — handle carefully

**[HYPOTHESIS — animal data, cited within a human paper]**

Mosconi 2021 states: *"Continued reliance on ketones leads to compromised mitochondrial function, WM catabolism, and cellular apoptosis in animals."*

**The course's inference:** the menopausal brain, short of glucose, generates ketones from available lipid **including its own white matter** — so dietary ketones may spare that tissue.

**>>> COACH RULES:**
- **Animal data, and the sparing inference is reasoning, not a finding.** Present it that way
- **May say:** the brain's shift toward ketones in menopause is well documented, and the source of those ketones plausibly matters
- **Must not say:** that a ketogenic diet has been shown to prevent white matter loss, protect against dementia, or preserve cognition in women
- **Do not use this to frighten a user into carbohydrate restriction.** It's a mechanism, not a prognosis

**>>> VERIFY before this reaches published copy.** The most rhetorically powerful sentence in the chapter, which is exactly why it must be right.

### >>> What the coach can and cannot say

**Can say:**
- Estradiol regulates brain glucose metabolism; its decline is associated with measurable brain hypometabolism — published in *Nature Reviews Endocrinology*
- Postmenopausal brain imaging shows reduced cerebral glucose metabolism, most pronounced in the posterior cingulate
- The brain adaptively upregulates ketone transport and ketone-utilizing enzymes as glucose access declines
- **Brain fog in perimenopause has a documented metabolic basis. It is not imagined, and it is not simply stress or age.** *(The single highest-value thing the coach can tell a perimenopausal user.)*
- The researchers describing this propose bioenergetic intervention and call perimenopause a window of opportunity

**Cannot say:**
- That ketones replace or substitute for estradiol
- That a ketogenic diet prevents dementia, Alzheimer's, or cognitive decline
- **Anything for or against hormone therapy — absolute** *(referral framework pattern 8)*
- That dietary ketones improving long-term outcomes in women is established. **It isn't** — the shift is documented; the therapeutic benefit is a hypothesis under study

**>>> Always pair with the HRT note.** Hormone therapy addresses the decline directly, is a legitimate option, the risk picture has been substantially revised since original WHI reporting, and it's a clinician conversation. **The two approaches address different parts of the same problem and are not in competition.**

## 8.10 Keto flu and electrolytes

**[ESTABLISHED]** Reduced insulin decreases renal sodium reabsorption, producing natriuresis and fluid loss. Symptoms — fatigue, headache, fog, cramping — largely reflect sodium and fluid shifts plus incomplete fat-oxidation capacity.

**>>> CALIBRATION:** the natriuresis is **largely front-loaded to the first one to two weeks**; renal adaptation follows. **Do not assume persisting sodium depletion in a long-term low-carbohydrate eater who salts to taste.**

**>>> SODIUM SAFETY:** never recommend liberal sodium to a user reporting **hypertension, kidney disease, heart failure, or taking antihypertensives** without routing to her physician. Published Lessons 8 and 15 both advise salting liberally without this caveat.

**[UNVERIFIED]** The lesson's claim that B1 and B5 demand "skyrockets" during transition. Thiamine and pantothenate are genuine cofactors *(Lesson 4 layer)*, but **the magnitude claim is unsourced. Do not quantify it.**

## 8.11 Measurement

**[ESTABLISHED]**
- **Urine acetoacetate strips** — useful roughly the first one to two weeks. **Become unreliable with adaptation as renal ketone excretion falls.** A fading strip typically indicates *better* adaptation, not loss of ketosis. **A very common cause of premature abandonment.**
- **Blood BHB meters** — the accurate option
- **Breath acetone** — reasonable correlation, less validated

**>>> WATCH FOR QUANTIFICATION SPIRALS.** A user tracking ketones, glucose, weight, macros, HRV, and sleep simultaneously may be in a pattern where more data is worsening outcomes. **Symptomatic markers suffice for most users** — stable energy, absent cravings, comfortable fasting intervals. **If measurement behaviour appears compulsive or distressing, reduce rather than add.**

## 8.12 Exogenous ketones

**[ESTABLISHED]** Ketone salts and esters reliably raise circulating BHB. They also **suppress endogenous ketogenesis** via negative feedback — elevated circulating ketones reduce hepatic production and reduce lipolysis.

**>>> COACH RULE:** a ketone reading produced by a supplement carries **no information** about endogenous fat oxidation. **Do not recommend exogenous ketones for fat loss.** Limited legitimate uses: transition support, specific cognitive or athletic applications. **Never frame as a shortcut.**

## 8.13 LDL elevation

**[CONTESTED]** A subset — disproportionately lean and otherwise metabolically healthy — exhibits substantial LDL-C and ApoB elevation on carbohydrate restriction.

**>>> COACH RULE:**
- **Do not dismiss it.** When triglycerides are low, LDL-C and ApoB tend to be **concordant** — a marked rise in a low-triglyceride person is more likely accurate than misleading *(Lesson 3 layer)*
- **Recommend ApoB** as the clarifying test
- **Route to physician. Never advise on statins or lipid-lowering medication — absolute**
- **Do not take a side in the hyper-responder debate.** State that it's unresolved

## 8.14 Duration and metabolic flexibility

**[FRAMEWORK]** The lesson correctly identifies metabolic flexibility rather than sustained ketosis as the endpoint.

**>>> COACH USE:** strict ketosis is generally a means, not a destination. Carbohydrate reintroduction after adaptation is appropriate for most users. Some populations may benefit from sustained restriction — significant insulin resistance, certain neurological indications.

**>>> WATCH FOR:** a user expressing **fear** about reintroducing carbohydrate, distress at a higher strip reading, or framing foods as forbidden. **These are disordered eating signals and take precedence over any metabolic discussion** *(referral framework pattern 12; Lesson 18 layer)*.

# COMMON USER QUESTIONS

**"I take Jardiance. Can I do keto?"**

**Not without talking to your prescriber first, and here's exactly why.**

That medication makes your kidneys flush sugar out through your urine. Which means if your ketones ever climbed to a dangerous level, **your blood sugar would still read normal.** The high blood sugar that normally warns everyone something is wrong just isn't there. It's called euglycemic ketoacidosis and it gets missed for exactly that reason.

**This isn't a reason you can't eat this way.** It's a reason your doctor needs to be part of it. Say: *"I'm planning to significantly reduce my carbohydrates. I know that raises ketoacidosis risk on this medication. How do you want to handle it?"*

**Know the warning signs regardless:** nausea, vomiting, stomach pain, deep or fast breathing, unusual fatigue or confusion, fruity breath. **If any appear, get seen the same day and tell them you're on this medication and changed your diet. Don't wait for high blood sugar — it may not come.**

**>>> Then offer to help with what's in scope. Do not end on the referral.**

**"My ketone strips stopped showing anything. Did I fall out of ketosis?"**

**Almost certainly the opposite.** As you adapt, your body stops wasting ketones in your urine because it's getting better at using them. **The strips fade exactly when you're doing well.**

This is one of the most common reasons people quit. If you want an accurate number, use a blood meter. **Or stop measuring** — stable energy, no cravings, and comfortably going five hours tells you more than a strip.

**"I'm in ketosis but not losing weight."**

**Ketosis means the door is open. It doesn't tell you which way the traffic is going.**

Your fat cells release and repackage fat constantly — in fasting people, researchers have measured **up to 60% of released fatty acids going straight back into storage** rather than being burned. What decides the direction is whether your body actually needs the energy. **If you've eaten enough fat to cover it, there's no reason to burn what you released.**

**Look at added fats specifically** — butter in coffee, extra oil, fat bombs. Useful during transition, frequently the reason things stall afterward.

**>>> Under gating: qualitative only, no numbers.**

**"I feel terrible. Should I push through?"**

**Depends entirely on timing.**

Rough in the first two weeks is normal — the transition plus the sodium and fluid shift. Electrolytes and adequate salt usually fix it.

**Still rough at week six, or newly rough at week six, is not adaptation.** That's a signal. The most common causes are under-eating, thyroid, or electrolytes. **Don't push through past six weeks.**

**>>> Sodium advice is gated — not for hypertension, kidney disease, heart failure, or antihypertensives without her physician.**

**"I'm never hungry. Is that a problem?"**

It can be, and this is the most common way low-carb goes wrong in healthy people. **Appetite suppression is a feature — and it's how people end up eating far less than they intended without deciding to.**

Under-eating is what causes the thyroid drop, the cold hands, the hair thinning, and the stall. **Eat your protein on a schedule rather than waiting to want it.** And if you feel worse rather than better after the first couple of weeks, eating more is usually the correction.

**>>> Disordered eating signal present → escalate rather than discussing intake.**

**"Why is my brain so foggy since perimenopause started?"**

**Because there's a measurable metabolic reason, and almost nobody tells women this.**

Estradiol regulates how your brain takes up and uses glucose. As it declines, research published in *Nature Reviews Endocrinology* describes the estrogen system becoming **uncoupled** from your brain's energy machinery — the glucose transporters in your neurons decline, and imaging shows reduced glucose metabolism in postmenopausal women.

**So your brain is genuinely producing less energy. That's not stress and it's not your imagination.**

Your brain does adapt — it shifts toward using ketones, upregulating the machinery to bring them in and burn them. Which is part of why eating this way can help.

**Two honest things:** this is active research rather than settled treatment. And hormone therapy addresses the decline directly and is a legitimate option worth discussing with a practitioner who's current on it — **that's not something I can advise on.**

**"Will keto protect my brain / prevent Alzheimer's?"**

**I can't tell you that, and anyone who does is going beyond what's known.**

Documented: the menopausal brain shows reduced glucose metabolism, adaptively shifts toward ketones, and researchers propose nutritional ketosis as a strategy worth investigating.

**Not established: that it changes long-term outcomes.** The researchers describing this most carefully call perimenopause a "window of opportunity" — the language of a promising hypothesis, not a proven treatment.

The reasoning is sound. It's still reasoning.

**"Should I do keto or take HRT?"**

**That's a false choice, and the HRT half isn't mine to advise on.** Hormone therapy addresses the hormone decline directly; current understanding of its risks differs substantially from the headlines of twenty years ago. **Talk to a gynecologist or menopause specialist who's current.**

This course covers the metabolic side — fuel, insulin, muscle, inflammation. **Different parts of the same problem, not in competition.**

**"Should I take exogenous ketones?"**

They'll raise your blood ketones and won't help you burn your own fat — **if anything they reduce it in the moment**, because your liver makes fewer when they arrive from outside. A high reading from a supplement tells you nothing about fat loss.

Possibly useful during transition. **Not a shortcut.**

**"How many carbs can I have?"**

Starting points, not laws — individual thresholds vary a lot.

**One thing worth knowing:** research found carbohydrate at roughly 50 grams or above prevented the thyroid change that happens on very low intake. **If you're getting cold, tired, or your cycle is off, that's a number to keep in mind.**

**>>> Suppress specifics under gating.**

**"Is keto good for cancer?"**

**Actively researched and not settled** — preclinical evidence runs in both directions, including studies suggesting harm in specific tumour types. **Route to oncology. Do not encourage.**

*Epilepsy is different — genuinely established, and where the diet originated medically.*

**>>> Never claim ketosis treats any disease.**

**"My cholesterol went way up."**

This happens to a meaningful minority, often the leanest people, and **I wouldn't dismiss it.**

**Get an ApoB test** — it counts actual particles rather than the cholesterol inside them. Then take the whole panel to your doctor.

**>>> Never advise on statins. Never take a side on the hyper-responder debate.**

**"Do I have to do this forever?"**

**No, and most people shouldn't.** The point of the strict phase is regaining an ability you'd lost. Once you have it, the goal becomes being *able* to get into fat-burning easily.

**What matters is that you're choosing it rather than trapped in it.** If reintroducing a sweet potato feels frightening, that's worth paying attention to separately.

**>>> Fear of carbohydrate reintroduction is a signal. Escalate rather than reassure.**
