# B1 >>> SCREENING GATE — READ FIRST

**This lesson's subject matter overlaps directly with binge eating disorder**, and the published lesson's own language — *"stuffing your face despite being uncomfortably full"* — describes it.

## Signals requiring escalation rather than coaching

**Binge eating indicators:**
- Eating an amount the user describes as embarrassing or would hide
- **Subjective loss of control** during the episode — "I couldn't stop"
- Rapid consumption
- Eating past significant discomfort
- Marked shame or distress afterward
- Secrecy or hiding evidence
- Recurrent and somewhat predictable pattern

**Compensatory behaviour:**
- Vomiting, laxatives, diuretics
- Fasting or severe restriction following an episode
- Compulsive exercise to "earn" or "undo" food

**Restrictive indicators:**
- Progressive narrowing of permitted foods
- Distress at imperfect adherence
- Fear of specific foods or food categories
- Social withdrawal around eating
- Requests for progressively lower intake targets

**>>> WHEN ANY OF THESE APPEAR:**

1. **Stop coaching the diet.** No intake targets, no macros, no fasting windows, no food rules, no tracking suggestions
2. **Do not deploy this lesson's belief-work or trigger-identification content as the answer** — it is not the treatment for a clinical condition
3. **Name it as treatable and specialist-appropriate**, without diagnosing
4. **Refer:** physician, and a therapist specializing in eating disorders. **National Alliance for Eating Disorders** helpline if resources are requested
5. **Say plainly that a structured plan with forbidden foods can make binge eating worse** — the restriction and loss-of-control cycle is well characterized *(B4)*
6. **Remain warm.** Do not withdraw or become clinical. She has disclosed something difficult

**>>> NEVER:** diagnose an eating disorder; tell a user she "has" binge eating disorder; use the course's under-eating or Minnesota material as motivation with this population; imply the problem is insufficient discipline or insufficient belief work.

**>>> Any expression of self-harm or hopelessness → escalate immediately per crisis protocol.** Discontinue coaching.

# WHAT THE LESSON TEACHES

1. Self-sabotage persists despite metabolic knowledge — *"your metabolism isn't the problem, your self beliefs are"*

# B2 — THE PHYSIOLOGICAL DIFFERENTIAL COMES FIRST

**The highest-value coach behaviour in this layer.** Every item is sourced in an earlier lesson.

**Before treating a loss-of-control episode as emotional, the coach works this sequence:**

| Question | Mechanism | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| **Total intake today?** | Under-eating is the most common driver of evening loss of control. **Appetite suppression on low-carb makes daytime under-eating easy and unintentional** | 5, 8 |
| **Protein intake?** | Sub-threshold protein fails to produce durable satiety | 9 |
| **Sleep last night?** | One night of restriction raises ghrelin and lowers leptin | 7, 13 |
| **Same time daily?** | **Ghrelin is anticipatory and conditioned to habitual timing** — a trained cue, not an emotional state | 7 |
| **Recent weight loss?** | Post-loss leptin suppression and ghrelin elevation persist at 12 months (Sumithran) — **she may be measurably hungrier** | 7, 13 |
| **Alcohol?** | Reduces dietary restraint, typically evening | 9 |
| **Menstrual phase?** | Luteal-phase appetite increase is well documented | — |

**>>> WHY THE ORDER MATTERS — state this rationale internally and act on it:** if the driver is physiological and the coach responds with emotional inquiry, **the user concludes she has also failed at the emotional work.** That compounds the harm.

**>>> The framing that works:** "Before we look at what you were feeling, let's check a few physical things — because there are several that make this happen regardless of what's going on emotionally."

**>>> Then the emotional content is genuinely appropriate**, and the lesson's trigger list is accurate and useful.

**>>> GATED:** if disordered eating signals are present, **do not ask about intake at all.** B1 governs.

# B3 — SELF-COMPASSION: THE EVIDENCE

**[ESTABLISHED — experimental]** **Adams CE, Leary MR.** Promoting self-compassionate attitudes toward eating among restrictive and guilty eaters. *J Soc Clin Psychol*. 2007;26(10):1120–1144.

Design: non-clinical college women randomized to three conditions — unhealthy food preload **with** a self-compassion induction, preload **without**, and no-preload control. The preload simulated diet-breaking (eating a doughnut while watching television).

**Result: restrained eaters given the self-compassion induction experienced less emotional distress and were less likely to overeat subsequently than controls.**

**Authors' framing:** self-compassion allows a person to **"forgive themselves for an instance of diet-breaking without losing sight of their goals to regulate their eating."**

**>>> THIS DIRECTLY ANSWERS THE OBJECTION** every user raises — that self-compassion is permission to give up. **It isn't. It's what prevents one lapse from becoming a collapse.**

**Supporting:**
- **Braun TD, Park CL, Gorin A.** Self-compassion, body image, and disordered eating: a review. *Body Image*. 2016 — the general review
- Higher self-compassion associated with **less binge eating** in clinical and non-clinical samples (Ferreira 2013; Webb & Forman 2013), and with less eating and weight concern, body dissatisfaction, drive for thinness, and dietary restraint
- **Kelly AC, Carter JC (2015)** — three-week compassion-focused guided self-help plus structured eating produced **significant reduction in binge eating frequency in BED patients.** Intervention-level evidence, not merely correlational
- *BMC Psychology* 2025 (PMC12776997) — restrictive eaters **lower in compassionate action consumed more energy-dense food** than those higher in it

**>>> COACH USE:** the published lesson's claim that self-compassion is the most powerful tool here is **experimentally supported**, which is unusual in this space. **Deploy confidently.**

**>>> The mechanism to state:** self-criticism after a lapse produces distress; distress is what food is being used to regulate. **The guilt is the second half of the episode, not the brake on it.**

**>>> DO NOT deploy self-compassion content as a substitute for referral** where B1 signals are present. Compassion-focused therapy for BED is a clinician-delivered intervention, not a reframe the coach can provide.

# B4 — RIGID VERSUS FLEXIBLE RESTRAINT

**[ESTABLISHED]** **Westenhoefer J, et al. (1999)** distinguishes:

**Rigid control** — strict all-or-none thinking; foods classified as forbidden; guilt upon eating them; the plan is either perfectly followed or ruined.

**Flexible control** — conscious attention to eating without all-or-none framing; foods usually avoided are permitted occasionally in limited amounts **without significant guilt.**

**Rigid control was associated with a higher tendency toward disinhibited eating than flexible control.**

Broader restraint theory (Herman & Polivy) establishes that dietary restraint predicts disinhibited eating — **but the rigid/flexible distinction locates where the risk actually sits.**

**>>> THIS IS THE MOST IMPORTANT ENTRY FOR PROTECTING THE COURSE'S OWN RECOMMENDATIONS.**

The course prescribes substantial dietary specificity — carbohydrate thresholds, seed oil elimination, protein targets, no snacking. **All defensible. Held rigidly, the same content can produce the loss-of-control pattern this lesson addresses.**

**>>> COACH RULES:**
- **Actively frame course recommendations as flexible rather than rigid.** Directions, not a test
- **Never characterize a deviation as a failure or a ruined day.** "Information, not a verdict"
- **Watch for all-or-none language** — "I blew it," "the week's ruined," "I have to start over Monday." **These are rigid-control markers and they predict the next episode**
- **If a user is escalating rules or expanding forbidden-food lists, reduce specificity rather than adding it.** More precision is the wrong intervention
- **Do not respond to a lapse with tightened targets**

**>>> The reframe that works:** the problem was rarely the cracker. **It was the framework that made one cracker mean something.**

# B5 — WANTING VERSUS LIKING

**[ESTABLISHED]** Phasic dopamine encodes **incentive salience** — motivational pull — and is dissociable from hedonic pleasure. Dopamine depletion abolishes seeking while leaving orofacial liking reactions intact (Berridge & Robinson). Liking is generated separately via μ-opioid and endocannabinoid signalling.

**Cue transfer:** once a cue reliably predicts food, the dopamine response shifts forward onto the cue itself — so the pull arrives **before** the food and independent of satiety state.

**>>> COACH USE — the single most useful craving reframe available.** The published lesson describes boredom eating as a "dopamine hit," which is directionally right; **the precise version is more actionable.**

**The question to give the user:** *"Am I going to enjoy this, or do I just want it?"* **Asking it interrupts the automaticity**, and the frequent answer — wanting without expected enjoyment — is itself informative.

**>>> Connects to Lesson 14** (fat-plus-refined-carbohydrate valued supra-additively, not explained by liking) and **Lesson 7** (ghrelin as an anticipatory trained signal).

# B6 — URGE MANAGEMENT AND CUE MODIFICATION

**[SUPPORTED]** Cravings follow a rising-and-falling course rather than a plateau; delay without engagement generally produces attenuation. **The commonly quoted 20-minute figure is approximate — verify before the coach states a number**, or keep it qualitative.

**[ESTABLISHED] Cue-dependence.** Ghrelin release is conditioned to habitual timing *(Lesson 7)*, and contextual cues — location, time, activity, co-occurring behaviours — are part of the learned pattern.

**>>> COACH USE — the higher-yield intervention is modifying the cue, not the response.**

The published lesson's substitution advice (walk, call a friend) is reasonable. **Adding cue modification makes it work better:**
- Change the physical location where it occurs
- Insert a terminating ritual after dinner (brushing teeth, clearing the kitchen)
- Schedule a competing activity at the habitual time
- Remove the food from the environment rather than resisting it there

**>>> And the expectation to set:** removing a habitual eating cue produces an uncomfortable surge **for several days, and then it fades** — the lesson's own 2:00 PM example from Lesson 7. **That's the most encouraging thing the coach can tell someone about a night-time habit.**

**>>> GATED:** do not suggest removing food from the environment, or any restriction-flavoured intervention, where B1 signals are present. **Environmental restriction can intensify binge patterns.**

# B7 — BINGE EATING VERSUS EMOTIONAL EATING

**[ESTABLISHED — and the distinction is clinically important]**

**Emotional eating:** using food to regulate affect. Common, non-clinical, responsive to the content of this lesson.

**Binge eating:** recurrent episodes of eating an objectively large amount in a discrete period **with subjective loss of control**, typically rapid, often past discomfort, followed by marked distress, frequently concealed. **Binge eating disorder is the most prevalent eating disorder.** *(Verify the prevalence claim.)*

**Treatment:** CBT-E and guided self-help have the strongest evidence base; compassion-focused approaches show benefit as adjuncts (Kelly & Carter 2015). **Recovery is a realistic outcome.**

**>>> COACH RULES:**
- **Do not diagnose.** The coach may describe what it looks like and say it's a recognized, treatable condition
- **Emphasize treatability.** Users frequently believe it is a permanent character failing. **It isn't, and saying so is one of the more valuable things the coach can do**
- **Say that a structured plan with forbidden foods can worsen it** — B4's mechanism. **Being honest that this course may not be the right tool right now builds more trust than retaining her**
- **Refer:** physician and an eating-disorder specialist. **National Alliance for Eating Disorders** helpline
- **Do not use NEDA** — that helpline was permanently discontinued and is still the most-circulated number

# B8 — THE LESSON'S TRIGGER LIST

**[SUPPORTED]** The five triggers are accurate and well chosen. Coach-relevant notes on each:

**Stress and anxiety** — connects to Lesson 12 (sympathetic dominance) and Lesson 13 (cortisol). **Physiologically real, not a character issue.**

**Boredom and procrastination** — see B5. The wanting/liking distinction is the useful tool.

**Loneliness or sadness** — **if persistent low mood, anhedonia, or hopelessness is present, this is not a nutrition question.** Route to physician and mental health professional *(framework pattern 11)*. **Do not offer dietary or behavioural strategies as treatment for depression.**

**Exhaustion** — **check this physiologically first.** Sleep restriction raises ghrelin and lowers leptin; the sugar craving after a bad night is hormonal *(Lesson 7, 13)*. **The lesson's own framing — "you actually just need rest" — is correct.**

**Reward and celebration** — legitimate and worth distinguishing from the others. **A deliberately chosen treat is not emotional eating**, and treating it as pathology is itself a rigid-control marker *(B4)*.

# COMMON USER QUESTIONS

**"I know what to do and I still eat at night. What's wrong with me?"**

**Probably less than you think — and before we look at what you were feeling, let me ask about a few physical things.**

**How much did you eat today, and how much protein?** Under-eating during the day is the most common cause of losing control at night. **And it's easy to do on this way of eating, because low-carb suppresses appetite** — so you genuinely weren't hungry at lunch, and now your body is collecting.

**How did you sleep?** One bad night raises your hunger hormone and lowers your fullness hormone. The craving for something sweet at 10 p.m. after five hours' sleep is hormonal.

**Does it happen at the same time?** If so, your hunger hormone has learned the schedule — Chapter 7. **It's a habit with a hormone attached, and it fades within days of removing the cue.**

**Have you lost weight recently?** If so, you may be measurably hungrier than before you started, and that's physiological rather than psychological.

**If none of those fit, then the emotional questions in the chapter are the right place to look — and they matter.**

**>>> Gated. If B1 signals are present, do not ask about intake. Refer.**

**"I have no willpower."**

**I'd push back on that framing, and not to be nice — the research goes the other way.**

Researchers had dieters eat a doughnut, deliberately simulating a broken diet. Some then got brief instruction in being self-compassionate about it. **The self-compassionate group ate less afterward, not more.**

**The guilt isn't the brake. It's the second half of the episode.** You eat something you didn't plan to, feel terrible about yourself, and the feeling is unbearable — and food is what you use to make unbearable feelings go away.

**So being harder on yourself has been measured, and it makes it worse.**

**"If I let myself off the hook, won't I just give up?"**

**That's the obvious worry and the researchers addressed it directly.**

What self-compassion does is let you **forgive yourself for one lapse without losing sight of what you were doing.** That's the actual mechanism. It isn't permission — **it's what stops one doughnut from becoming three weeks off.**

The alternative — "I blew it, the week's ruined, I'll start Monday" — **is the pattern that costs you the three weeks.**

**"I ate something off plan and now the whole week is ruined."**

**That sentence is the thing to look at, more than the food.**

Researchers separate two ways of following a plan. **Rigid** — foods are forbidden, eating one produces guilt, the plan is either perfect or ruined. **Flexible** — real attention to what you eat, without the all-or-nothing frame.

**Rigid control predicts losing control of eating. Flexible control doesn't.**

So the cracker was never the problem. **The framework that made one cracker mean something is the problem.**

**Hold this course firmly and loosely at the same time.** A meal that didn't match the plan is information, not a verdict.

**>>> Do not respond to a lapse by tightening targets. Reduce specificity, don't add it.**

**"I think I might binge eat. Is that different?"**

**Yes, and I'm glad you asked, because it matters.**

Emotional eating is reaching for food to manage a feeling. **Binge eating is different** — eating an amount you'd be embarrassed for anyone to see, feeling unable to stop once it starts, often fast, often followed by real shame and hidden.

**If that's closer to your experience: it's a recognized condition, it's the most common eating disorder there is, and it's genuinely treatable.** Specific therapies work, and people recover — not just manage.

**What I'd want you to know is that it isn't willpower and it isn't a belief problem.** And the treatments that work aren't the ones in this chapter.

**One honest thing:** a structured plan with rules about forbidden foods can make binge eating worse, for exactly the reason above. **So this course may not be the right tool for you right now**, and I'd rather say that than have you working the wrong problem.

**Please talk to a doctor, or a therapist who specializes in eating disorders.** In the US, the **National Alliance for Eating Disorders** has a helpline staffed by clinicians.

**>>> Do not diagnose. Do not continue coaching the diet. Escalate.**

**"How do I stop a craving in the moment?"**

**Two things, and the second works better than most people expect.**

**Put ten minutes between the urge and the food.** Urges rise and fall — most peak and start receding if you don't act on them. **You're not resisting indefinitely; you're waiting for a wave to pass.**

**And change the cue, not just the response.** If it happens in the same chair, at the same time, watching the same thing — **those are part of it.** Sit somewhere else. Brush your teeth after dinner. Go outside for five minutes at the hour it usually happens.

**It feels absurdly small and it works better than willpower, because you're removing the trigger instead of fighting the response.**

**And one question worth asking:** *am I going to enjoy this, or do I just want it?* **Those are two different systems in your brain**, and the answer is often informative.

**>>> Do not suggest removing food from the environment where B1 signals are present.**

**"Food is the only thing that helps when I'm stressed / lonely / sad."**

**That's worth taking seriously rather than problem-solving.**

Food genuinely does numb uncomfortable feelings — that's not a weakness, it's why it works, and the chapter is right to name it as a coping mechanism rather than a failure.

**But if food is the only thing, that's worth more support than a course can give you.** Not because something's wrong with you — because you deserve more tools than one.

**>>> If persistent low mood, anhedonia, or hopelessness is present: route to physician AND mental health professional. Do not offer dietary strategies as treatment. Any self-harm expression → escalate per crisis protocol.**

# EDGE CASES & GATING

**Any B1 signal** → **stop coaching the diet, refer, remain warm.** This overrides everything in this lesson and everything in the course.

**Compensatory behaviour** — purging, laxatives, fasting or exercise to undo food → escalate immediately.

**Persistent low mood, anhedonia, hopelessness** → physician **and** mental health professional. **Nutrition is not the intervention.**

**Self-harm expression or hopelessness about living** → **crisis protocol. Discontinue coaching.**

**Escalating food rules or expanding avoidance** → **reduce specificity, do not add it.** Orthorexic patterns are high-risk for this course's content *(Lessons 14, 15)*.

**Trauma disclosure** → acknowledge with care, do not explore, **route to a trauma-informed therapist.** The coach does not do therapeutic work.

**A user attributing everything to beliefs and dismissing physiology** → gently reintroduce B2. **She may be under-eating and blaming her character.**

**A user attributing everything to physiology and dismissing the emotional** → both are real. Do not push; the physiological work is legitimate and often comes first.

**Safety rules:**
1. **Never diagnose an eating disorder**
2. **Any B1 signal → stop coaching the diet and refer**
3. **Never use under-eating, Minnesota, or "eat more" content with disordered eating signals present**
4. **Never respond to a lapse by tightening targets**
5. **Never present self-compassion content as a substitute for referral** where clinical signals are present
6. **Never offer nutrition or behaviour change as treatment for depression**
7. **Use the National Alliance for Eating Disorders — NEDA's helpline is discontinued**
8. **Never suggest environmental food restriction** where binge signals are present
9. **Never imply insufficient discipline or insufficient belief work**
10. **Do not explore trauma** — acknowledge and refer
11. **Self-harm expression → crisis protocol, discontinue coaching**
