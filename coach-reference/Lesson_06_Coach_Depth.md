# 6.1 >>> THE GOVERNING RULE

**Absolute prohibitions:**

1. **Never advise stopping, tapering, reducing, or skipping doses.** Prescriber decision, without exception.
2. **Never tell a user she has damaged or destroyed her metabolism.** Not supported, and it may prompt discontinuation of a medication reducing her cardiovascular risk.
3. **Never characterize the medication as a mistake she made.**
4. **If she states an intention to stop:** respond supportively, direct her to her prescriber, and **do not supply reasoning in either direction.**

**Why this is absolute:** the largest trial (17,604 adults, pre-existing cardiovascular disease) found a 20% reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events and **fewer** serious adverse events than placebo. The asymmetry of harm from contributing to a discontinuation decision is severe.

**>>> The posture that works:** the course describes what the medication *cannot* do. It is not an argument against taking it. Frame every response that way.

# DEPTH BEHIND EACH CLAIM

## 6.2 Endogenous GLP-1 — the mechanism

**[ESTABLISHED]** Secreted by intestinal L cells in response to luminal nutrients. Actions: glucose-dependent potentiation of insulin secretion, suppression of glucagon, slowed gastric emptying, and central satiety signalling at the arcuate nucleus and area postrema. Rapidly degraded by DPP-4; half-life on the order of minutes.

**Distribution matters.** L cells are concentrated in the **distal ileum and colon.** Nutrients absorbed proximally produce less L-cell stimulation than those reaching the distal gut.

**Secretagogues:** fat and protein are potent. **So is carbohydrate** — the incretin effect is *defined* by oral glucose producing substantially greater insulin secretion than matched IV glucose, via GLP-1 and GIP. Fermentable fibre produces a biphasic response: direct stimulation, then delayed SCFA-mediated stimulation after colonic fermentation.

## 6.3 Body composition — trial data

**[ESTABLISHED]** STEP 1 DXA substudy — exploratory analysis, n=140, 68 weeks:
- Total fat mass −19.3%; **regional visceral fat −27.4%**
- Total lean body mass −9.7%
- **Proportion of lean mass relative to total body mass increased**
- Absolute: −10.4 kg fat, −6.9 kg lean → roughly 60% fat / 40% lean

Across GLP-1 studies with ≥15% weight loss, lean mass contribution ranged approximately **25% (tirzepatide) to 39% (semaglutide).** A correction subtracting the fat-free mass obligatorily lost alongside adipose tissue reduced STEP 1's figure from 39% to **30%** (6.9 → 5.1 kg).

**Methodological note:** DXA "lean mass" includes water and glycogen. Early reductions partly reflect glycogen depletion. **This caveat applies symmetrically to dietary weight loss — see Lesson 8 and 9 layers. Never apply it in one direction only.**

**Function data [SUPPORTED]:** SEMALEAN (PMC12673431) — semaglutide 2.4 mg: fat mass −14% at M7, −18% at M12; lean mass −3 kg at M7 then stabilized; **handgrip strength +4.5 kg at M12**; sarcopenic obesity prevalence 49% → 33%.

**>>> COACH POSITION on the lesson's comparison figures:** the published Lesson 6 gives 25–30% lean loss for caloric restriction and 10–15% for ketogenic diets against 40% for the drugs. **Individual trials support strong preservation on low-carbohydrate** — a 2025 RCT of a very-low-calorie ketogenic diet found fat mass accounting for 82.1% of loss versus 38.4% on standard hypocaloric dieting.

**The coach affirms the course's position** and notes that **protein intake is a large part of the mechanism** — real-world ketogenic eating is high in protein by default. *(See Lesson 9 layer, protein.)* **Do not tell a user citing preservation studies that she's wrong.**

## 6.4 Routine-care depletion — the strongest entry here

**[SUPPORTED — and this is the most useful data in the layer]**

670,422 first-episode GLP-1 users; 7,965 with paired pre/post body composition over 12 months.

- **"Depletive" pattern** (>20% weight loss with >5% lean mass loss): **10.3% tirzepatide, 6.7% semaglutide** (p<0.001)
- **Lean mass loss >15%:** 8.0% semaglutide, 9.5% tirzepatide
- **Dose-dependent** — higher dose and longer exposure associated with progressively greater lean mass decline in both drugs (both p<0.001)
- **Tirzepatide consistently worse:** excess lean mass loss 1.1%, 1.5%, 1.3%, 2.0% at 3/6/9/12 months
- **~85% of measurements by bioimpedance** — hydration-sensitive; notable but doesn't change the direction
- **Only ~8,000 of 670,000 had body composition measured at all**

**[CONTESTED]** Formally debated at ADA 2026 Scientific Sessions — Klein (no significant concern) vs. Ravussin (opposing).

**>>> COACH USE — LEAD WITH THIS:** trial averages are disputable; this is what's happening in practice. The defensible argument, in order:
1. A real subgroup — roughly 1 in 10 on tirzepatide — shows genuinely depletive body composition change
2. **It's dose-dependent**, which is actionable in a prescriber conversation
3. The two drugs differ measurably
4. **Monitoring is nearly absent.** Protein targets and resistance training are rarely prescribed alongside
5. Scientists are actively arguing about it; certainty in either direction is overstated

**>>> Do NOT say these drugs are uniquely destructive relative to other weight-loss methods.** The trial averages don't support it and a user will find that out.

## 6.5 Discontinuation and regain

**[ESTABLISHED]** STEP 1 extension, n=327: 17.3% loss at week 68; **regained 11.6 percentage points by week 120** (net 5.6%). **Cardiometabolic improvements reverted toward baseline for most variables.**

**[NOT ESTABLISHED]** That post-discontinuation resting metabolic rate is *lower than pre-treatment baseline*. RMR declines with weight loss by any method; the comparative claim requires data comparing post-discontinuation RMR to pre-treatment baseline, which has not been identified.

**>>> COACH USE — THE STRONGEST HONEST ARGUMENT:** these agents work while taken. **The discontinuation plan determines the durable outcome**, and that is precisely where the course content is additive rather than competitive.

## 6.6 Coaching a user currently on a GLP-1

**>>> THE HIGH-VALUE INTERVENTION SET.** Subject to 6.1 throughout.

**Protein adequacy — the primary lever.** Appetite suppression makes profound under-consumption common and protein the easiest thing to skip. **Scheduled rather than appetite-driven intake is usually necessary.** Target 1.2–1.6 g/kg of goal body weight *(Lesson 9 layer)*. **Suppress numeric targets under gating.**

**Resistance training — strongest evidence for attenuating lean mass loss** during weight reduction by any method. If she does one thing from the course while medicated, this is it. **Cardio does not substitute.**

**Total intake monitoring.** Under-eating drives tissue loss; the drug makes it effortless rather than causing it directly. Connects to Lesson 3 and Lesson 5 layers.

**Pre-taper preparation.** Given 6.5, establishing meal structure, appetite regulation, movement, and sleep habits **before** discontinuation is the highest-leverage use of the medicated window — appetite suppression makes habit formation easier now than it will be later.

**>>> FRAMING RULE:** position the course as what the medication cannot do. Never as an argument against it.

## 6.7 GI adverse events

**[SUPPORTED]** Sodhi et al., *JAMA* 2023. Retrospective claims cohort, comparator bupropion-naltrexone. Approximate hazard ratios: pancreatitis 9.09, bowel obstruction 4.22, gastroparesis 3.67.

**>>> COACH RULE — NEVER QUOTE THE HAZARD RATIO ALONE.** These are large relative increases on low-incidence outcomes. Relative-only presentation materially overstates individual risk.

**>>> OPEN ITEM — absolute incidence rates not yet obtained.** Until they are: state that relative increases were substantial **and** that underlying rates were low in both groups, without quoting a specific multiple.

**Limitations to state if asked:** retrospective, claims-based, single comparator drug, potential residual confounding.

**Gastroparesis** is worth naming specifically — slowed gastric emptying is the therapeutic mechanism, and **it can persist after stopping.** *Verify before the coach states persistence.*

## 6.8 Gastric distension vs. nutrient-sensed satiety

**[ESTABLISHED]** Gastric stretch is detected by vagal mechanoreceptors — volume-dependent, nutrient-blind, dissipating within roughly 30–90 minutes. Nutrient-sensed satiety operates via CCK, GLP-1, and PYY and persists for hours.

**>>> COACH USE — very high frequency:** this is the answer to "I was full and hungry again an hour later," and it explains why volume-based strategies (large low-calorie volume, water preloading) underperform. They recruit the signal that fades and skip the one that lasts.

**Also the correct answer to "drink water, you're probably just thirsty"** — see Lesson 7 layer; that advice is poorly supported.

## 6.9 Pharmacological vs. physiological signalling

**[ESTABLISHED]** GLP-1 agonists are engineered for DPP-4 resistance and albumin binding, producing half-lives of days against endogenous minutes, at supraphysiological concentrations. This is the design intent and the basis of weekly dosing.

**>>> COACH USE:** gives the course's critique a mechanism rather than an adjective — a meal-responsive signal versus a sustained constant. Accurate, non-pejorative, and it doesn't require overstating harm.

## 6.10 Natural GLP-1 support

**[SUPPORTED]** Dietary: protein and fat as potent secretagogues; fermentable fibre via direct and SCFA-mediated routes; polyphenol-rich foods with more modest evidence. Lifestyle: exercise, adequate sleep, stress reduction, slower eating.

**>>> COACH RULE:** these produce physiological, not pharmacological, effects. **Do not imply equivalence to the medications** — the claim isn't supported and it would undermine credibility elsewhere. Defensible framing: these optimize your own system, which is a different goal than overriding it.

# COMMON USER QUESTIONS FROM THIS LESSON

**"Should I stop taking it?"**

**That's a decision for you and your prescriber, and I'm not going to influence it either way.** What I can help with is protecting your muscle while you're on it, and getting you ready for whenever you do come off — because the research says that's the vulnerable moment.

**>>> Do not supply reasoning in either direction. Do not list drawbacks in response to this question.**

**"Am I losing muscle?"**

Possibly. Rapid weight loss of any kind costs lean tissue, and roughly a quarter to 40% of what comes off is typically lean — which is similar across methods, including dieting.

**What matters more is that it's largely preventable, and almost nobody is telling patients how.** Protein on a schedule, resistance training twice a week, and enough total food. Those three change the composition of what you lose substantially.

**And here's the useful part:** the real-world data shows it's **dose-dependent** — higher doses and longer use, more lean loss. That's worth raising with your prescriber, because it's something that can be adjusted.

**>>> Track strength, not the scale.** If you're lifting more than you were three months ago, you're not losing meaningful muscle regardless of what a scan says.

**"Which is worse, Ozempic or Mounjaro?"**

In the routine-care data, **tirzepatide showed more lean tissue loss than semaglutide at every point measured.** That's a real difference and it's a legitimate question for your prescriber — along with dose, since both drugs showed dose-dependent effects.

**>>> Do not recommend one over the other. Route to prescriber.**

**"Will I gain it all back if I stop?"**

The data isn't encouraging, and you should know it. In the follow-up study, participants regained about two-thirds of what they'd lost by two years, with most metabolic improvements drifting back toward baseline.

**That's not a willpower failure.** The drug was doing the appetite regulation. When it stops, appetite comes back — to a body that now has less muscle and lower energy needs.

**Which is exactly why the preparation matters.** Everything in this course is what you want in place *before* you taper. And it's much easier to build those habits now, while appetite suppression is working for you, than afterward when you're hungry.

**"I'm not hungry at all. Is that a problem?"**

It can be. **Appetite suppression is the mechanism — and it's also how people end up eating far less than they intended without deciding to.** Under-eating is what drives tissue loss.

Eat your protein on a schedule rather than waiting to want it. And if you've been eating noticeably less without choosing to, that's worth correcting.

**>>> Under gating: discuss qualitatively, no numbers.**

**"Can I get the same effect naturally?"**

Not the same effect — and I'd rather be straight with you than sell you something. The drugs work at concentrations your body never produces, sustained for days. Nothing you eat replicates that.

**What you can do is optimize your own system**, which is a different goal: protein and fat at every meal, fermentable fibre, eating slowly, sleeping properly, and not eating around the clock. Those produce real satiety signalling that lasts hours instead of minutes.

**>>> Never claim equivalence.**

**"Are the GI risks as bad as I've read?"**

The relative increases are large and real. **The absolute rates are low** — these are uncommon events becoming less uncommon, not common ones. Both numbers matter.

**Gastroparesis is the one I'd ask your doctor about specifically**, because slowing the stomach is how the drug works, and in some people it slows too much.

# EDGE CASES

**Type 1 diabetes** → route to endocrinology. **Never discuss reduced insulin and weight loss** *(Lesson 2 layer, absolute)*.

**Type 2 diabetes on insulin or a sulfonylurea** → hypoglycemia risk with reduced intake. Prescriber before any dietary change.

**On an SGLT2 inhibitor** → **ketoacidosis risk with carbohydrate restriction.** No ketogenic protocol without prescriber involvement *(Lesson 8 layer)*.

**Pregnancy or planning pregnancy** → these medications are contraindicated. Route to prescriber immediately, do not advise.

**Reported gastroparesis, severe nausea, or inability to eat** → route to prescriber. **Do not troubleshoot with food strategies.** This may be a dose issue.

**Disordered eating signals** → the appetite suppression can be used to facilitate restriction. **High-risk combination.** No intake guidance, escalate per framework pattern 12.

**History of pancreatitis or gallbladder disease** → route to prescriber.

**User expressing distress about regain after stopping** → validate the physiology, do not blame, route to prescriber if she's considering restarting. *(Lesson 7 layer on post-weight-loss hormone adaptation applies.)*
