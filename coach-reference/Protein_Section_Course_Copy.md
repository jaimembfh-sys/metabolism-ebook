# PART 1 — HOW MUCH PROTEIN YOU ACTUALLY NEED

## Course copy

> ## How Much Protein? Let's Be Specific
>
> I've told you protein is the foundation of every meal. That's useless without a number, so here it is.
>
> Almost every guideline you've seen is built on the RDA of 0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight. **That number was never meant to be a target.** It's the minimum required to stop your body breaking down — the amount that keeps you from losing nitrogen, not the amount that keeps you strong.
>
> For what you're actually trying to do — hold onto muscle while losing fat, through a decade when your body is getting worse at using protein — the research points somewhere considerably higher.
>
> ### The number
>
> **1.2 to 1.6 grams per kilogram per day.** For most women, toward the upper end of that.
>
> Here's where it comes from. A large analysis of weight-loss studies found that protein intake **above 1.05 g/kg** preserved meaningfully more lean tissue than intake below it — and the advantage grew the longer people stayed on the diet. Critically, protein had **no effect on how much fat they lost.** It didn't help them lose more. It changed *what* they lost.
>
> That's the whole point. Protein isn't a fat-loss tool. It's a muscle-protection tool. And it works whether you're eating low-carb, low-calorie, or anything else.
>
> ### Calculate it from your goal weight, not your current weight
>
> This matters and almost nobody gets it right.
>
> If you're carrying 60 extra pounds, your protein need is set by the **metabolically active tissue** you have and want to keep — not by the fat you're carrying. Fat tissue barely needs protein. So using your current weight will give you a number that's too high.
>
> **Use the weight you're aiming for.** Here's a starting point by height:
>
> | Your height | Goal weight | Protein target |
> |---|---|---|
> | 5'0" | ~118 lb (53 kg) | **64–85 g/day** |
> | 5'2" | ~126 lb (57 kg) | **68–91 g/day** |
> | 5'4" | ~134 lb (61 kg) | **73–98 g/day** |
> | 5'6" | ~142 lb (65 kg) | **78–104 g/day** |
> | 5'8" | ~151 lb (69 kg) | **83–110 g/day** |
> | 5'10" | ~160 lb (73 kg) | **88–117 g/day** |
>
> If you're already at a healthy weight and carry decent muscle, use your actual weight. If you're an athlete or lifting seriously, go higher.
>
> ### What that looks like on a plate
>
> Numbers are abstract until you see the food:
>
> - 6 oz cooked beef or steak — **45 g**
> - 6 oz chicken breast — **50 g**
> - 3 large eggs — **19 g**
> - 6 oz salmon — **38 g**
> - 1 cup cottage cheese — **25 g**
> - 4 oz ground beef — **28 g**
>
> **So a 5'4" woman targeting 90 grams needs roughly three eggs at breakfast, six ounces of chicken at lunch, and six ounces of beef at dinner.** That's it. But look at how much food that is — because most women eating "a healthy amount of protein" are getting about half of it.
>
> ### Per meal matters, not just per day
>
> Here's the part that surprises people. Muscle building isn't triggered by protein trickling in across the day. It needs a **large enough dose in one sitting** to flip the switch.
>
> Spread 60 grams thinly across three meals and you may miss the threshold at all three. Concentrate 90 grams into three real servings and you hit it three times.
>
> **Rough targets per meal: 25–30 grams if you're younger, 35–40 grams if you're over 50.**
>
> ### Why the number goes *up* with age
>
> This is the most important thing in this section and it's the least known.
>
> As you get older, your muscles become **less responsive to the same amount of protein.** Researchers call it anabolic resistance. A portion that built muscle at 30 may not be enough to maintain it at 60.
>
> **Your protein requirement rises at exactly the age most women start eating less of it.** Appetite drops. Cooking for fewer people. Meat feels heavy. And the requirement is going the other direction.
>
> If you take one number from this entire course, take this one.
>
> ### A note if you have kidney disease
>
> Higher protein intake has not been shown to harm healthy kidneys — that concern comes from studies of people who already had kidney disease.
>
> **But if you do have reduced kidney function, protein intake is a genuine medical question** and needs to be set with your doctor rather than from a course. Please don't raise your protein substantially without that conversation.

# PART 2 — CAN YOU EAT TOO MUCH?

## Course copy

> ## Yes — But It Takes More Than You'd Think
>
> I've just told you to eat more protein than you probably are. So let me be equally clear about the other end, because I'm watching a lot of people overshoot it badly right now.
>
> ### There is a real physiological ceiling
>
> Your liver converts the nitrogen from protein into urea so your kidneys can clear it. **That process has a maximum rate.** Exceed it and you get nausea, weakness, and genuine illness.
>
> Historically this was called "rabbit starvation" — early explorers who ate nothing but very lean game got sick and sometimes died, not from lack of food but from too much protein with no fat to go with it.
>
> That ceiling sits somewhere around **35 to 40 percent of your total calories.** You have to work hard to reach it, and almost nobody eating normally does.
>
> ### Where the benefit stops
>
> More useful than the ceiling is knowing where the return disappears.
>
> **Above roughly 2 grams per kilogram, additional protein stops doing much** for most people who aren't competitive athletes. You're not getting more muscle protection. You're just eating more protein.
>
> For a 5'4" woman that's about 120 grams. **Above that, you're past the point of benefit.**
>
> ### The carnivore problem
>
> I'm bringing this up specifically because all-meat eating has become popular and I'm seeing a consistent pattern.
>
> A lot of people eating carnivore are at **250 to 350 grams of protein a day.** For a 150-pound woman that's around 4 grams per kilogram — **triple** the target above, and well past where any benefit has been shown.
>
> Three things happen:
>
> **Your blood sugar can go up, and it confuses people.** Protein stimulates a hormone called glucagon, which tells your liver to release glucose. On a diet with essentially no carbohydrate, glucagon runs high and insulin runs low — so your liver produces more glucose than it otherwise would. **Fasting glucose of 100 to 110 is common in long-term carnivore eaters**, and it genuinely alarms them.
>
> **Here's what I want you to understand about that.** It's usually **adaptive, not diabetic.** Your muscles deliberately turn down glucose uptake to save it for your brain. Fasting glucose rises while fasting insulin stays low and your HbA1c stays excellent. It looks like early diabetes on one reading and it isn't.
>
> It also means a **glucose tolerance test after months of carnivore or strict keto will look terrible** — and that reverses within days of eating carbohydrate again. People get badly frightened, and some get misdiagnosed.
>
> The way to read this is **fasting insulin, HbA1c, and your triglyceride-to-HDL ratio together** — never a fasting glucose alone. Low insulin and a good ratio alongside a glucose of 105 is an entirely different situation.
>
> **Stress stacks on top.** Cortisol also tells your liver to release glucose. Someone eating enormous protein, under-sleeping, and training hard has three inputs on the same pathway. If your glucose is creeping up, look at your sleep and your stress before you look at your meat.
>
> **And protein crowds out fat.** This is the practical one. Protein is filling — that's why it works. But if protein takes over your plate on a low-carb diet, you can end up short on total energy without ever deciding to eat less. Which is the failure mode from Chapter 8: not hungry, not eating enough, thyroid dropping, progress stalling.
>
> ### So what does "moderate" actually mean?
>
> You'll hear keto described as a "moderate protein" diet, and that phrase does real damage — because most people hear it as *not much.*
>
> **Moderate means 1.2 to 1.6 grams per kilogram of your goal weight.** For most women that's 80 to 110 grams a day, which is considerably more than they're eating now.
>
> It does *not* mean 300 grams. And it does not mean keeping protein low to protect your ketone levels — very high protein does nudge ketones down slightly, but **protecting your muscle matters far more than protecting a number on a test strip.**
>
> Hit the target. Don't triple it. Get the rest of your calories from quality fat.

# PART 3 — WHY MUSCLE LOSS IS THE REAL COST

**Placement: Lesson 6 (replacing the current muscle-loss section) and referenced from Lesson 13.**

## Course copy

> ## The Thing Nobody Is Measuring
>
> When you lose weight quickly, you don't only lose fat. And the part you lose alongside it matters more than almost anything else in this course.
>
> ### Why muscle is the wrong thing to lose
>
> Muscle is where your blood sugar goes. When insulin does its job, **skeletal muscle is the primary destination** — it's the biggest container you have for glucose.
>
> So losing muscle doesn't just cost you strength. It shrinks the container. The same meal now needs more insulin to handle, because there's less room to put it. **You come out of the weight loss more insulin resistant than you went in.**
>
> It also lowers your energy requirement — partly because muscle costs energy to maintain, and partly because less muscle means less movement, less capacity, and a body that's decided to spend less across the board.
>
> Which is exactly the setup for regain: **lower energy needs, less glucose disposal capacity, and the appetite hormones from Chapter 7 still pushing you toward food.**
>
> ### What the numbers actually say
>
> On the weight-loss medications, across studies where people lost at least 15% of their body weight, **lean tissue accounted for roughly 25 to 39 percent** of the total. On conventional calorie restriction it's broadly similar — often quoted around 25 to 30 percent.
>
> **I want to be straight with you about two things.**
>
> First, "lean mass" on a body scan is **not the same as muscle.** It includes water, glycogen, bone, and organs. When you cut carbohydrate you lose your glycogen stores plus well over a kilogram of the water bound to them — and that shows up as "lean mass loss" with no muscle involved at all. **This applies to the medication numbers too**, and a correction accounting for the tissue obligatorily lost alongside fat brought one of the headline figures from 39% down to 30%.
>
> Second, in the clinical trials, physical function generally **improved.** One study found grip strength up substantially over a year even as lean mass fell.
>
> So no — I'm not going to tell you these drugs are uniquely destroying everyone's muscle. The trial averages don't support that, and you'd find out.
>
> ### Here's what genuinely concerns me
>
> **A real subgroup is being depleted, and nobody is checking.**
>
> A study of routine clinical practice — not a trial, actual patients — looked at over 670,000 people on these medications. Among those with before-and-after body composition measured, roughly **one in ten on tirzepatide** and **one in fifteen on semaglutide** showed a genuinely depletive pattern: heavy weight loss with substantial lean mass loss. Lean mass loss over 15 percent occurred in about 8 to 9 percent of patients.
>
> **It's dose-dependent.** Higher doses and longer use produced progressively more lean mass decline in both drugs. That's information you can act on.
>
> **And the two drugs differ.** Tirzepatide showed consistently more lean mass loss than semaglutide at every time point measured.
>
> Now the part that should bother you most: of those 670,000 patients, **only about 8,000 had their body composition measured at all.** These medications are being prescribed at enormous scale with essentially no monitoring of what tissue is being lost — and usually no protein target and no strength training attached.
>
> **There's an open scientific argument about this.** Researchers debated it formally at a major diabetes conference in 2026, with serious people on both sides. Anyone telling you it's settled — in either direction — isn't being straight with you.
>
> ### The same applies to plain old dieting
>
> This isn't a drug problem. It's a **rapid-weight-loss-without-protein-and-lifting** problem.
>
> A woman eating 1,200 calories a day with 50 grams of protein and doing nothing but walking is losing muscle. She'll finish lighter, weaker, with a smaller glucose container and a lower energy requirement — and she'll regain, and she'll blame herself.
>
> That's been happening for forty years. The medications didn't invent it. They just made it faster.
>
> ### What to actually do — the same three things regardless of method
>
> **Hit your protein target.** The number from earlier in this course. On appetite-suppressing medication this requires eating on a schedule rather than waiting to be hungry, because you won't be.
>
> **Lift something heavy, twice a week minimum.** This is the intervention with the strongest evidence for protecting lean tissue during weight loss, by any method. Cardio does not do this job.
>
> **Track strength, not just the scale.** If your grip is stronger and you're lifting more than you were three months ago, you're not losing muscle — whatever the scan says. **Function is the honest measure.** Get a grip dynamometer if you like data; they cost very little.
>
> Do those three and the composition of your weight loss changes substantially. Skip them and it doesn't much matter how you lost it.

# SOURCES

**Verified:**

1. **Krieger JW, Sitren HS, Daniels MJ, Langkamp-Henken B.** Effects of variation in protein and carbohydrate intake on body mass and composition during energy restriction: a meta-regression. *Am J Clin Nutr*. 2006;83(2):260–274. **PMID: 16469983.** doi:10.1093/ajcn/83.2.260
   - Protein >1.05 g/kg → **+0.60 kg fat-free mass retention** vs ≤1.05 g/kg; **+1.21 kg** in studies >12 weeks
   - **No significant effect of protein on body mass or fat mass loss**
   - *Quality note: DARE flagged limitations — search scope, no study-quality weighting, exploratory meta-regression. Direction solid, exact figures approximate.*

2. **Wycherley TP, Moran LJ, Clifton PM, Noakes M, Brinkworth GD.** Effects of energy-restricted high-protein, low-fat compared with standard-protein, low-fat diets: a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials. *Am J Clin Nutr*. 2012;96:1281–1298. 24 trials, 1,063 participants, isocaloric and fat-matched. High protein preserved fat-free mass better.

3. **GLP-1 lean mass range** — across GLP-1 studies with ≥15% weight loss, lean mass contribution ranged ~25% (tirzepatide) to ~39% (semaglutide). Correction subtracting obligatory fat-free mass reduced STEP 1's 39% to **30%** (6.9 → 5.1 kg). Presented at ADA 2026 Scientific Sessions debate (Klein vs. Ravussin).

4. **SEMALEAN study** (PMC12673431) — semaglutide 2.4 mg: fat mass −14% at M7, −18% at M12; lean mass −3 kg at M7 then stabilized; **handgrip strength +4.5 kg at M12**; sarcopenic obesity prevalence 49% → 33%.

5. **Routine-care body composition study** — 670,422 first-episode GLP-1 patients, 7,965 with paired measurements over 12 months. "Depletive" pattern (>20% weight loss with >5% lean mass loss): **10.3% tirzepatide vs 6.7% semaglutide** (p<0.001). Lean mass loss >15%: 8.0% semaglutide, 9.5% tirzepatide. Dose and duration both associated with progressively greater decline (p<0.001). Tirzepatide excess lean mass loss: 1.1%, 1.5%, 1.3%, 2.0% at 3/6/9/12 months. *~85% of measurements by BIA — hydration-sensitive.*

6. **Langer HT, et al.** Weight loss with GLP-1 medicines does not result in a disproportionate loss of muscle mass or function in obese mice and humans. *Cell Rep Med*. 2026;7:102665.

Add to `AI_Coach_Knowledge_Corpus.md`, superseding 9.8:

**>>> PROTEIN TARGETS — COACH RULES**
- Target **1.2–1.6 g/kg/day of goal body weight**, upper end for users over 50. Floor is 1.05 g/kg (Krieger).
- **Always compute from goal or ideal body weight, not current weight**, in users with obesity — actual weight overestimates need.
- Per meal: ~25–30 g under 50, ~35–40 g over 50.
- Benefit plateaus around 2 g/kg for non-athletes.
- **Protein affects lean mass retention, not fat loss.** Do not present it as a fat-loss lever.
- **>>> Any reported kidney disease or reduced eGFR → route to clinician before any protein recommendation.** Do not provide a target.
- **>>> Personal health situation flag active, or any disordered eating signal → provide no numeric protein targets, no gram counts, no goal-weight table.** Discuss protein qualitatively as "a substantial portion of each meal" and nothing more. Corpus 6.4 and 11.3 apply in full.

**>>> ADAPTIVE GLUCOSE ELEVATION ON VERY LOW CARBOHYDRATE**
- Very high protein plus near-zero carbohydrate raises glucagon and hepatic glucose output. Fasting glucose 100–110 is common in long-term carnivore/strict keto eaters.
- Usually adaptive (physiological glucose sparing), not diabetic: fasting insulin low, HbA1c normal.
- OGTT after prolonged carbohydrate restriction can look markedly abnormal and normalizes within days of carbohydrate reintroduction.
- **>>> DO NOT reassure a user that an elevated glucose is "just adaptive" without fasting insulin and HbA1c.** If insulin is elevated, or HbA1c is rising, or the user has any diabetes diagnosis or family history, route to a physician. The reassurance is correct in the right context and dangerous in the wrong one.

**>>> Update prohibited claims:** replace #62 (ketones preserve muscle better than other diets) with: *that ketosis itself, independent of protein intake, preserves lean mass better than other diets at equivalent weight loss.* Krieger's carbohydrate coefficient points the other direction (+0.69 kg FFM loss, +1.74 kg beyond 12 weeks), while individual high-protein ketogenic trials show strong preservation. **The coach attributes lean-mass protection to protein and resistance training, and notes that real-world ketogenic eating is typically high in protein by default — which likely explains much of the observed preservation.**
