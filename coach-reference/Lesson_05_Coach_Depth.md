# WHAT THE LESSON TEACHES

1. The thyroid sets metabolic pace — T4 is produced by the gland, but must be converted to active T3
2. **Conversion happens in the liver and gut, and is impaired by stress, inflammation, and nutrient deficiencies** — so thyroid symptoms can occur with a normally functioning gland
3. Standard thyroid tests can appear normal while this is happening
4. The nervous system acts as a safety switch — sympathetic dominance prioritizes immediate survival; parasympathetic supports digestion, repair, and energy use
5. Energy demand and heat production are driven by muscle and by movement that isn't exercise
6. Brown fat generates heat directly via UCP1; cold exposure is the most potent natural activator
7. **Cold exposure needs dosing carefully, particularly in women with a burdened nervous system** — the lesson's "A Note for Women" is well calibrated and should be applied as written
8. **(New)** What to test, Hashimoto's as an immune condition, the low-carb T3 effect, the under-eating trap, NEAT named

# DEPTH BEHIND EACH CLAIM

## 5.1 Peripheral conversion — the lesson's core claim

**[ESTABLISHED]** The thyroid secretes predominantly T4, which is largely inactive at target tissues. Conversion to T3 occurs peripherally via deiodinase enzymes — **principally in the liver**, with contributions from kidney, gut, and other tissues.

**Cofactors required:** the deiodinases are **selenoproteins**. Iron is required for thyroid peroxidase (heme-dependent) upstream. Zinc and iodine are also required. *(See Lesson 4 layer for the machinery detail.)*

**Impairment pathways:**
- **Inflammation** — IL-6 and TNF-α suppress D1 and D2 while inducing D3, shifting conversion toward inactivation. IL-6 appears to act partly by depleting a thiol cofactor, probably glutathione; N-acetylcysteine prevented the inhibition in vitro *(J Clin Invest 2011;121(5):1834–1841, PMID 21540553)*
- **Cortisol** — glucocorticoids suppress TRH expression centrally
- **Hepatic dysfunction** — the primary conversion site
- **Carbohydrate restriction** — see 5.3
- **Low energy availability** — see 5.4

**>>> KEY CONVERGENCE:** glutathione synthesis is largely hepatic. Liver function and inflammation are not independent problems here — they meet at the same cofactor. This unifies the Lesson 11 material with this lesson.

## 5.2 What a full panel covers

**[ESTABLISHED]**
- **TSH** — pituitary signal to the gland. Detects primary gland failure. Says nothing about peripheral conversion.
- **Free T4** — predominant circulating hormone, largely inactive at tissues
- **Free T3** — the active hormone. Produced mainly by peripheral deiodination
- **TPO and thyroglobulin antibodies** — identify autoimmune thyroiditis
- **Reverse T3** — see 5.5

The pattern the course describes — **normal TSH, normal free T4, low free T3** — is invisible on a TSH-only panel. This is the single most common reason a symptomatic user has been told her thyroid is fine.

**>>> COACH RULE:**
- The coach may explain what each test measures and suggest **free T3** as the highest-value addition
- The coach **must not** interpret a user's results, diagnose, or state that her physician was wrong
- **Framing that works:** "TSH answers whether your gland is being asked to work harder. You're asking whether the hormone is being activated — that's a different question and a different test."
- Never characterize a TSH-only workup as bad medicine. Within conventional practice it is a defensible screen for gland failure
- **Route interpretation to a practitioner.** See referral framework pattern 1.

**>>> If the user is already on thyroid medication and still symptomatic:** that is a dose and formulation conversation with her prescriber. **Never suggest adjusting it.** The coach may note that some people do better on formulations containing T3 and that it's worth raising — nothing more specific.

## 5.3 Carbohydrate restriction and T3

**[ESTABLISHED]** **Spaulding SW, Chopra IJ, Sherwin RS, Lyall SS.** *J Clin Endocrinol Metab*. 1976;42(1):197–200.

- Total fasting (7–18 days): T3 ↓53%, reciprocal rT3 ↑58%
- No-carbohydrate hypocaloric diet (800 kcal, 2 weeks): T3 ↓47%, **no significant rT3 change**
- Isocaloric diets containing **≥50 g carbohydrate**: no significant change in either
- T3 decline correlated significantly with blood glucose and ketones; **no correlation with insulin or glucagon**

**The effect is carbohydrate-specific, not calorie-driven.**

**[SUPPORTED — the adaptive interpretation]** Three lines support reading this as downregulation rather than damage:
1. **TSH remains normal** — central feedback does not register a deficit *(Nutrients review, PMC12468144)*
2. **rT3 behaves differently than in starvation or illness** — carbohydrate restriction alone dropped T3 without significantly raising rT3, while fasting raised both
3. **Free T4 often normal or slightly elevated** — a conversion story, not a gland-output story

**[CONTESTED] — the limits:**
- **Mechanism unresolved.** The *Nutrients* review attributes it to reduced insulin suppressing deiodinase activity; Spaulding found no correlation with insulin. **The coach must not assert a mechanism.**
- **"Adaptive" is an interpretation.** The same pattern in chronic illness is not benign. What distinguishes them is symptoms, not the value.
- **Contradicting rT3 data:** a 1980 *Metabolism* study found an all-fat isocaloric diet dropped T3 ~50% and raised rT3 ~123% — equal to total starvation. **Obtain full text.**
- **Population caveat:** women appear more vulnerable to HPT and HPG axis suppression under carbohydrate restriction, particularly with concurrent energy deficit.

**>>> THE OPERATIVE RULE — apply this first in any low-carb thyroid conversation:**

**Symptoms decide.** Low T3 with normal TSH in a user who feels well is very likely adaptation — don't chase the number. Low T3 in a user who feels worse needs investigation.

**>>> THE TIMELINE IS DIAGNOSTIC:**
- Days 2–14, resolving = keto-adaptation *(see Lesson 8 layer)*
- **Onset at or persistence past week 4–6 = investigate.** Never advise pushing through past week six.

**>>> Delayed-presentation signals to ask about actively** rather than waiting for the user to volunteer:
- **Hair shedding — lags the trigger by roughly three months.** Ask what she was doing three months ago, not what she's doing now.
- Cycle length or regularity changes — commonly attributed to stress or age
- Cold intolerance
- Non-improving fatigue

## 5.4 Low energy availability — the most common failure mode

**[SUPPORTED]** Carbohydrate restriction suppresses appetite, producing inadvertent energy deficit. Sustained low energy availability suppresses both the thyroid and reproductive axes — well documented in the athlete and RED-S literature. Loucks established disruption of LH pulsatility below an energy availability threshold, independent of body composition.

**[SUPPORTED — single study] Sex difference.** In one-day fasting among obese adults, female participants showed altered cortisol rhythm — higher amplitude, earlier peak — while **none of the rhythm parameters changed in males** *(Front Nutr 2023;10:1078508)*.

**>>> COACH USE — HIGH VALUE:** for a long-term low-carb user reporting cold intolerance, fatigue, hair thinning, or cycle disruption, **increasing intake is frequently the correction, not further restriction.** Counterintuitive and commonly right. Check actual intake before adjusting macros.

**>>> TERMINOLOGY:** do not use "adrenal fatigue." *(Cadegiani & Kater, BMC Endocr Disord 2016 — systematic review found no substantiation as a clinical entity.)* Use "chronic stress" or "under-eating," which are defensible and point to the same intervention.

**>>> Subject to disordered eating gating.** This content identifies under-eating and must never be deployed as encouragement toward restriction-then-refeed cycling. *(Referral framework pattern 12.)*

## 5.5 Reverse T3

**[HYPOTHESIS — coherent from mechanism, not validated as a clinical test]**

Logic: carbohydrate restriction alone lowered T3 without raising rT3 (5.3); rT3 is produced by D3, which inflammation and illness induce (5.1).

Proposed pattern: low T3 + normal rT3 + normal TSH + asymptomatic → consistent with adaptation. Low T3 + **elevated** rT3 → evaluate inflammation, infection, hepatic function, energy availability.

**Two caveats that must always accompany it:**
1. The 1980 all-fat data found very high fat intake alone raised rT3 ~123%
2. rT3 testing is genuinely contested in mainstream endocrinology — assay variability and a documented history of over-interpretation in functional medicine

**>>> COACH RULE:** one input among several, never a verdict. Always recommend practitioner interpretation.

## 5.6 Autoimmune thyroid disease

**[ESTABLISHED]** In iodine-sufficient populations, autoimmune thyroiditis is the **predominant** cause of hypothyroidism. Markedly more prevalent in women. **Antibodies frequently precede measurable TSH or hormone change by years.**

**>>> THE COURSE'S FRAMING IS CORRECT AND WORTH PRESERVING:** Hashimoto's is an immune condition targeting the thyroid, not a thyroid condition. The gland is often functionally capable and under attack.

**Why this matters practically:** it redirects the investigation toward gut barrier function, inflammatory load, and chronic burden *(Lessons 10, 11, 13)* rather than toward thyroid support alone. It also explains why many users on thyroid replacement remain symptomatic — replacement addresses the hormone, not the attack.

**>>> COACH RULE:** do not diagnose Hashimoto's, do not interpret antibody titres, do not advise on thyroid hormone replacement. **Refer** — framework pattern 1.

## 5.7 Iodine — safety-relevant

**[SUPPORTED]** Iodine is required for thyroid hormone synthesis; deficiency remains a significant global problem. **But supraphysiological intake is associated with increased thyroid autoimmunity and can precipitate or worsen dysfunction in susceptible individuals.**

Dietary iodine at normal intakes is not the concern. High-dose supplementation is.

**>>> COACH RULE:** antibody status should be known before iodine supplementation above food levels. Route the question to a practitioner.

**>>> This qualifies the published iodine recommendations in Lessons 13 and 15.** The coach attaches the caution.

**>>> VERIFY the thresholds before this reaches course copy.** The relationship is dose-dependent and the course would be qualifying its own prior recommendation — it needs to be right.

## 5.8 Brown adipose tissue and cold exposure

**[ESTABLISHED]** Brown adipose tissue expresses UCP1, which uncouples substrate oxidation from ATP synthesis, dissipating the proton gradient as heat. Cold is the most potent physiological activator via sympathetic signalling.

**[CONTESTED] Magnitude and durability in adults.** Recruitment increases with repeated exposure and **regresses when exposure ceases** — trainable, not permanent. Contribution to adult resting energy expenditure is real but modest and debated.

**>>> The published Lesson 5 says "permanently."** The coach should say "while the practice continues" if asked directly, without characterizing the lesson as wrong.

**>>> SAFETY — apply the lesson's own "A Note for Women," which is well calibrated:**
- Minimum effective dose; brief mild stimulus usually sufficient
- **The user's own response is the guide** — energized and warm within minutes is fine; exhausted, deeply chilled for hours, or feeling stressed means too much
- **Reduce or skip entirely** where the user reports poor sleep, high training load, high life stress, cycle disruption, or any signal from 5.3's delayed-presentation list
- **No protocols** for users reporting cardiovascular disease, arrhythmia, uncontrolled hypertension, or pregnancy — route to physician
- Personal health situation flag active → no durations, temperatures, or protocols

## 5.9 NEAT

**[ESTABLISHED]** Non-exercise activity thermogenesis — ambulation, posture maintenance, spontaneous movement — constitutes a substantial and highly variable component of total daily expenditure, typically exceeding structured exercise in non-athletes.

**NEAT is suppressed under energy restriction, largely unconsciously.** A major component of adaptive thermogenesis and a principal mechanism by which restriction produces less loss than predicted.

**>>> COACH USE:** explains the plateau without invoking metabolic damage. Reframes the intervention — increasing incidental movement reaches a larger expenditure pool than adding a training session. Connects to Lesson 17.

**>>> Levine's Mayo work is the standard reference. Pin before quoting proportions.**

## 5.10 Autonomic framing

**[ESTABLISHED in outline; FRAMEWORK in application]** Sympathetic activation raises hepatic glucose output, reduces splanchnic blood flow and digestive secretion, and prioritizes immediate availability over storage and repair. Parasympathetic dominance supports digestive secretion, motility, and absorption.

**>>> COACH RULE:** the framing is sound and useful. **Do not use it to explain away a medical cause.** A user reporting persistent fatigue, cold intolerance, hair loss, or cycle disruption goes through the referral framework. "Your nervous system is in fight-or-flight" is not an answer to undiagnosed Hashimoto's or iron deficiency.

**>>> For reading autonomic state**, direct the user to the Lesson 3 self-check signals — morning temperature, resting heart rate, peripheral warmth. Subject to the quantification-spiral caution in that layer.

# COMMON USER QUESTIONS FROM THIS LESSON

**"My TSH is normal but I have every symptom."**

This is the single most common question this lesson will generate, and the answer is the chapter's whole point. TSH measures the signal *to* your gland. It doesn't measure whether the hormone is being activated. **Ask for free T3 and thyroid antibodies specifically** — many doctors won't run them unless asked.

Also worth checking at the same time: **ferritin** and **B12**, because iron deficiency produces a nearly identical picture and a normal blood count doesn't rule it out.

**>>> Route per framework pattern 1. Do not interpret her results.**

**"Should I take iodine for my thyroid?"**

Your thyroid needs iodine and food sources are fine. **Before a high-dose supplement, get thyroid antibodies checked** — if your immune system is already attacking the gland, high-dose iodine can make it worse. One blood draw settles it.

**"My T3 came back low on keto. Should I stop?"**

Depends entirely on how you feel. Low T3 with normal TSH in someone who feels well is very likely your body needing less active hormone while running on fat. **If you feel well, don't chase the number.**

If you feel worse — cold, tired, hair thinning, cycle off — that's different, and the answer is usually **more carbohydrate and more food overall**, not less. The safe starches from Chapter 9.

**Timing matters:** rough for the first two weeks is normal. Still rough at week six, or newly rough at week six, is a signal.

**"I'm losing hair. Is it the diet?"**

Possibly, and here's the detail nobody mentions: **hair shedding lags about three months behind whatever caused it.** So look at what you were doing three months ago, not what you're doing now.

Most common causes in this context: under-eating, low thyroid, and low ferritin. **All three are worth testing** — ferritin, full thyroid panel including free T3. And be honest with yourself about whether you've actually been eating enough.

**"Am I eating too little? I'm just not hungry."**

This is extremely common on low-carb and it's the most underdiagnosed problem the course encounters. Appetite suppression is a feature, and it's how people end up in a deficit they never chose.

**For your first month, eat protein on a schedule rather than waiting to want it.** And if you feel worse rather than better after the first couple of weeks, eating more is usually the correction.

**>>> Do not provide numeric targets under gating. Subject to framework pattern 12.**

**"How cold, how long, how often?"**

Less than you think. The lesson's own guidance is the right answer: minimum effective dose, and **your own response is the instruction.** Energized and warm within a few minutes is right. Exhausted or chilled for hours afterward is too much.

**Skip it entirely** if you're sleeping badly, training hard, under real stress, or your cycle is off. It's an addition, never a requirement.

**>>> No protocols for cardiovascular disease, arrhythmia, uncontrolled hypertension, or pregnancy.**

**"Does cold exposure really raise my metabolism permanently?"**

It raises resting energy expenditure modestly **while you keep doing it.** It works like training — build it up and it holds; stop and it fades. Worth doing as one input among several, not as a replacement for anything.

**"Is 'adrenal fatigue' real?"**

The stress physiology is real. That particular label doesn't hold up — a systematic review found no support for it as a distinct condition, and using it tends to send people toward supplements instead of the actual problem.

**What is real:** chronic stress and under-eating both suppress thyroid output, and both respond to being addressed directly. Same symptoms, better explanation, clearer fix.

# EDGE CASES

**Already on thyroid medication and still symptomatic** → prescriber conversation about dose and formulation. **Never suggest a change.**

**Pregnancy or trying to conceive** → thyroid requirements change substantially and the stakes are higher. Route to obstetric care. No carbohydrate restriction guidance.

**Known Hashimoto's** → no high-dose iodine. Emphasize the gut and inflammation material. Route thyroid management to clinician.

**Reported cardiovascular disease, arrhythmia, uncontrolled hypertension, pregnancy** → no cold exposure protocols.

**Heavy menstrual bleeding with fatigue** → ferritin plus gynecological evaluation. Iron loss is the most common cause of deficiency in women and is frequently overlooked.

**Users under 20 or over 70** → energy availability and thyroid physiology both differ. Be conservative and route more readily.
