# ADD 1 — Thirty-Six Men in Minnesota

**Placement: immediately after "This response is protective, not broken."**

This is the anchor the chapter is missing. Not more explanation — proof, with human beings in it.

> #### Thirty-Six Men in Minnesota
>
> In 1944, researchers at the University of Minnesota ran an experiment we would never be allowed to run today.
>
> They took young men — healthy, screened for physical and psychological fitness, conscientious objectors who volunteered for the study instead of going to war — and cut their food roughly in half for six months. Then they measured everything.
>
> What happened was not just weight loss.
>
> Their metabolic rate dropped far more than their smaller bodies could account for. They were cold all the time. Their pulses slowed. They lost interest in sex, in their friends, in nearly everything they used to care about.
>
> And they became consumed by food. Not hungry — **consumed.** They collected recipes. They read cookbooks for hours. They cut their food into tiny pieces to make meals last longer. Some developed rituals around eating that they couldn't explain and couldn't stop.
>
> When the study ended and they were finally allowed to eat freely, many ate enormous amounts and still couldn't feel full. For months.
>
> **Now read that list again, slowly, and think about who you know.**
>
> Cold hands. No energy. No interest in sex. Not wanting to see anyone. Thinking about food constantly. Losing control around it at night.
>
> That is not a description of a woman with a willpower problem. **That is a description of a body being underfed.** And it was produced deliberately, reliably, in healthy, motivated young men who had chosen to be there — using nothing but restriction.
>
> **Your body cannot tell the difference between a famine and a diet.** It only knows how much food is arriving.

# ADD 2 — What Your Body Cuts First

**Placement: directly after Minnesota.**

This is where the chapter becomes useful. It turns a concept into a list she can check herself against.

> #### The Triage: What Gets Cut First
>
> When your body decides resources are short, it doesn't cut spending evenly across the board. It triages. And the order tells you almost everything.
>
> **Funded no matter what:**
> Your brain. Your heart. Keeping your blood sugar from crashing. These get paid first, always.
>
> **Cut early, without hesitation:**
> Fertility and your menstrual cycle. Body temperature. Hair and nail growth. Digestion. The drive to move, to socialize, to be interested in things. Anything your body considers optional during a famine.
>
> **This is why the first signs of underfeeding look nothing like a nutrition problem.**
>
> Your cycle goes irregular and you blame stress. Your hands are always cold and you blame the weather. Your hair thins in the shower and you blame your age. You stop wanting to go out and you blame your personality.
>
> None of it is any of those things. It's a budget decision your body made without consulting you, and it cut the things it thought it could survive without.
>
> **And it explains something that sounds impossible until you understand the triage: you can be eating too little and gaining weight at the same time.**
>
> Those aren't contradictory. A body that has concluded resources are scarce becomes extremely reluctant to spend them — and extremely eager to hold onto whatever arrives.

# ADD 3 — How to Read Your Own Signals

**Placement: after the triage, before the measurement section.**

**This is the most practically valuable thing I can add to this chapter.** The chapter tells her metabolism is a signaling system and then jumps to blood tests. Most of her body's signals are free, immediate, and she can start reading them tomorrow morning.

It also bridges naturally into your lab section — soft signals first, then blood work.

> #### You Can Read Most of This Without a Blood Test
>
> Before we talk about lab work, understand this: your body is already telling you what state it's in. You just haven't been taught what to listen for.
>
> These cost nothing and you can start tomorrow.
>
> **Your morning temperature.** Take it with a basic thermometer before you get out of bed, same time each day. A body in conservation mode runs cool. If you're consistently below about 97.8°F, your body is spending less than it should.
>
> **Your resting heart rate.** Check it first thing, before you sit up. Write it down for two weeks. **The number itself matters less than the direction.** A rate that's drifting down while you're eating less and training more is not fitness improving — it's your body slowing down to conserve.
>
> **Your hands and feet.** Are they cold when nobody else's are? Do you need socks to bed? That's one of the earliest and most reliable signs on the list.
>
> **Your cycle.** Length, regularity, and how heavy it is. Your body treats reproduction as optional in a famine, so this is often the first thing to change — and the last thing anyone connects to food.
>
> **Your hair.** In the shower drain, on your pillow, in your brush. **Important: hair shedding lags about three months behind whatever caused it.** So if you're losing hair now, look at what you were doing in the spring. Almost nobody makes that connection.
>
> **Your sleep.** Not how long, but whether you wake rested, and whether you wake at 3am for no reason. Waking in the small hours is frequently a blood sugar and cortisol signal, not a stress one.
>
> **Your energy pattern.** Does it dip hard mid-afternoon? Do you need caffeine to function rather than to enjoy? Do workouts leave you energized or wrecked for the rest of the day?
>
> **Your appetite.** How long can you go comfortably between meals? A body that's fueling well can go four or five hours without drama. A body in trouble gets shaky, irritable, or panicky by hour two.
>
> **Here's how to use this.** Write down where you are on all eight today. Don't do anything with it yet. Come back in eight weeks and look again.
>
> **These are the signals that move first** — before the scale, before your lab work, before your doctor notices anything. If your temperature is coming up, your hands are warming, your hair is settling, and you can go five hours without thinking about food, **your metabolism is improving whether or not the scale has moved yet.**
>
> And if they're all going the other way while you're eating less and exercising more, that's your body telling you the plan is wrong. Not that you need to try harder.

# ADD 4 — What Changing the Signals Actually Means

**Placement: closing the chapter.**

Your chapter ends on lab values, which leaves its own argument unresolved. Your best line — *you must first change the signals it is receiving* — is a promise the chapter doesn't cash.

> #### So What Are the Signals?
>
> If your body is running a protection program, there are only two ways forward.
>
> One is to fight it. Restrict harder, exercise more, push against a system built over millions of years specifically to resist being pushed. People do win this way sometimes. It costs enormously and it almost never holds.
>
> The other is to change what your body is hearing. So let's be specific about what that means, because these are the levers for the rest of this book:
>
> **Enough food.** This is the one nobody expects. A body convinced food is scarce will not let go of stored energy, and the fastest way to convince it otherwise is to feed it consistently.
>
> **Enough protein.** The single clearest signal that you're building rather than surviving.
>
> **Sleep.** One bad night measurably changes how your body handles food the next day. You cannot out-eat a nervous system running on four hours.
>
> **Regular meals rather than chaos.** Your body learns your patterns and prepares for them. Erratic eating is itself a stress signal.
>
> **Morning light.** It sets the clock that everything else runs on.
>
> **Movement that signals capacity, not crisis.** Strength and walking, not punishment.
>
> **Lowering the insulin load.** Which is where most of this book lives — and it's one lever among several, not the whole answer.
>
> **Notice what's missing from that list.** There's no single trick, no product, no shortcut. You've been sold single levers before and they didn't work, and this is why.
>
> Your metabolism isn't waiting to be forced. **It's waiting to be convinced.**
>
> And it's why, when we reach the chapter on what stalls progress, some of the advice will be to eat *more.* If that sounds backwards, come back and read about the men in Minnesota.

# OPTIONAL — Two additions to your lab section

Both are pure additions. Your existing text stands.

**ApoB.** You tell her the triglyceride-to-HDL ratio matters more than LDL. **ApoB is the single test that settles the question** — it counts the actual particles rather than measuring the cholesterol inside them. One line gives her something specific to ask for:

> If you want the most direct measure available, ask for an **ApoB** test. It counts the actual number of particles in your blood rather than estimating the cholesterol inside them, and it's the number that tells you the most for the least money. Most doctors will run it if you ask.

**HOMA-IR.** If she has fasting insulin and fasting glucose from the same draw, she already has what's needed:

> If you have both a fasting insulin and a fasting glucose from the same blood draw, those two numbers can be combined into a single score called **HOMA-IR** that estimates insulin resistance better than either one alone. Many practitioners will calculate it, and some labs report it automatically. If you've already had both tests, you already have the answer sitting in a drawer.
