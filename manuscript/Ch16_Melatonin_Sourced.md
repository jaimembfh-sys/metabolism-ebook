**Replaces "The Melatonin and Insulin Connection."** Your existing framing is intact — this adds the sourcing and the variant material.

> #### The Melatonin and Insulin Connection
>
> Sleep is the most underappreciated pillar of metabolic health. You already know poor sleep raises cortisol and hunger hormones. **But there's a deeper connection, and most people — including most doctors — have never heard it.**
>
> **Your pancreas has melatonin receptors on it.**
>
> Not metaphorically. The beta cells that produce your insulin carry receptors for melatonin, and researchers have confirmed this in human pancreatic tissue. **When melatonin binds to them, it tells those cells to reduce insulin production.**
>
> Which means your body is deliberately designed to have **its lowest insulin levels while you sleep** — allowing deep cellular repair and overnight fat burning. **Melatonin isn't just a sleep hormone. It's part of how your body clears insulin out of the way overnight.**
>
> #### Which Creates a Problem If You Eat Late
>
> Now picture eating a carbohydrate-heavy meal or a sugary snack right before bed.
>
> **Your blood sugar rises, demanding insulin. And your rising melatonin is simultaneously telling your pancreas to make less of it.**
>
> Two systems working against each other. The result is worse blood sugar control **and** disrupted sleep architecture — you get the metabolic cost and the poor sleep together.
>
> **This is why the same meal is handled differently at 8 a.m. and 9 p.m.** It isn't in your head. The hormonal conditions are genuinely different.
>
> **Aim to finish your last meal at least two to three hours before bed**, letting insulin fall before melatonin rises.
>
> #### And Here's Why Some People Get Away With It
>
> This is the part that explains a great deal, and it's why comparing yourself to other people is a waste of time.
>
> **There's a common variation in the gene for one of those melatonin receptors.** People who carry it produce **more** of the receptor on their beta cells — which means melatonin suppresses their insulin **more strongly.**
>
> This isn't a rare mutation. **Roughly half of people of European ancestry carry it.** About seventy percent of East Asians.
>
> And it matters. That variant is **the single strongest known genetic link to elevated fasting blood sugar and impaired insulin release** — it turned up in large-scale genetic studies as one of the most robust findings connected to type 2 diabetes.
>
> **Researchers tested it directly.** They gave people melatonin and measured how well they handled glucose afterward. **The impairment showed up only in the carriers. In non-carriers, nothing.**
>
> **So when your friend eats dessert at 10 p.m. and seems fine — she might genuinely be fine.** And you might genuinely not be. **Roughly half of the women reading this are in the more sensitive group, and none of you know which.**
>
> **Which is why I'd default to the earlier eating window rather than testing it.** If you're in the sensitive half, late meals cost you more than they cost other people. And if you're not, eating earlier costs you nothing.
>
> #### A Word About Melatonin Supplements
>
> This is also why I'd be careful with melatonin as a supplement, and it's the opposite of what you'll hear.
>
> **Melatonin use has increased roughly fivefold in recent decades**, mostly for sleep. But read the mechanism above again: **melatonin suppresses insulin secretion.**
>
> A study published this year gave healthy people supplemental melatonin and measured their glucose handling. **In carriers of that gene variant, it impaired both glucose tolerance and beta-cell function. In non-carriers, it didn't.** The researchers concluded that melatonin's metabolic effects need to be taken seriously and that a personalized approach may be warranted.
>
> **And the doses in commercial products are frequently far higher than what your body actually produces.**
>
> **I'm not telling you never to take it.** If you use it occasionally for travel or a disrupted night, that's a reasonable decision to make with your doctor — particularly if your blood sugar is already an issue.
>
> **What I'd rather you do is raise your own.** Morning light. Genuinely dim evenings. A consistent wake time. **Those produce melatonin at the right time in the right amount, rather than adding an arbitrary dose at an arbitrary hour** — and they're free.

# SOURCES

**Melatonin receptors on beta cells and inhibition of insulin secretion:**

1. **Ramracheya RD, Muller DS, Squires PE, et al.** Function and expression of melatonin receptors on human pancreatic islets. *J Pineal Res*. 2008;44:273–279. — **human islet tissue. This is the citation for the core claim.**

2. **Mühlbauer E, Peschke E.** Evidence for the expression of both the MT1- and, in addition, the MT2-melatonin receptor, in the rat pancreas, islet and beta-cell. *J Pineal Res*. 2007;42:105–106.

3. **Peschke E, Bach AG, Mühlbauer E.** Parallel signaling pathways of melatonin in the pancreatic beta-cell. *J Pineal Res*. 2006;40:184–191.

**Mechanism:** MT2 signals via inhibitory G-proteins (Gi), reducing cAMP and thereby suppressing insulin secretion. Confirmed in clonal beta cells — **overexpression of MTNR1B produces stronger melatonin-mediated inhibition of insulin secretion.**

**Review:** **Mulder H, et al.** Melatonin signalling and type 2 diabetes risk: too little, too much or just right? *Diabetologia*. 2017;60. doi:10.1007/s00125-017-4249-8. — **good single-source overview if you want one citation covering the whole picture.** Also: Mulder H, Nagorny CLF, Lyssenko V, Groop L. Melatonin receptors in pancreatic islets — good morning to a novel type 2 diabetes gene. *Diabetologia*. 2009;52:1240–1249.

**The genetic variant:**

4. **Lyssenko V, Nagorny CLF, Erdos MR, Wierup N, Jonsson A, Spégel P, et al.** Common variant in MTNR1B associated with increased risk of type 2 diabetes and impaired early insulin secretion. *Nat Genet*. 2009;41(1):82–88.

5. **Prokopenko I, Langenberg C, Florez JC, Saxena R, Soranzo N, Thorleifsson G, et al.** Variants in MTNR1B influence fasting glucose levels. *Nat Genet*. 2009;41(1):77–81.

**The variant is rs10830963 (G allele).** Key facts:
- **The strongest association among known SNPs with elevated fasting glucose and impaired insulin secretion**
- Described in the literature as a **"remarkably robust"** genetic association with type 2 diabetes
- **Allele frequency: 48% in Europeans, 70% in East Asians, 13% in African Americans**
- **More G alleles → higher MTNR1B mRNA expression → stronger melatonin-mediated inhibition of insulin secretion**
- Mechanism: the risk SNP appears to introduce a binding site for the transcription factor NEUROD1, driving increased receptor expression in beta cells
- **Note:** a 2025 stem-cell paper states that a causal relationship for rs10830963 is "yet to be established" — **the association is robust; the causal chain is still being worked out.** Worth knowing if challenged.

**The human experimental data:**

6. **Garaulet M, Gómez-Abellán P, Rubio-Sastre P, Madrid JA, Saxena R, Scheer FAJL.** Common type 2 diabetes risk variant in MTNR1B worsens the deleterious effect of melatonin on glucose tolerance in humans. *Metabolism*. 2015;64(12):1650–1657.
   - **The decrease in glucose tolerance from exogenous melatonin occurred only in risk variant carriers — not in non-carriers — in both morning and evening testing.**

7. **Qian J, Stefanovski D, Andersen PAK, Vujovic N, Kelly L, Lepson J, Nguyen H, Byrne S, Wang W, Mandrup-Poulsen T, Adler G, Garaulet M, Saxena R, Scheer FAJL.** Melatonin impairs glucose tolerance, first-phase insulin secretion, and insulin feedback inhibition; interaction with MTNR1B diabetes risk variant. *Diabetes Care*. 2026. doi:10.2337/dc26-0164
   - 21 healthy participants of European ancestry; **randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover**; 10 risk carriers, 11 non-carriers
   - Highly controlled 5-day laboratory protocol; **5 mg oral melatonin or placebo**
   - **Melatonin impaired glucose tolerance and beta-cell function in risk carriers but not non-carriers**
   - Authors note **melatonin use has quintupled in recent decades** and call for a genotype-based personalized approach
   - **This is the citation for the supplement caution, and it's from this year.**

**Additional detail worth knowing but handle carefully:**

8. One study reported that melatonin administered **in the morning** increased OGTT glucose AUC by 186% and peak glucose by 21%; **in the evening**, AUC by 54% and peak by 27% *(cited within PMC9207528 — trace the primary before quoting)*.
   - **Note the counterintuitive pattern:** the morning effect was larger, because endogenous melatonin is low then, so an exogenous dose has more relative impact. **The evening figures are the real-world-relevant ones. Don't quote the 186% without that context** — it would mislead.

**Also emerging:**

9. **A 2026 randomized crossover trial** examined MTNR1B genotype and breakfast composition (protein-enriched vs carbohydrate-rich) on postprandial glucose, concluding that **genotype significantly affects markers associated with type 2 diabetes risk** and that genotype-based advice on meal timing and composition may reduce risk. Preprint stage — **worth watching, don't cite yet.**

## B4 — Melatonin, insulin secretion, and MTNR1B

**[ESTABLISHED]** MT1 and MT2 melatonin receptors are expressed on human pancreatic beta cells (Ramracheya 2008; Mühlbauer & Peschke 2007). **MT2 signals via inhibitory G-proteins, reducing cAMP and suppressing glucose-stimulated insulin secretion** (Peschke 2006). Endogenous melatonin rise therefore coincides with reduced insulin secretory capacity.

**Consequence:** carbohydrate intake during the melatonin-rising window produces a glycemic load when insulin secretion is least available — **impaired evening glucose tolerance relative to morning.** The published lesson's claim is correct and well supported.

**[ESTABLISHED] The MTNR1B variant — and this explains individual variation:**

**rs10830963 (G allele)** is the strongest known single-SNP association with elevated fasting glucose and impaired insulin secretion, and a robust type 2 diabetes risk locus (Lyssenko 2009, *Nat Genet*; Prokopenko 2009, *Nat Genet*).

- **Allele frequency: 48% Europeans, 70% East Asians, 13% African Americans**
- **More G alleles → higher MTNR1B expression → stronger melatonin-mediated suppression of insulin secretion**
- **Garaulet 2015 (*Metabolism*):** exogenous melatonin impaired glucose tolerance **only in carriers**, morning and evening
- **Qian 2026 (*Diabetes Care*):** randomized double-blind placebo-controlled crossover, 5 mg melatonin — **impaired glucose tolerance and beta-cell function in carriers, not non-carriers**

**>>> COACH USE — the answer to "why does my friend eat late and stay fine?"** Roughly half of European-ancestry individuals carry the variant. **Individual variation in late-eating tolerance has a specific genetic basis**, and it removes the moral framing entirely.

**>>> COACH RULE — do not recommend genotype testing.** The actionable advice is identical either way: eat earlier. **If she carries it, late eating costs more; if she doesn't, eating earlier costs nothing.** Testing changes no recommendation.

**>>> CALIBRATION:** the association is robust; **a causal relationship for rs10830963 is described in the 2025 literature as not yet established.** Present as a strong association with a plausible mechanism.

**>>> EXOGENOUS MELATONIN — the caution is now well sourced:**
- **Do not recommend melatonin as a sleep or metabolic intervention.** Route to clinician
- May state: **melatonin use has increased roughly fivefold**, it suppresses insulin secretion by design, and a 2026 *Diabetes Care* trial found impaired glucose tolerance and beta-cell function in variant carriers at 5 mg
- Commercial doses frequently exceed physiological production substantially
- **Particular caution warranted in any user with dysglycemia, prediabetes, or type 2 diabetes** — route to prescriber
- **The free alternative raises endogenous melatonin at the correct time:** morning light, dim evenings, consistent wake time

**>>> Do not quote the 186% morning OGTT figure** without the context that morning administration produces a larger relative effect because endogenous melatonin is low. **The evening figures (~54% AUC, ~27% peak) are the relevant ones.** And trace the primary before using either.
