> #### Stomach Acid: The Step Nobody Talks About
>
> Before bile, before enzymes, before absorption — **there's stomach acid. And it does more jobs than you'd expect.**
>
> **It breaks down protein.** Which matters enormously given everything Chapter 9 said. **You can eat all the protein in the world and not get the amino acids out of it if your stomach acid is low.**
>
> **It's your first line of defense.** Strong acid kills most of the pathogens that arrive with your food. **Weak acid lets them through** — which is one of the main ways gut problems start in the first place. This is also why long-term acid suppression is associated with a higher risk of intestinal infections and bacterial overgrowth.
>
> **It triggers the next step.** The acidity of what leaves your stomach is the signal that tells your gallbladder to release bile and your pancreas to release enzymes. **Low acid means a weak signal, which means poor bile release** — and you now know from the section above what that costs you.
>
> **And it's required to absorb minerals.** Iron, B12, zinc, magnesium, calcium. **All of them need acid.** Which matters given what Chapter 4 said about iron and magnesium being structural parts of your energy machinery.
>
> #### Why It Declines
>
> Production tends to fall with age. **Chronic stress reduces it** — the same shutdown we cover in Chapter 12. And acid-blocking medications reduce it by design.
>
> #### Signs It May Be Low
>
> Counterintuitively, **low stomach acid often feels like too much.** Reflux, burping shortly after meals, food sitting heavily. Undigested food in your stool. Feeling worse rather than better after a protein-heavy meal. Nutrient deficiencies despite eating well.
>
> #### What Helps
>
> **Start with the free things, because they work and they're the foundation:**
>
> Eat slowly and chew thoroughly — Chapter 12 explains why this matters more than it sounds. Don't drink large amounts with meals. Try a splash of apple cider vinegar or lemon in water shortly before eating. Include bitter foods — arugula, dandelion greens, radicchio — which stimulate the whole digestive cascade.
>
> **And if that isn't enough: betaine HCl.**
>
> This is supplemental stomach acid, taken with protein-containing meals. It's a well-established tool and for the right person it can be genuinely transformative — protein finally digests, bloating resolves, and the whole downstream cascade starts working again.
>
> **One important caution.** If you have or have had **an ulcer, gastritis, H. pylori, or any active inflammation of the stomach lining, betaine HCl is not for you.** Adding acid to an already-irritated or eroded surface works against healing.
>
> **The same applies if you take anti-inflammatory painkillers regularly** — ibuprofen, naproxen, or daily aspirin. Those thin the protective layer that keeps your own acid off your stomach lining, so adding more isn't a good idea while you're taking them.
>
> **And if you're currently on an acid-blocking medication, don't add acid alongside it.** That's a conversation with your prescriber, not a supplement decision. Please don't stop that medication based on this chapter either — rebound acid can be genuinely awful, and many people need them.
>
> **If any of that applies to you, talk to a practitioner before starting.** Not because HCl is dangerous in general — it isn't — but because these are the specific situations where it's the wrong tool, and they're worth ruling out first.
>
> **If none of it applies, this is reasonable to try.** Start with one capsule with a protein-containing meal and see how you feel. Most people who need it know within a few meals.
>
> **And regardless of whether you use it — start with the free things.** Slowing down, chewing, and not flooding your stomach with liquid do a remarkable amount, and they're the foundation everything else sits on.

## B6a — Betaine HCl

**[SUPPORTED as a tool; contraindications are specific rather than general]**

The course now includes betaine HCl with named contraindications. **The coach may discuss it accordingly.**

**Mechanism of concern:** the gastric mucosa is protected by a prostaglandin-maintained mucus and bicarbonate layer that holds surface pH near neutral despite luminal pH ~1.5. **With an intact barrier, supplemental acid is handled as endogenous acid is.** With a compromised barrier, acid reaches tissue not built for it, impairing healing and potentially extending a lesion. **This is why acid suppression is the treatment for ulcers** — not because acid caused them, but because reducing exposure permits closure.

**Contraindications the coach must state:**
- Active or prior peptic ulcer
- Gastritis or any active gastric inflammation
- *H. pylori*, current or untreated
- Erosive esophagitis or Barrett's
- **Regular NSAID use** — ibuprofen, naproxen, daily aspirin. **The most commonly missed one.** NSAIDs impair the prostaglandin-mediated protective layer
- Corticosteroids
- Current acid-blocking medication
- Pregnancy

**>>> COACH RULES:**
- **Screen for NSAIDs explicitly.** Do not wait for the user to mention them — ask. This is the highest-yield question, and it is disproportionately relevant in women over 50 taking them for joint pain.
- **Never advise stopping or reducing an acid-blocking medication.** Route to prescriber.
- **If any contraindication is present or uncertain: route to a practitioner** who can assess and, where relevant, test for *H. pylori*. Do not provide a protocol.
- **If none is present:** the coach may state that it's reasonable to try, starting low with a protein-containing meal. **Lead with the food-based approaches regardless** — slow eating, thorough chewing, limiting fluid volume with meals, bitters, and acidic preload. Those are the foundation and carry no risk.
- **Do not titrate a user upward.** The standard escalating-dose method uses gastric warmth as the endpoint, and that sensation is indistinguishable from irritation. **Dose escalation belongs with a practitioner.**
- **Any report of pain, worsening reflux, or new symptoms after starting → stop and refer.**

**>>> ON THE PATHOGEN ARGUMENT — for accuracy if a user raises it:**

Gastric acid *is* a genuine antimicrobial barrier, and chronic suppression demonstrably increases risk of enteric infection and small intestinal overgrowth. **That argument is correct and the coach can affirm it.**

**But it does not make HCl appropriate in *H. pylori*.** That organism colonizes the mucus layer where surface pH is near neutral, and buffers its immediate microenvironment with urease — it is not exposed to luminal acid in the first place. Notably, **acid suppression is part of standard eradication therapy**, because raising pH makes the organism more metabolically active and more susceptible to amoxicillin.

**>>> Be precise about what's claimed:** the *H. pylori* contraindication rests on **mucosal irritation**, not on a demonstrated failure to kill the organism. There is no good evidence that supplemental HCl worsens *H. pylori* colonization. **Do not claim that it does.**

**>>> VERIFY before this reaches course copy:**
- Prostaglandin-mediated mucosal defense and NSAID impairment of it — standard, pin a reference
- Betaine HCl re-acidification in PPI users (the one human study establishing it does what it claims)
- **PPIs as part of standard *H. pylori* eradication therapy and the mechanism** — this is the detail that undoes the pathogen argument and it should be exact
- Silent ulcer prevalence and the NSAID asymmetry — **the celecoxib label statement that only one in five patients with a serious GI event is symptomatic**, and the case-control finding of a 5.4 odds ratio in women versus 1.9 in men
