# ADD 1 — THE QUESTIONS THAT COME FIRST

**Placement: before or inside "Common Emotional Triggers."**

**The most valuable addition to this chapter**, and every tool in it is from your own course. Your trigger list is accurate — these questions just come earlier in the sequence.

> #### Before You Ask What You're Feeling, Ask These
>
> Your trigger list is the right place to look — **and there are a few physical questions that come first**, because you've spent seventeen chapters learning why.
>
> If you're standing in the kitchen at 10 p.m. and you can't work out why, **run this before you reach for the emotional explanation:**
>
> **Did you actually eat enough today?**
>
> This is the most common answer and almost nobody checks it. **Under-eating during the day is the single biggest driver of losing control at night.** And it's particularly easy on this way of eating, because low-carb suppresses appetite — so you genuinely weren't hungry at lunch, and now it's 10 p.m. and your body is collecting.
>
> **That isn't emotional eating. That's your body doing arithmetic.**
>
> **Did you get enough protein?**
>
> Same question, more specific. **Chapter 9's number.** Protein is the signal that shuts appetite down for hours, and it's the easiest thing to fall short on when you're not hungry.
>
> **How did you sleep?**
>
> One bad night raises ghrelin and lowers leptin — Chapter 7. **Your brain is being told to eat and simultaneously not being told to stop.** The cravings for something sweet at 10 p.m. after four hours' sleep are hormonal, not emotional.
>
> **Is this the same time it always happens?**
>
> Chapter 7 again. **Ghrelin is trained.** If you've eaten at 10 p.m. for six months, your body now produces a hunger signal at 9:45 in anticipation. **It's a habit with a hormone attached** — and the good news is that it fades within days of removing the cue.
>
> **Have you lost weight recently?**
>
> If so, you may be **genuinely, measurably hungrier than before you started.** Chapter 13 covers the study — leptin down, ghrelin up, still shifted a full year later. That's not weakness returning. That's a physiological headwind.
>
> **Did you drink alcohol?**
>
> It reliably lowers restraint around food, usually at night, when the pull is already strongest. Chapter 9.
>
> #### Why the Order Matters
>
> **Because if it's one of those, no amount of emotional work will fix it** — and you'll conclude you failed at the emotional work too.
>
> A woman who ate 700 calories and 40 grams of protein and slept five hours is not standing in the pantry because of her beliefs about her worth. **She's standing there because she's hungry**, and telling her it's emotional adds a layer of shame to something that was physical.
>
> **So: food first, sleep second, patterns third. Then the emotional questions below** — which are real, and which matter enormously once the physical ones are ruled out.

# ADD 2 — THE STUDY BEHIND YOUR CENTRAL CLAIM

**Placement: in "The Root of Self-Sabotage," supporting your existing self-compassion line.**

**Your claim that self-compassion is the most powerful tool here is experimentally supported**, and most people making it don't know that.

> #### And This Isn't Just a Nice Idea
>
> When I say self-compassion is the most powerful tool you have here, **that's not a sentiment. Researchers tested it.**
>
> They took women who were restrained eaters — dieters — and had them eat a doughnut while watching television, deliberately simulating the experience of breaking a diet.
>
> Then some of them received brief instruction in responding to it **self-compassionately.** The others didn't.
>
> **The women given the self-compassion instruction experienced less distress — and were less likely to overeat afterward.**
>
> Not more likely. **Less.**
>
> #### Which Answers the Objection You're Probably Having
>
> Most women's instinct here is that being kind to yourself about eating is just permission to give up. **The researchers addressed that directly.**
>
> What self-compassion does is let you **forgive yourself for one instance of breaking a diet without losing sight of what you were doing.** That's the whole mechanism. It isn't permission. It's the thing that stops one doughnut from becoming a three-week collapse.
>
> **The self-criticism is what does the damage.** You eat something you didn't plan to, you feel terrible about yourself, the feeling is unbearable, and food is what you use to make unbearable feelings go away. **The guilt is the second half of the binge, not the brake on it.**
>
> And this shows up consistently in the broader research: **people higher in self-compassion have less binge eating, less body dissatisfaction, and less drive for thinness** — in both clinical and non-clinical groups.
>
> **So when I say give yourself grace, I'm not being soft. I'm telling you the harsh version has been measured and it makes things worse.**

# ADD 3 — RIGID VERSUS FLEXIBLE

**Placement: after Add 2.**

**This is the distinction that protects your course**, and it's more useful than a general warning about restriction.

> #### How You Hold the Rules Matters More Than the Rules
>
> There's a distinction in this research that I want you to have, because it explains why the same plan works beautifully for one woman and falls apart for another.
>
> Researchers separate two ways of controlling what you eat.
>
> **Rigid control** is all-or-nothing. Foods are forbidden. Eating one produces guilt. The plan is either being followed perfectly or it's been ruined.
>
> **Flexible control** is paying real attention to what you eat without the all-or-nothing frame. Some foods are usually avoided and occasionally eaten, in limited amounts, **without it meaning anything about you.**
>
> **Rigid control is associated with a substantially higher tendency toward losing control of eating than flexible control is.**
>
> Read that carefully, because it's not what you'd expect. **It isn't the discipline that predicts the collapse. It's the rigidity.**
>
> #### Which Matters for Everything in This Book
>
> I've given you a lot of specifics. Lower your carbohydrates. Remove seed oils. Hit your protein. Stop snacking. **All of it is good advice and I stand behind it.**
>
> **And if you hold it rigidly, it can produce exactly the problem this chapter is about.**
>
> The woman who eats a cracker at a party, decides the week is ruined, and finishes the bowl — **her problem was never the cracker. It was the framework that made one cracker mean something.**
>
> **So hold this book firmly and loosely at the same time.** These are directions, not a test you can fail. **A meal that doesn't match the plan is information, not a verdict.**
>
> And if you notice yourself treating food as forbidden rather than as a choice — **that's worth more attention than another round of discipline.**

# ADD 4 — WHEN IT'S SOMETHING ELSE

**Placement: after the emotional triggers section, before "Progress Takes Time."**

Your chapter already says to work with a professional. **This names what for**, because the specific thing is treatable and a lot of women don't know it has a name.

> #### When It's Something More Than Emotional Eating
>
> I want to draw a line here, carefully, because these get lumped together and they're not the same thing.
>
> **Emotional eating** is reaching for food to manage a feeling. Uncomfortable, frustrating, and very common. Everything above applies.
>
> **But there's something different, and it deserves to be named.**
>
> If you find yourself eating an amount you'd be embarrassed for anyone to see. If it happens fast, and you feel like you cannot stop once it starts. If it's followed by real shame, and you hide it. If it happens with a kind of regularity you could almost predict.
>
> **That's binge eating, and it's a recognized condition — the most common eating disorder there is.**
>
> **It is also genuinely treatable.** There are specific therapies with strong evidence behind them, and people recover. **Not "manage" — recover.**
>
> **What I want you to know is that it isn't a willpower problem and it isn't a belief problem.** It's a condition, and the treatments that work for it aren't the ones in this chapter. **Reading a metabolism course harder is not going to touch it.**
>
> #### And One Honest Thing
>
> **If that's where you are, this course may not be the right thing for you right now** — and I'd rather tell you that than take your money and leave you working the wrong problem.
>
> A structured eating plan with rules about what to avoid can make binge eating considerably worse, for exactly the reason in the section above. **The restriction and the loss of control feed each other.**
>
> **Please talk to someone who specializes in it.** A doctor, or a therapist who works specifically with eating disorders. In the US, the **National Alliance for Eating Disorders** has a helpline staffed by clinicians.
>
> Everything in this book will still be here afterward. **And it'll work far better once this part is handled.**

# TWO SMALL REFINEMENTS

## The boredom trigger

Your text says boredom eating provides "a temporary distraction or dopamine hit." **True, and the precise version is more useful** — and it's the material from Chapter 14.

> **Boredom and Procrastination:** Eating because food provides a temporary distraction — and because of something specific about how wanting works.
>
> **The pull you feel toward food is not the same system as enjoying it.** Those come apart constantly, which is why you can want something intensely and then find, halfway through, that it isn't even good. **The wanting did its job. It was never about the pleasure.**
>
> Which is genuinely useful to know in the moment. **If you can ask "am I going to enjoy this, or do I just want it?" you've already interrupted something.**

## The substitute advice

Your three steps — notice it, give yourself grace, find a substitute — are sound. **Two additions make them more likely to work.**

> **4. Put time between the urge and the food.** Not forever. **Ten minutes.**
>
> Urges are not flat; they rise and fall. Most of them peak and start receding within about twenty minutes **if you don't act on them.** You're not resisting indefinitely — you're waiting for a wave to pass, and it does. Set a timer if it helps.
>
> **5. Change the cue, not just the response.**
>
> If it happens in the same chair, at the same time, watching the same programme, **the chair and the time and the programme are part of it.** Chapter 7 explained that your hunger hormone learns your patterns.
>
> So don't only substitute the behaviour — **interrupt the setup.** Sit somewhere else. Brush your teeth after dinner. Go outside for five minutes at the hour it usually happens. **It feels absurdly small and it works better than willpower does**, because you're removing the trigger rather than fighting the response.

# SOURCES

**Self-compassion (Add 2) — the key citation:**

1. **Adams CE, Leary MR.** Promoting self-compassionate attitudes toward eating among restrictive and guilty eaters. *J Soc Clin Psychol*. 2007;26(10):1120–1144.
   - Non-clinical college women, randomly assigned: **unhealthy food preload with self-compassion induction; preload without; no-preload control**
   - The preload simulated a diet break — participants ate unhealthy food (a doughnut) while watching television
   - **Restrained eaters given the self-compassion induction experienced less emotional distress and were less likely to overeat subsequently than controls**
   - The authors' own framing: self-compassion allows people to **"forgive themselves for an instance of diet-breaking without losing sight of their goals to regulate their eating"** — this directly answers the permission-to-give-up objection
   - **Described in the subsequent literature as the seminal study in this area. Obtain full text.**

2. **Braun TD, Park CL, Gorin A.** Self-compassion, body image, and disordered eating: a review of the literature. *Body Image*. 2016. — **the review to cite for the general association.**

3. **Higher self-compassion associated with less binge eating** in both clinical and non-clinical samples (Ferreira et al. 2013; Webb & Forman 2013), and with **less eating and weight concern, body dissatisfaction, drive for thinness, and dietary restraint** (Adams & Leary 2007; Ferreira 2014; Kelly 2014; Maraldo 2016; Wasylkiw 2012).

4. **Kelly AC, Carter JC (2015)** — a three-week compassion-focused guided self-help intervention with a structured eating plan produced **significant reduction in binge eating frequency** in patients with binge eating disorder. **Relevant to Add 4 — compassion-focused approaches have intervention-level evidence, not just correlational.**

**Rigid versus flexible restraint (Add 3):**

5. **Westenhoefer J, et al. (1999)** — distinguishes **rigid control** (strict all-or-none thinking, forbidden foods, guilt when eating them) from **flexible control** (conscious attention without all-or-none framing; unhealthy foods permitted in limited quantities without significant guilt). **Their data showed rigid control was associated with a higher tendency toward disinhibited eating than flexible control.** Cited within Adams & Leary. **Obtain the primary.**

6. **Restraint theory** — Herman & Polivy. Dietary restraint and disinhibited eating. **Pin.**

7. **Restrained eating, energy-dense food consumption, and self-compassion as moderator** — *BMC Psychology* 2025, doi:10.1186/s40359-025-03531-0, PMC12776997. Found that **restrictive eaters lower in compassionate action consume more energy-dense food than those higher in it.** Recent and directly supportive.

**Binge eating (Add 4):**

8. **Binge eating disorder is the most prevalent eating disorder.** **Verify the prevalence claim** before publishing.
9. **CBT-E and guided self-help** as first-line evidence-based treatments for BED. **Pin.**
10. **National Alliance for Eating Disorders** helpline — **and note deliberately: NEDA's helpline was permanently discontinued.** Do not direct readers there.
