# A1 — HOW MUCH PROTEIN

**Placement: replacing "secure a substantial portion of protein."**

> ## How Much Protein? Let's Be Specific
>
> I've told you protein is the foundation of every meal. That's useless without a number.
>
> ### The number
>
> **1.2 to 1.6 grams per kilogram per day.** For most women, toward the upper end.
>
> And here's something that will surprise you: **that's the current federal recommendation.**
>
> In January 2026, the Dietary Guidelines for Americans were updated — raising protein from the 0.8 grams per kilogram that had stood for **eighty-four years** to 1.2–1.6. For a 150-pound woman that's roughly **82 to 109 grams a day.** Roughly double.
>
> ### Where the old number came from
>
> The 0.8 figure was never meant to be a target.
>
> The researcher whose work produced it wrote, plainly, what it was: *"intended as a minimum value... the lowest level that will maintain nitrogen balance in nearly all healthy individuals."*
>
> **The floor below which you start visibly breaking down.** Not the amount to build muscle, or support a working thyroid, or keep a metabolism running for another fifty years.
>
> And it sat there for eighty-four years. **Three generations of women were handed the floor and told it was the goal.**
>
> What makes that stranger: **in the 1890s the USDA recommended over 110 grams a day.** Atwater — the same man whose calorie formula is still on your food labels — set his standard at 125. It took until 2026 to get back to a number we already had in 1900.
>
> ### Calculate it from your goal weight
>
> This matters and almost nobody gets it right.
>
> If you're carrying extra weight, your protein need is set by the **tissue that's metabolically active** — the muscle and organs you have and want to keep. **Fat tissue barely uses protein.** So using your current weight gives you a number that's too high.
>
> **Use the weight you're aiming for.** If you're already at a healthy weight with decent muscle, use your actual weight. If you lift seriously, go higher.
>
> ### Per meal, not just per day
>
> Muscle building isn't triggered by protein trickling in across the day. **It needs a large enough dose in one sitting to flip the switch.**
>
> Spread 60 grams thinly across three meals and you may miss the threshold at all three. Concentrate 90 grams into three real servings and you hit it three times.
>
> ### And the number goes *up* with age
>
> **This is the most important thing in this section and the least known.**
>
> As you get older, your muscles become **less responsive to the same amount of protein.** A portion that built muscle at 30 may not maintain it at 60.
>
> **Your protein requirement rises at exactly the age most women start eating less of it.** Appetite drops. Cooking for fewer people. Meat feels heavy. And the requirement goes the other way.
>
> **If you take one number from this entire book, take this one.**
>
> ### Can you eat too much?
>
> Yes, but it takes more than you'd think — and I'm raising it because I'm watching a lot of people overshoot.
>
> **Above roughly 2 grams per kilogram, more protein stops doing much** for anyone who isn't a competitive athlete. Beyond that you're just eating more protein.
>
> Some people eating all-meat diets are at **triple the target.** Two things happen. **Protein crowds out fat**, so total energy can quietly fall short. And **fasting blood sugar can rise** — very high protein with essentially no carbohydrate raises the hormone that tells your liver to release glucose. Readings of 100–110 are common in long-term carnivore eaters.
>
> **That's usually adaptive rather than diabetic** — your muscles are deliberately sparing glucose for your brain. **But read it alongside fasting insulin and HbA1c, never alone.** Chapter 3.
>
> **So what does "moderate protein" mean?** You'll hear keto described that way, and most people hear it as *not much.* It means **1.2 to 1.6 grams per kilogram of your goal weight** — considerably more than you're eating now, and nowhere near 300 grams.
>
> *One note: if you have reduced kidney function, protein intake is a genuine medical question. Higher protein hasn't been shown to harm healthy kidneys — but don't raise it substantially without talking to your doctor.*

# A2 — WHAT PROTEIN IS ACTUALLY FOR

**Placement: immediately after A1.**

> ## Protein Was Never About Muscle
>
> Now here's why protein matters far more than muscle.
>
> When you eat protein, your body takes it apart into **amino acids** — about twenty building blocks. Nine of them your body **cannot make.** They have to come from food, every day, or you start taking them out of yourself.
>
> ### Your thyroid hormone is made from protein
>
> Thyroid hormone — the thing that sets your metabolic rate, the subject of an entire chapter of this book — **is built from an amino acid called tyrosine, with iodine attached.**
>
> Not supported by protein. **Made of it.**
>
> ### So is the molecule that carries fat into your cells
>
> Remember the doorway from Chapter 8 — the one fat has to be carried through to be burned?
>
> **The carrier is called carnitine, and your body builds it from two amino acids** — using vitamin C to assemble it.
>
> **No carnitine, no fat through the door.** Your ability to burn fat depends on a molecule made from protein.
>
> ### And your master antioxidant
>
> Your cells' most important protective compound, **glutathione**, is built from three amino acids.
>
> This one matters more than it sounds. In Chapter 5 you learned that inflammation blocks your body from activating thyroid hormone. **The way it does that is by depleting glutathione.** So the raw materials for glutathione are also raw materials for a working thyroid.
>
> ### The short version
>
> Amino acids also build your **neurotransmitters** — tryptophan becomes serotonin, tyrosine becomes dopamine. They build **every enzyme in your body**, your immune cells, your collagen, and the protein part of every hormone you have.
>
> **Protein isn't the muscle macronutrient. It's the raw material for the machinery.**
>
> Which is why eating too little doesn't just cost you muscle. **It quietly limits your thyroid, your fat burning, your antioxidant defenses, and your ability to repair anything.**
>
> ---
>
> ## Not All Protein Contains the Same Things
>
> **Different foods contain different amino acids, in different amounts.** Twenty grams of protein from two sources is not the same twenty grams.
>
> **Animal proteins** — meat, fish, eggs, dairy — contain all nine essential amino acids in roughly the proportions your body needs. That's why they're called complete.
>
> **Plant proteins** are usually short on at least one. Grains tend to be low in lysine; beans tend to be low in methionine. Not a problem if you eat a variety — that's the reasoning behind rice and beans, and behind every traditional cuisine that pairs a grain with a legume. **But it means you need more total plant protein to get the same usable amount.**
>
> ### And one amino acid does the triggering
>
> Of the nine, one is the signal that tells your muscle to start building: **leucine.**
>
> This is why the *per meal* amount matters. **You need enough leucine in one sitting to flip the switch** — and leucine is concentrated in animal protein, whey, and eggs.
>
> Another reason 30 grams at each of three meals beats 90 at dinner.
>
> ---
>
> ## The One Almost Everyone Is Short On
>
> This is the part I most want you to know, because the fix is simple.
>
> **Glycine.**
>
> It's technically non-essential — your body can make it. **The problem is that it can't make enough.**
>
> In 2009, biochemists did the arithmetic on how much glycine the human body requires versus how much it can produce. **They found a shortfall of roughly 10 grams a day** — and showed it's *structural.* Not about eating badly or having a slow metabolism. **The pathway itself has a ceiling, and our needs exceed it.**
>
> They called it "a weak link in metabolism" and argued glycine should be reclassified as essential.
>
> ### Why you need so much
>
> **Glycine is the main ingredient in collagen** — it occupies every third position in the collagen molecule. And collagen is the most abundant protein in your body: roughly eight pounds of it, in your bones, cartilage, tendons, skin, and the framework holding every organ together.
>
> It's also required for **glutathione** (your master antioxidant — glycine appears to be the rate-limiting ingredient), **creatine**, **heme** (the iron-carrying part of your blood and the iron built into your energy chain from Chapter 4), **your liver's detoxification pathways**, and **sleep and temperature regulation.**
>
> ### And why modern diets run short
>
> **We stopped eating the parts of the animal that contain it.**
>
> Glycine is concentrated in skin, connective tissue, cartilage, tendon, and bone — the gelatinous parts. **Muscle meat, which is nearly all we eat now, is comparatively low in it.**
>
> Your great-grandmother made stock from bones. She ate the skin, the tougher cuts, the parts that turn gelatinous when they cook. **We eat chicken breast.**
>
> So we get plenty of the amino acids concentrated in muscle and very little of the one concentrated in everything else.
>
> ### There's also a balance problem
>
> Muscle meat is high in **methionine**, another essential amino acid. You need it.
>
> But in animal research, **a high-methionine, low-glycine pattern accelerates aging, while glycine appears to counterbalance it.**
>
> Modern eating produces exactly the wrong ratio: plenty of methionine from muscle meat and eggs, very little glycine because we skip the rest.
>
> **The answer isn't less meat. It's the rest of the animal.**
>
> ### What to do
>
> One of the easiest fixes in this book:
>
> **Bone broth or stock.** Real stock that gels when it cools — **that gelling *is* the collagen.**
>
> **Eat the gelatinous cuts.** Oxtail, shanks, short ribs, chuck, chicken thighs with the skin, anything slow-cooked with the bone in. **These cost less than the lean cuts** — a rare case where the better choice is cheaper.
>
> **Eat the skin.** On chicken, on fish.
>
> **Collagen or gelatin powder** if the cooking isn't realistic. It isn't a magic supplement — it's the part of the animal you stopped eating, in a jar.
>
> *A note on the research: the 10-gram shortfall is calculated rather than measured directly, and supplementation trials in healthy people are limited. But the calculation is sound, the biochemistry behind the ceiling is well understood, and the fix is food your ancestors ate routinely.*
>
> ---
>
> ## Putting Protein Together
>
> **Eat enough** — the amount from earlier in this chapter.
>
> **Mostly from animal sources**, because they contain all nine essentials in usable proportions and the most leucine.
>
> **Spread across meals**, so you hit the trigger more than once a day.
>
> **And eat more of the animal than the muscle.** Bones, skin, connective tissue, the slow-cooked cuts. **Whole animal, not just the lean parts** — same principle as whole food. The parts we discarded turned out to matter.

# A3 — THE FAT CELL DILEMMA, EXPANDED

**Placement: inside your Fat Cell Dilemma, after the hypertrophy explanation.**

> #### The Hormone That Goes the Other Way
>
> There's a second thing that happens when fat cells inflate, and it's easy to miss.
>
> Healthy fat tissue produces a hormone called **adiponectin** — one of the genuinely helpful ones. It improves insulin sensitivity, lowers inflammation, and encourages your cells to burn fat rather than store it.
>
> **Hypertrophied fat cells make less of it.** As they inflate, they produce **less adiponectin and more inflammatory signals** — and more leptin, the one your brain has already stopped hearing.
>
> So sick fat storage isn't passive. **The unhelpful signal goes up and the helpful one goes down, at the same time.**
>
> Deep abdominal fat produces even less adiponectin than fat under the skin — **which is part of why your waist measurement tells you more than the scale.** Worth remembering when the number won't move: a tape measure can see progress a scale can't, because **the scale can't tell the difference between losing fat and losing muscle.**

# A4 — WHY SEED OILS PUSH THE WRONG WAY

**Placement: after your existing seed oil paragraph.**

> #### How Exactly Does This Happen?
>
> Two ways, and the second is the one operating in you right now.
>
> **Linoleic acid converts in your body to arachidonic acid**, which directly drives fat tissue development through a specific signalling pathway. Researchers demonstrated it cleanly: mice whose mothers ate a linoleic-acid-rich diet were **40% heavier** shortly after weaning — and **the effect disappeared completely in mice bred without the receptor that pathway uses.**
>
> Same calories. Different fat. Different bodies.
>
> **The second route is the one that matters for you now. Omega-6 excess drives inflammation, and inflammation blocks the healthy kind of fat storage.**
>
> When researchers examined insulin-resistant people, their fat tissue had **lost much of its ability to make new fat cells** — with the specific genetic switches for that process turned down. The culprit was an inflammatory signal produced by their own fat tissue.
>
> So the sequence is: **seed oils feed inflammation. Inflammation shuts down your ability to make new small fat cells. And the energy has nowhere to go except into the cells you already have.** They inflate, become inflamed themselves, and the cycle tightens.
>
> **Remember this in Chapter 10.** The same inflammation doing this is what instructs your body to build insulin-blocking ceramides. **One problem wearing two costumes.**
>
> #### And the Encouraging Half
>
> **Omega-3 fats appear to do the reverse.** Studies show fish oil *reducing* fat cell size while *increasing* the formation of new small ones.
>
> This axis moves in both directions depending on what you eat.
>
> ---
>
> #### It's Not the Fat. It's What They Do to It.
>
> Let me be precise about what I'm asking you to avoid.
>
> **Your body needs omega-6. You'd get sick without it.** A handful of walnuts, some sunflower seeds, an avocado — that's omega-6 in the form nature made it: wrapped inside the food, protected from air and light by the vitamin E that came with it.
>
> **What happens in an industrial refinery is a different thing entirely.**
>
> The oil gets extracted. It's heated past 400 degrees to strip out the smell — **which also strips out the vitamin E protecting it.** Then it sits on a shelf under fluorescent lights for months. Then it goes into a restaurant fryer and is heated over and over for days.
>
> Fragile fat, stripped of its protection, heated repeatedly. **What comes out is not what went in.** It's full of broken-down fragments your body has to deal with — and those fragments drive exactly the inflammation we've been discussing.
>
> **So the rule isn't "avoid omega-6." It's this:**
>
> **Eat fat in the form it came in. Avoid fat that's been refined, bottled, and reheated.**
>
> Olive oil, butter, avocado, coconut, tallow, whole nuts and seeds — fine.
>
> Canola, soybean, corn, cottonseed, safflower, sunflower in a clear plastic bottle — and especially anything fried in a restaurant — not fine.

# A5 — YOUR FAT CELLS ARE A REVOLVING DOOR

**Placement: expanding "Why Your Body Might Burn Fuel Differently."**

This is the answer to the single most common frustration your readers have.

> #### Your Fat Cells Are Not a Storage Unit
>
> You probably picture fat storage like a warehouse. Energy comes in, gets locked away, stays there until you burn it off. **That isn't how it works at all.**
>
> **Your fat cells are breaking down and rebuilding fat constantly — right now, and while you sleep.**
>
> ### How stored fat is built
>
> Stored fat is called a **triglyceride**, and the name describes it: **three** fatty acids on **one** glycerol backbone. Think of the glycerol as a rack holding three items.
>
> To store fat, your body needs both the fatty acids and the rack.
>
> **And here's the surprising part: when a fat cell breaks a triglyceride apart, it cannot reuse its own rack.** Fat cells lack the enzyme needed to recycle glycerol. The rack gets released into your bloodstream and floats away.
>
> So every time a fat cell stores fat again, **it has to build a brand new rack from scratch.**
>
> ### Where the rack comes from
>
> **When you eat carbohydrate**, your fat cells build racks easily — glucose is the raw material and it's right there. **Carbohydrate makes storage easy.**
>
> **When you don't**, your fat cells build racks anyway — from lactate, from amino acids, from other available materials. The pathway was discovered in 1967.
>
> **And here's what should catch your attention: your body turns that pathway UP during fasting.** The gene for the key enzyme is actively switched on when food is scarce.
>
> **Your body has a dedicated system for rebuilding fat, and it runs hardest when you're not eating.**
>
> ### Which explains the most frustrating thing about fat loss
>
> In fasting humans, researchers have measured **as much as 60% of the fatty acids released from fat cells being put straight back into storage.**
>
> Released. Not burned. Repackaged.
>
> Scientists call this a **futile cycle**, and it literally is — **it costs your body six units of energy currency to reattach three fatty acids.** Your body spends energy to store fat it just released.
>
> **This is what's actually happening when people say they're in fat-burning mode and not losing fat.**
>
> You *are* releasing fat. Most of it is going back in. Whether your fat stores shrink depends on whether, across days and weeks, **more comes out than goes back** — not on whether the door is open.
>
> ### So what determines the direction?
>
> Two things, and they're both familiar.
>
> **How much fat is released** — mostly insulin. Lower insulin, more release. Chapters 2 and 8.
>
> **How much gets burned versus repackaged** — whether your tissues actually need the energy. **If you've eaten enough fat to meet your needs, your body has no reason to burn what it just released.** So it pays the cost and puts it back.
>
> **Which is the honest answer to a question that confuses a lot of people.** *If low-carb means low insulin, and low insulin means fat release, why am I not losing fat?*
>
> Because release isn't loss. **You've opened the door, and your body is walking the fat right back through it** — because you gave it enough dietary fat that it doesn't need yours.
>
> **So once you're fat-adapted, the lever is usually the added fats.** The butter in the coffee, the extra oil, the fat bombs. Useful during the transition. Frequently the reason nothing moves afterward.
>
> ### And one genuinely beautiful thing
>
> Remember the rack has to leave the fat cell because it can't be reused there?
>
> **It goes to your liver — and your liver turns it into glucose.**
>
> So there's a complete loop running in your body right now:
>
> **Stored fat breaks apart** → fatty acids and glycerol
> **Fatty acids** → burned for energy, or converted to ketones for your brain
> **Glycerol** → to the liver → **becomes the small amount of glucose your body still needs**
>
> That's how you maintain normal blood sugar on almost no dietary carbohydrate. **Part of it is being made from the backbone of your own fat.**
>
> ### One correction worth making
>
> You may have heard that you **can't** store fat without carbohydrate. **Partly right mechanically, wrong as an absolute.**
>
> Carbohydrate makes building the rack much easier, so storage is easier when it's around. **But your body can build the rack without it, and it will.**
>
> I'm telling you because I'd rather you hear it from me. **There are no loopholes in your metabolism** — only conditions that make one direction easier than the other. Lowering insulin makes release easier. That's real and valuable. **It isn't a door that locks behind you.**

# A6 — WHERE FAT IS STORED

**Placement: after A5.**

> #### Fat Isn't All Stored in One Place
>
> When we say "body fat," most people picture one thing. Your body keeps it in four places, and they behave completely differently.
>
> **In your fat cells** — what you see in the mirror.
>
> **Inside your muscle cells** — small droplets stored right next to the machinery that burns them. **This one is a fuel tank, not a storage unit.**
>
> **In your liver** — where it causes problems if it piles up. Chapter 11.
>
> **In your blood** — travelling to where it's needed. This is what a blood test measures.
>
> Four separate compartments. **More in one doesn't mean more in another.** And one of them is actually a sign of fitness.
>
> #### The Finding That Surprised Everyone
>
> Fat inside muscle was assumed to be bad. In sedentary people it tracks reliably with insulin resistance.
>
> Then researchers measured endurance athletes.
>
> **Athletes have as much fat inside their muscles as people with type 2 diabetes.** And they're among the most insulin-sensitive people you can find.
>
> Same fat. Same place. Opposite health. Scientists called it the **athlete's paradox**, and it took ten years to solve.
>
> #### And the Answer Changes How You Should Think About This
>
> **Stored fat inside a muscle cell is harmless.** It's a fuel tank.
>
> The damage happens when fatty acids get turned into *other* compounds instead — and trained muscle has far fewer of those. Chapter 10.
>
> **Then researchers found something that flips the intuition.** The faster someone was *packaging* fat into storage inside her muscle, the healthier her blood sugar was.
>
> **Storing fat safely is how your muscle protects itself.** A fatty acid parked as storage can't be turned into something harmful.
>
> #### So the Goal Was Never "Less Fat"
>
> **Stored fat isn't your enemy. It's your energy system.** What you want isn't less of it — it's **fat that moves.** In and out, stored where it belongs, used when needed.
>
> A trained woman has *more* fat inside her muscles than an untrained one and is metabolically healthier. **Because hers is cycling through instead of sitting there.**
>
> **It isn't how much you store. It's whether it moves.**
>
> *One thing so this doesn't confuse you: your* blood *triglycerides are a different pool, and lower really is better there. Exercise lowers those, because trained muscle gets much better at pulling fat out of your bloodstream and using it. Two pools, opposite directions, both good.*

# A7 — WHY PIZZA IS DIFFERENT

**Placement: replacing the mechanism inside your Randle Cycle section.** The full explanation moves to Chapter 4; this stays.

> #### Why Pizza Is Different
>
> There's a reason certain combinations feel impossible to stop eating, and it isn't a character weakness.
>
> **When both fuels arrive at once, in quantity, your cells congest.** Not because they can't use either one — because they can't commit to one while the other keeps arriving. The result is half-finished byproducts and cellular exhaust, and that exhaust interferes with insulin's signal directly. **Chapter 4 covers what's happening inside the cell.**
>
> **And nature almost never packages the two together.** Nothing that grows is both high in fat and high in refined starch at high density. Bread and butter. Cheese and crackers. Pastries. Chips. Ice cream. **That combination has to be manufactured.**
>
> There's a second reason those foods are hard to stop eating, and it has nothing to do with your cells. **When researchers put people in a scanner, foods combining fat and refined carbohydrate were valued more than the sum of their parts** — people worked harder and paid more for them, and it wasn't explained by calories or even by how much they said they liked the food.
>
> So it's engineered on two levels. **The combination overwhelms your cells, and it also overrides the signal that tells you to stop.**

# A8 — ALCOHOL

**Placement: new section.**

> #### Alcohol: The One Nobody Wants to Talk About
>
> This deserves its own section, because for a lot of women it's the single biggest obstacle and they have no idea why.
>
> **Your body treats alcohol as something to get rid of, not something to use.** It goes to the front of the queue — and **while your liver is clearing it, fat burning essentially stops.** Not slows. Pauses.
>
> So the problem isn't mainly the calories in the drink. **It's that two glasses of wine can shut down fat burning for hours** — during a window when everything else you did that day had you well positioned.
>
> Three more things stack on top:
>
> **It fragments your sleep** even when it helps you fall asleep — which feeds into the hunger hormones from Chapter 7 and the stress hormones in Chapter 13.
>
> **It reliably lowers restraint around food**, usually at night, when the pull is already strongest.
>
> **And the liver clearing it is the same liver** doing everything in Chapter 11 — including converting your thyroid hormone into its active form.
>
> **You don't have to quit.** But if your progress has stalled and there's a nightly drink in the picture, **that's the first thing I'd test — and the test is two weeks without, not one.** One week is mostly water weight.

# A9 — CARB BACK-LOADING: TWO DETAILS

**Placement: revising your carb back-loading section.**

> **This isn't just theory.** In a controlled study, people who ate a carbohydrate meal **four hours before bed fell asleep measurably faster** than those eating a lower-glycemic version. A later review found carbohydrate quantity and quality significantly affected how fast people fell asleep, how efficiently they slept, and how much deep and REM sleep they got.
>
> **Two details make the difference:**
>
> **Timing.** Four hours before bed worked. **One hour before bed did not.** So this means **dinner** — a real dinner, finished with time to spare. Not a bedtime snack.
>
> **Quality.** Use your safe starches: sweet potato, winter squash, plantain. **Not dessert.** High-sugar food late at night suppresses melatonin and fragments sleep, which is the opposite of what you're after.
>
> *You'll sometimes see this explained as carbohydrate boosting serotonin. That mechanism barely works once there's protein in the meal — and your dinner will have protein. The effect is real; that explanation isn't the reason.*

# A10 — ARTIFICIAL SWEETENERS

**Placement: short, after the carbohydrate section.**

> #### What About Artificial Sweeteners?
>
> **It is theorized that some sweeteners may trigger an insulin response in some people, just from the taste.** The research isn't settled — it seems to depend on the sweetener, the form, and the individual. Worth keeping in mind, because someone who responds strongly would be quietly keeping her fat-burning gates shut with something she believed was free.
>
> **But there's a second effect that's better documented and may matter more.**
>
> A 2025 study scanned the brains of 75 people after they drank sucralose, real sugar, or water. Sucralose **increased activity in the hunger center of the brain and increased their actual hunger by about 17%.** And it produced **no fullness hormones at all.** As the lead researcher put it: there's a sweetness signal, but no hormone signal telling you you're full.
>
> Effects were strongest in people already carrying extra weight. **And women showed bigger changes in that brain region than men.**
>
> **So the real problem isn't insulin. It's that you get the promise without the payoff, and your body responds by asking for more.**
>
> Which points at the actual answer, and it isn't a product. **The goal isn't finding a sweet thing that's safe. It's no longer needing the sweet thing.** Sweet cravings are largely learned and maintained by exposure — and they fade faster than most people expect. Try two weeks without and watch.

## B1 — Protein requirements

**[ESTABLISHED — and now official]** The **2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines for Americans**, released 7 January 2026, raised the recommendation from 0.8 g/kg/day to **1.2–1.6 g/kg/day.** The prior figure stood essentially unchanged since 1941.

**The 0.8 figure was explicitly a minimum.** Rose (1957): *"intended as a minimum value... the lowest level that will maintain nitrogen balance in nearly all healthy individuals."* IOM reaffirmed it in 2002 **with the caveat that it reflects the minimum to prevent deficiency, not optimal health.**

**Historical:** USDA recommended **over 110 g/day** in the 1890s; Atwater set 125 g/day; Voit recommended 118 g plus 3,000 kcal.

**[SUPPORTED] Protein is a lean-mass lever, not a fat-loss lever.** Krieger et al., *Am J Clin Nutr* 2006;83(2):260–274 (**PMID 16469983**), controlling for energy intake: protein >1.05 g/kg produced **+0.60 kg fat-free mass retention** (+1.21 kg beyond 12 weeks) with **no significant effect on body mass or fat mass loss.**

**[ESTABLISHED] Per-meal threshold** — muscle protein synthesis responds to per-meal dose in a threshold manner, driven substantially by leucine via mTORC1. Sub-threshold distribution can fail to trigger synthesis at any meal.

**[ESTABLISHED] Anabolic resistance** — older adults require a higher per-meal dose for equivalent synthesis. Reduced postprandial perfusion, impaired amino acid transport, blunted mTORC1 activation.

**>>> COACH RULES:**
- **1.2–1.6 g/kg/day of goal body weight**, upper end over 50
- **Always compute from goal or ideal body weight** in users with obesity — adipose tissue has low protein turnover
- Per meal: ~25–30 g under 50, ~35–40 g over 50
- Plateau around **2 g/kg** for non-athletes
- **Present as current federal guidance**, not a contrarian position. Newly true, and it changes how the recommendation lands
- **Never state what a user should weigh.** If she has a goal weight, use hers

**>>> ABSOLUTE GATES:** reported kidney disease or reduced eGFR → no target, route to clinician. Pregnancy or lactation → route. **Personal health situation flag or any disordered eating signal → no gram targets, no g/kg figures, no food-quantity examples.** Qualitative only.

## B2 — Amino acids and their uses

**[ESTABLISHED]** Nine essential amino acids. Specific metabolic uses relevant to this course:

- **Tyrosine → thyroid hormone.** T4 and T3 are iodinated tyrosine residues. **The hormone setting metabolic rate is constructed from an amino acid.** *(Lesson 5 layer.)*
- **Lysine + methionine → carnitine**, vitamin C–dependent. **Carnitine is required for CPT-1-mediated fat transport into mitochondria** *(Lesson 8 layer)*. **Fat oxidation capacity depends on protein and vitamin C adequacy.**
- **Glycine + cysteine + glutamate → glutathione.** **Glutathione depletion is the mechanism by which IL-6 impairs T4→T3 conversion** *(Lesson 5 layer)*.
- **Tryptophan → serotonin** *(Lesson 7 layer — and note the transport competition; corpus myth section)*. **Tyrosine → dopamine.**
- **Glycine + arginine + methionine → creatine.** **Glycine → heme** (iron transport, and the iron in the electron transport chain — Lesson 4 layer).
- **Leucine** is the primary trigger of muscle protein synthesis.

**Protein quality [ESTABLISHED]:** animal proteins contain all nine essentials in proportions approximating human requirements. Plant proteins typically have a limiting amino acid — lysine in cereals, methionine in legumes. **DIAAS** is the current quality scoring method.

**>>> COACH USE — the highest-value reframe in this layer:** protein is not the muscle macronutrient. **Thyroid hormone, the fat-transport carrier, and the master antioxidant are all built from amino acids.** Inadequate protein limits thyroid function, fat oxidation, and antioxidant defense — not only muscle.

**>>> Verify tyrosine/thyroid and carnitine biosynthesis before the coach states them as fact.** Both are textbook; pin references.

## B3 — Glycine

**[SUPPORTED — calculated, not directly measured]** **Meléndez-Hevia E, De Paz-Lugo P, Cornish-Bowden A, Cárdenas ML.** A weak link in metabolism: the metabolic capacity for glycine biosynthesis does not satisfy the need for collagen synthesis. *J Biosci*. 2009;34(6):853–872.

- **Shortfall of approximately 10 g/day for a 70 kg human** (other analyses cite 10–12 g)
- The constraint is **stoichiometric** — glycine synthesis via serine hydroxymethyltransferase cannot exceed consumption of one-carbon units, and the reaction is thermodynamically irreversible. **A structural ceiling, not a dietary failure**
- A 70 kg human contains roughly **3,700 g of collagen** (~1,600 g in bone)
- Authors argue glycine is semi-essential and should be a dietary requirement

**Uses:** collagen (glycine occupies every third position), glutathione (**dietary glycine appears rate-limiting** — McCarty, O'Keefe & DiNicolantonio, *Ochsner J* 2018;18(1):81–87), creatine, porphyrins/heme, purines, bile salts, hepatic conjugation and detoxification, neurotransmission.

**Dietary source pattern:** glycine is concentrated in skin, connective tissue, cartilage, tendon, and bone. **Muscle meat is comparatively low.** Modern consumption of predominantly muscle meat produces a high-methionine, low-glycine pattern.

**[HYPOTHESIS — animal data] Methionine/glycine balance and aging.** High methionine with low glycine associated with accelerated aging in animal models; glycine appears to counterbalance. **Present as animal data.**

**Recent review:** *Metabolism* 2025 — glycine as conditionally essential. **Note it recommends co-administration with L-serine in deficient states, and that trials in non-deficient people are lacking.**

**>>> COACH RULES:**
- Present the shortfall as **calculated from a well-understood pathway constraint**, not measured in individuals
- **Food first:** stock and bone broth that gels, gelatinous cuts, skin. Collagen or gelatin powder as a practical substitute
- **Do not recommend high-dose isolated glycine supplementation** — the recent review notes trials in non-deficient people are lacking and raises serine co-administration considerations
- Gated under disordered eating signals like all other intake guidance

## B4 — Hypertrophy vs hyperplasia

**[ESTABLISHED]** Adipose expands by hypertrophy (enlargement) or hyperplasia (new adipocytes from precursors). **Hypertrophy** promotes inflammation, hypoxia, macrophage recruitment, dysregulated adipokines, insulin resistance. **Hyperplasia** produces smaller adipocytes with better insulin sensitivity.

**Adipokine consequence** (PMC11980453): hypertrophic adipocytes show **decreased adiponectin** and **increased leptin**, MCP-1, IL-6.

**Review:** Ghaben AL, Scherer PE. *Nat Rev Mol Cell Biol*. 2019;20(4):242–258. Historical primary: Salans, Knittle & Hirsch, *J Clin Invest*. 1968;47(1):153–165.

**>>> COACH USE:** the most explanatory single idea in the course. Accounts for metabolically healthy obesity and metabolically unhealthy normal weight. **Deploy freely.** Connects to Lesson 7 (leptin, adiponectin) and B7 below.

## B5 — Seed oils and adipose development

**[SUPPORTED — strong mechanistic animal data; human causal data limited]**

**Direct adipogenic route.** Massiera et al., *J Lipid Res* 2003, **PMID 12576509**: arachidonic acid promoted preadipocyte differentiation substantially more than saturated, monounsaturated, or n-3 fats. Blocked by COX inhibitors → **prostacyclin receptor**, cAMP pathways regulating C/EBPβ and C/EBPδ. LA-rich maternal diet produced offspring with higher fat mass and **larger adipocytes at 8 weeks.**

Ailhaud, Guesnet & Cunnane: pups of LA-fed mothers **40% heavier** one week post-weaning; **effect abolished in prostacyclin-receptor-null mice.** *The strongest causal demonstration available.*

**Inflammatory route — the one operating in adults.** IL-6 impairs human subcutaneous adipogenesis in obesity-associated insulin resistance (PMC5506102, *Diabetologia*): insulin-resistant pre-adipocytes showed reduced adipogenic capacity with PPARγ and C/EBPα downregulated; **IL-6 treatment reduced insulin-sensitive cells to the insulin-resistant level.**

**Sequence:** n-6 excess → inflammation → impaired subcutaneous adipogenesis → energy partitioned into existing adipocytes → hypertrophy → further inflammation.

**Omega-3 counterpoint [SUPPORTED]:** fish oil reduced adipocyte volume and **increased** adipose cellularity (PMC7918199).

**>>> COACH FRAMING — the refined/unrefined distinction:**
- **Linoleic acid is essential.** Lead with that — requirements met at roughly 1–2% of energy, deficiency produces real disease
- The defensible target is **industrial processing**: high-heat deodorization (200–250°C) generating oxidation products and stripping endogenous vitamin E; repeated fryer heating; storage under light
- **4-hydroxynonenal** and related aldehydes from omega-6 peroxidation are the strongest specific evidence. **Verify before quoting**
- **Lead with heat and oxidation, not solvent residue.** Hexane residues are at the edge of detection and the argument becomes contested; the oxidation chemistry is measurable

**>>> CALIBRATION:** causal data is predominantly rodent and cell culture. **A well-supported mechanism, not a demonstrated human causal chain.** Do not claim a specific seed oil quantity produces a specific degree of hypertrophy.

## B6 — The triglyceride/fatty acid futile cycle

**[ESTABLISHED]** **Reshef L, Olswang Y, Cassuto H, et al.** Glyceroneogenesis and the triglyceride/fatty acid cycle. *J Biol Chem*. 2003;278(33):30413–30416.
- **"As much as 60% of the total fatty acids formed by lipolysis are re-esterified in fasting humans"**
- Explicitly a **"futile cycle"** — six ATP to activate three fatty acids
- **White adipose tissue has negligible glycerol kinase** — it cannot reuse released glycerol; brown adipose has considerable activity
- Rosiglitazone lowers plasma FFA substantially via PEPCK-C transcription and increased re-esterification

**Hanson RW, Reshef L.** Glyceroneogenesis revisited. *Biochimie*. 2003;85(12):1199–1205. **PMID 14739071.** Up to **65%** re-esterified. Pathway **active during fasting**; **PEPCK-C transcription induced in adipose tissue and liver during fasting.** Discovery: Ballard et al. and Reshef et al., 1967.

**Nye C, Kim J, Kalhan SC, Hanson RW.** **PMID 14641009** — G3P cannot come from glucose when glycolysis is reduced, nor from glycerol given negligible glycerol kinase; **glyceroneogenesis supplies it from pyruvate, lactate, or amino acids.**

**Franckhauser S, et al.** *Diabetes*. 2002. **PMID 11872659** — **PEPCK overexpression in adipose → increased re-esterification, larger adipocytes, greater fat mass, higher body weight, but DECREASED circulating FFAs, normal leptin, and fully preserved glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity.**

**>>> This last finding is the strongest support available for "it's not fat mass, it's where the fatty acids go."** Fatter mice, no insulin resistance, because fatty acids stayed contained.

**Glycerol → hepatic gluconeogenesis:** the liver has glycerol kinase and converts released glycerol to glucose. Standard biochemistry.

**>>> COACH USE — answers the single most common frustration:** positive ketones, good energy, no body composition change. **Release isn't loss.** The determinant is whether tissues need the energy; if dietary fat covers requirements, released fatty acids are repackaged at ATP cost. **The lever is added fats.**

**>>> Also the honest correction to a common overclaim:** "you can't store fat without carbohydrate" is mechanistically partial and practically false. Glyceroneogenesis supplies the backbone from non-carbohydrate precursors, **and is upregulated during fasting.**

## B7 — Where fat is stored, and the athlete's paradox

**[ESTABLISHED]** Four distinct triglyceride pools: adipose, intramuscular (IMTG), hepatic, and circulating. **Not interchangeable.**

**Goodpaster BH, He J, Watkins S, Kelley DE.** *J Clin Endocrinol Metab*. 2001;86(12):5755–5761 — endurance-trained athletes have IMTG **equivalent to individuals with type 2 diabetes** with very low diabetes risk. **The athlete's paradox.**

**Amati F, Dubé JJ, et al. (Goodpaster BH).** *Diabetes*. 2011;60(10):2588–2597. **PMID 21873552** — **trained muscle has lower DAGs and ceramides despite higher IMTGs.** **The resolution, and it's a ceramide finding — this is the Lesson 10 citation.**

**Bergman BC, et al.** *Am J Physiol Endocrinol Metab*. 2018;314:E152–E164 — **IMTG synthesis rate positively related to insulin sensitivity** and to **decreased C18:0 ceramide.** **Safe storage competes with ceramide formation for the same fatty acids.**

**Dubé JJ, et al.** **PMID 18319352** — moderate exercise training in **previously sedentary, overweight-to-obese, insulin-resistant older adults** increased IMCL, oxidative capacity, **and insulin sensitivity together.** **The practical citation — this population is the reader.**

*Science Advances* 2025, doi:10.1126/sciadv.adr8849 — athletes showed lower novel PKC activation despite higher DAG, pointing toward lipid **localization** rather than amount.

**>>> COACH USE:** "it isn't how much you store, it's whether it moves." Reframes the goal from elimination to turnover. **And manage the two-pool confusion explicitly** — blood triglycerides lower is better; muscle triglyceride with good turnover is fine. Lesson 3 and Lesson 9 are both correct.

## B8 — Dietary fat, insulin, and fat oxidation

**[ESTABLISHED]** Isolated fat produces minimal insulin response relative to carbohydrate. **However fat stimulates GIP**, which potentiates glucose-dependent insulin release — fat co-ingested with carbohydrate amplifies the response relative to carbohydrate alone. **Supports the course's own combination-food argument.**

**[SUPPORTED]** Recently ingested fat is preferentially oxidized over endogenous stores; individual variation in fat oxidation capacity is real with genetic contributions.

**>>> The published lesson states this more definitively than its cited sources support, and two of its five citations are non-peer-reviewed blogs.** Give the claim with hedging; **do not cite the lesson's source list.**

**[ESTABLISHED] Tissue specificity:** skeletal muscle and cardiac muscle oxidize fatty acids directly. **The heart derives the majority of its ATP from fatty acid oxidation.** Ketones exist for the brain *(Lesson 8 layer)*.

## B9 — Very high protein with very low carbohydrate

**[SUPPORTED]** Protein is a potent glucagon secretagogue. Under near-zero carbohydrate, the insulin:glucagon ratio shifts toward glucagon, raising hepatic glucose output. Cortisol adds a third gluconeogenic input.

**Result:** fasting glucose commonly 100–110 mg/dL in long-term carnivore or strict ketogenic eaters, frequently with **low fasting insulin and normal HbA1c.**

**[ESTABLISHED] Adaptive glucose sparing** — skeletal muscle downregulates insulin-stimulated glucose uptake under sustained carbohydrate restriction, preserving glucose for obligate tissues. **Distinct from pathological insulin resistance.**

**[ESTABLISHED] Impaired oral glucose tolerance** after prolonged carbohydrate restriction, **reversing within days** of reintroduction. A frequent cause of alarm and occasional misdiagnosis.

**>>> COACH RULE — CRITICAL ASYMMETRY.** Do **not** reassure a user about elevated fasting glucose unless **all** of: fasting insulin low, HbA1c normal and not trending up, no diabetes diagnosis.

**Route to physician** if fasting insulin is elevated or unknown, HbA1c is rising, there's any diabetes diagnosis or strong family history, or she reports polyuria, polydipsia, unexplained weight loss, or vision change.

**Secondary:** protein's satiety can crowd out total energy, producing inadvertent deficit *(Lesson 8 layer)*. **Ketone suppression from high protein is not a reason to restrict protein** — lean mass outweighs a strip reading.

## B10 — Food sequencing

**[ESTABLISHED]** Protein and non-starchy vegetables before carbohydrate within the same meal substantially reduces postprandial glucose and insulin excursions, with identical food content. Mechanisms: delayed gastric emptying, GLP-1 stimulation, reduced rate of carbohydrate delivery.

**>>> COACH USE — HIGHEST ACTION-RATE RECOMMENDATION IN THE COURSE.** No food change, no counting, immediate effect. **Appropriate even where numeric targets are suppressed.**

**>>> VERIFY: Shukla et al. is the standard reference and the published lesson cites nothing.** Pin before quoting effect sizes.

## B11 — Carbohydrate timing and sleep

**[SUPPORTED]** Afaghi A, O'Connor H, Chow CM. *Am J Clin Nutr*. 2007;85(2):426–430. **PMID 17284739.** 12 healthy men, isocaloric rice meals (GI 109 vs 50), 4 h before bed. **Significant shortening of sleep onset latency with high GI; effective at 4 h, not at 1 h.**

**Limitations:** n=12, young healthy men. **Polysomnography showed no differences in sleep architecture — only onset latency.** Meal was 90.4% carbohydrate, 8% protein.

**Meta-analytic support** (PMC8069918): glycemic load explained variance in sleep efficiency (R²=89.2, p<0.001) and wake after sleep onset.

**Counter-direction:** evening high-GI carbohydrate can suppress nocturnal melatonin.

**>>> COACH RULES:** 3–4 hours before bed, not at bedtime. Moderate-GI whole-food starches. **Do not use the tryptophan/serotonin mechanism as the explanation** — it requires a near-protein-free meal, and using it contradicts the course's own myth corrections.

**>>> Course inconsistency:** Lessons 15 and 16 advise 2–3 hours before bed; this research used 4. Give 3–4 hours when asked specifically about carbohydrate for sleep.

## B12 — Carbohydrate Safety Principle

**[SUPPORTED — and unusual in this genre]** Well aligned with the energy availability literature and the fasting cortisol data.

**>>> COACH USE — HIGH VALUE:** the correct framework for users presenting with cold intolerance, cycle disruption, hair loss, poor sleep, or "wired but tired" on low carbohydrate. **Reintroducing moderate safe starches is frequently the correct intervention.**

**>>> Subject to disordered eating gating.** A user expressing **fear** about reintroducing carbohydrate, or framing starches as forbidden, is a signal that takes precedence over any metabolic discussion.

## B13 — Alcohol

**[ESTABLISHED]** Ethanol is oxidized with metabolic priority. Hepatic fatty acid oxidation is markedly suppressed during ethanol metabolism via altered NADH/NAD⁺ redox state and acetate load. **Whole-body fat oxidation falls substantially for hours.**

Secondary: fragmented sleep architecture with reduced REM *(Lesson 7)*; reduced dietary restraint, typically evening *(Lesson 18)*; competition for hepatic capacity including T4→T3 conversion *(Lesson 5)*.

**>>> COACH USE:** high-yield first variable for a stalled user with regular intake. **Two-week trial, not one** — a single week is confounded by fluid shifts.

**>>> Do not lecture.** If she declines, move to other levers. **Never frame as a moral issue.** If her description suggests dependence, **no reduction protocol** — route to support.

## B14 — Non-nutritive sweeteners

**[CONTESTED on insulin; ESTABLISHED on appetite]**

**On insulin:** results vary by sweetener, form, and individual. Some studies find a cephalic response, most don't. **More research needed. Do not deny the possibility and do not assert it as established.**

**On appetite.** Page KA, et al. *Nature Metabolism*. 2025. **PMID 40140714.** 75 adults, randomized crossover:
- Sucralose vs sweetness-matched sucrose: increased hypothalamic blood flow (P<0.018), **greater hunger (P<0.001), ~17% increase**
- **No rise in satiety hormones**
- Increased hypothalamic connectivity with anterior cingulate cortex
- **Strongest in participants with obesity; females showed greater hypothalamic change than males**
- NIH/NIDDK funded, no competing interests
- **Limit:** hunger and brain activity measured; subsequent food intake was not

WHO 2023 advises against non-sugar sweeteners for weight control.

**>>> COACH USE:** lead with the appetite finding. **It explains what she has actually experienced.**

## COMMON USER QUESTIONS

**"How much protein do I actually need?"**
1.2–1.6 g/kg of **goal** weight, upper end over 50. **That's the current federal recommendation** as of January 2026, raised from 0.8 which had stood since 1941 and was never a target. **Most women eating what they consider plenty are at about half.** *Gated — qualitative only under flags; route kidney disease.*

**"Isn't that bad for my kidneys?"**
Higher protein **hasn't been shown to harm healthy kidneys** — that concern comes from studies of people who already had kidney disease. **If you have reduced kidney function, that's a medical question for your doctor.**

**"Won't too much protein kick me out of ketosis?"**
Takes far more than people think — in most people your liver makes what's needed rather than converting whatever arrives. **Where it becomes real:** very high intake with essentially zero carbohydrate raises glucagon and hepatic glucose output. Fasting glucose of 100–110 is common in long-term carnivore eaters. **Usually adaptive rather than diabetic — but read it alongside fasting insulin and HbA1c, never alone.** *Do not reassure without both known and favourable.*

**"Should I take collagen?"**
**Food first** — stock that gels when it cools, gelatinous cuts, chicken skin. Those cost less than lean cuts. Collagen or gelatin powder is a reasonable practical substitute. **The shortfall is real and calculated from a known pathway ceiling**, but it's a food gap, not a supplement requirement. *Do not recommend high-dose isolated glycine.*

**"Are all seed oils bad? What about nuts?"**
**No.** Your body needs omega-6 — you'd get sick without it. Walnuts, sunflower seeds, avocado are fine. **The problem is industrial processing:** extracted, heated past 400 degrees, stripped of the vitamin E protecting it, then reheated in fryers. **Rule: eat fat in the form it came in.**

**"My weight isn't moving but my clothes fit better."**
**That's the scale failing you, not your body.** It can't distinguish losing fat from losing muscle. Your waistband can. Deep abdominal fat is the most harmful kind and it responds first. **Measurements and fit beat weight**, along with the Chapter 3 signals.

**"I'm in ketosis / low-carb and not losing fat."**
Your fat cells release and repackage constantly — **up to 60% of released fatty acids get put straight back** in fasting people. **Release isn't loss.** The determinant is whether your body needs the energy. **Look at added fats** — butter in coffee, extra oil, fat bombs. *Qualitative under gating.*

**"Should I drink diet soda?"**
**Better than sugar. Not the same as water.** The insulin question is unsettled and may be real for some. **The better-documented problem:** sucralose increased hunger about 17% versus sugar with **no fullness hormones at all.** Strongest in people carrying extra weight, larger in women. **Try two weeks without.**

**"Do I have to give up wine?"**
**No — but if you're stalled and there's a nightly drink, test that first.** While your liver clears alcohol, fat burning essentially pauses. It also fragments sleep and lowers restraint at night. **Two weeks, not one.** *Do not lecture. If dependence is suggested, route to support rather than providing a plan.*

**"Can I eat carbs at night?"**
**For sleep and stress, dinner is the better time** — a controlled study found carbohydrate about four hours before bed shortened sleep onset. **Four hours worked; one hour didn't.** So dinner, not a bedtime snack. **Safe starches, not dessert.**

**"Why does this work for my friend and not me?"**
Individual variation in fat oxidation is real. **But before concluding you're the exception:** total protein, added fats, alcohol, sleep, actual total intake, and whether you're eating across a 15-hour window. **In practice it's almost always one of those.**

## EDGE CASES & GATING

**Kidney disease or reduced eGFR** → no protein target. Route.
**Pregnancy or lactation** → requirements differ. Route.
**Disordered eating signals, or fear of reintroducing carbohydrate** → no numeric targets of any kind. Escalate.
**Suggested alcohol dependence** → no reduction protocol. Route to support.
**Diabetes medication** → carbohydrate changes require prescriber involvement *(Lesson 8 — SGLT2 especially)*.
**Athletes** → higher protein needs; carbohydrate restriction has real high-intensity performance costs. Be honest.
**Over 65** → anabolic resistance more pronounced; under-eating risk higher.
**Elevated fasting glucose on very low carbohydrate** → **B9 applies. Do not reassure without insulin and HbA1c.**

**Safety rules:**
1. No protein targets with kidney disease, pregnancy, personal health flag, or disordered eating signal
2. Never state what a user should weigh
3. Never reassure about elevated fasting glucose without insulin and HbA1c favourable
4. Never claim a specific seed oil quantity causes a specific effect
5. No alcohol reduction protocol where dependence is suggested
6. Fear of carbohydrate reintroduction is a signal, not a preference
7. Do not cite the lesson's blog sources
8. Present seed oil causal data as animal and cell-culture based
9. Do not recommend high-dose isolated glycine supplementation
