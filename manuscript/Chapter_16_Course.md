# A1 — CONSISTENCY BEATS DURATION

**Placement: opening the sleep material, before the melatonin section.**

**The strongest addition to this chapter**, and it's better advice than what most readers have been told.

> #### The Thing That Matters More Than Eight Hours
>
> Everyone tells you to get seven to nine hours. That's reasonable advice and it's genuinely hard to follow, and it turns out **it may not even be the most important thing.**
>
> Researchers analyzed more than **ten million hours** of sleep data from **60,977 people** wearing accelerometers — average age 63, more than half of them women. They compared two things: **how much people slept, and how consistently they slept at the same times.**
>
> **Consistency won.**
>
> The people with the most regular sleep-wake timing had **20 to 48% lower risk of death from any cause** than the least regular — and **22 to 57% lower risk of death from cardiometabolic causes** specifically. Adjusted for age, sex, lifestyle, and health status.
>
> **And regularity predicted mortality better than duration did.**
>
> #### Why This Should Be a Relief
>
> If you've been lying awake doing arithmetic about whether you'll hit seven hours, **this is much better news than it sounds.**
>
> **Going to bed and getting up at roughly the same times — including weekends — appears to matter more than the exact number of hours.** And consistency is something you can actually control. You cannot force yourself to fall asleep. You can decide when you get up.
>
> **This is also why your body has clocks rather than a timer.** Chapter 15 covered the clocks in your liver, muscle, fat, and pancreas. **Clocks need a predictable signal to stay set.** An irregular schedule doesn't just leave you tired — it leaves every one of those tissue clocks guessing about what time it is.
>
> **So if you change one thing about your sleep: pick a wake-up time and keep it.** Even on Saturday. Especially on Saturday.

# A2 — SLEEPING WITH A LIGHT ON

**Placement: inside the Light Exposure section, after the morning light material.**

Striking, directly metabolic, and it happened in a single night.

> #### One Night With a Light On
>
> Here's a study that will make you get up and close your blinds.
>
> Researchers had healthy adults sleep for one night in a **dimly lit room** — under 3 lux, essentially dark. Another group slept in **moderate room light**, 100 lux. Not bright. Roughly a lamp left on in the corner, or the light coming in from a hallway.
>
> **One night.**
>
> In the room-light group, **heart rate rose overnight, heart rate variability dropped** — meaning their nervous system shifted toward stress mode while they slept — **and their insulin resistance was measurably higher the next morning.**
>
> **From a single night. With a lamp on.**
>
> #### And the Mechanism Isn't What You'd Guess
>
> Here's the detail that makes this interesting. **Their melatonin didn't change.** So this wasn't melatonin suppression.
>
> **It was their nervous system.** The light kept their sympathetic — fight-or-flight — system partly activated all night, and the researchers found that the more sympathetic activation someone had overnight, the higher her insulin the next morning.
>
> **Which connects straight to Chapter 12.** Your body is supposed to spend the night in repair mode, with the parasympathetic side in charge. **A light in the room prevents that from happening fully — and you have no idea it's happening, because you're asleep.**
>
> #### What to Do
>
> **Make your room actually dark.** Not dim. Dark.
>
> Blackout curtains if streetlight comes in. Cover or remove the glowing electronics. Move the charging phone out of the room. **If you get up at night, use the dimmest light that lets you see** — not the overhead.
>
> **And if you can't control the light, use a sleep mask.** It costs almost nothing and it solves the problem completely.
>
> This is one of the cheapest interventions in this entire book. **A single night in a lit room measurably changed how these people handled glucose.** Multiply that by every night for twenty years.

# A3 — WHY SITTING BY A WINDOW DOESN'T WORK

**Placement: in the Light Exposure section, with the morning light recommendation.**

Your advice is right. **This is the number that makes people actually do it.**

> #### Why You Have to Go Outside
>
> When I say get natural light in the morning, I mean **outside.** Not by a window. And there's a number that explains why.
>
> Light is measured in **lux.**
>
> **A well-lit indoor room: 100 to 500 lux.**
> **Sitting right next to a bright window: maybe 1,000.**
> **Outside on a completely overcast day: around 10,000.**
> **Outside in direct sun: up to 100,000.**
>
> **Read those again.** A grey, miserable, rainy morning outdoors delivers **ten to a hundred times more light** than your brightest indoor room.
>
> Your brain's master clock was built to read the difference between outdoors and indoors, sun and shade, day and night. **Indoor lighting is, to your circadian system, essentially darkness.** It's bright enough to see by and nowhere near bright enough to set a clock.
>
> Which is why **"I get plenty of light, I sit by the window"** doesn't work. And it's why the dullest possible overcast morning still does.
>
> #### What This Actually Requires
>
> **Five to ten minutes outside, within the first half hour of waking.** No sunglasses. You don't need to look at the sun — the light reaching your eyes from the sky is the point.
>
> On a grey day, give it fifteen or twenty. **You still get far more than you would indoors.**
>
> Drink your coffee on the step. Walk to the end of the street and back. Stand in the doorway while the dog sniffs around. **It doesn't have to be a practice. It has to be outside.**
>
> #### And the Same Number Explains the Evening Problem
>
> Now run it in reverse. **Your evening light is far brighter than your body expects.**
>
> For most of human history, the light after sunset was firelight — a handful of lux. **Now it's 100 to 300 in an ordinary lit room**, plus a screen at arm's length.
>
> **So it isn't only that screens are bad. It's that your entire evening looks, to your brain, like late afternoon.** Which is why melatonin arrives late and you don't feel tired until midnight.
>
> **Dim the overheads. Use lamps rather than ceiling lights. Let the last hour be genuinely dim.** You're not trying to sit in the dark — you're trying to make your evening look like evening.

# A4 — CAFFEINE

**Placement: new short section in the sleep material.**

Practical, extremely common, and most people have the timing wrong.

> #### Your Afternoon Coffee Is Still Working at Bedtime
>
> This one is simple and almost nobody gets it right.
>
> **Caffeine has a half-life of roughly five to six hours.** Which means that after five or six hours, **half of it is still in you.**
>
> So a 3 p.m. coffee means **half that dose is still circulating at 9 p.m.**, and a quarter of it at 3 a.m.
>
> #### And Here's the Part That Fools People
>
> **You can fall asleep fine and still have your sleep degraded.**
>
> Caffeine reduces deep sleep specifically — the stage where most of the repair happens. **So you sleep the hours, wake up unrefreshed, and conclude you need more coffee.** Which is a loop.
>
> **It also varies enormously between people**, largely genetic. Some people clear it in three hours and some take nine. **If you're a slow clearer, an afternoon coffee is a genuine problem and you may have no idea** — because you can still fall asleep.
>
> #### What to Do
>
> **Cut it off by early afternoon.** Noon if you're sensitive, 2 p.m. as a general rule.
>
> **And if you're convinced caffeine doesn't affect your sleep — test it for two weeks rather than assuming.** People who are certain it doesn't affect them are frequently the ones it affects most, because they've never known anything different.

# A5 — TEMPERATURE

**Placement: short, in the sleep material.**

> #### Cool Room, Warm Hands
>
> One more physical lever, and it's easy.
>
> **Falling asleep requires your core temperature to drop.** That drop is part of the signal. Which is why a warm room makes sleep harder and why you sleep badly in a heatwave.
>
> **Keep the room cool** — most people do best somewhere in the mid-60s Fahrenheit, cooler than feels intuitive.
>
> **And here's the trick that seems backwards:** a warm bath or shower an hour or two before bed helps you fall asleep *faster.* Not because it warms you, but because **warming your skin sends blood to the surface, and you then shed heat rapidly** — producing exactly the core temperature drop that initiates sleep.
>
> Warm the outside to cool the inside. Then a cool room to keep it there.
