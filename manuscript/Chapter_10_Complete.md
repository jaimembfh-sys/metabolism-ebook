# A1 — THE EXPERIMENT THAT PROVES IT

**Placement: after "How Are They Created?"** This is the strongest addition available to the chapter.

> #### The Experiment That Settled This
>
> Everything I just told you — that saturated fat is the building block and inflammation is the foreman — sounds like an interpretation. **It isn't. Researchers tested it directly, and the result is about as clean as biology gets.**
>
> Here's what they did.
>
> They fed mice a diet high in saturated fat. As expected, the mice became insulin resistant and their ceramide levels climbed.
>
> **Then they ran it again — this time blocking the enzyme that assembles ceramides.** Same saturated fat. Same amount. The only difference was that the assembly line was shut down.
>
> **The insulin resistance never developed.** The ceramides never accumulated.
>
> Same fat, no ceramides, no insulin resistance.
>
> **That's the whole argument, demonstrated.** Saturated fat doesn't cause insulin resistance. **Saturated fat plus the instruction to build ceramides causes insulin resistance.** Remove the instruction and the fat is harmless — it goes back to being fuel, which is what it was always meant to be.
>
> #### And the Foreman Has a Name
>
> I've been calling inflammation the "foreman." It's more specific than that.
>
> Your cells have a detector on their surface called **TLR4.** Its job is to sense danger — bacterial fragments, inflammatory signals, the kind of thing that means something is wrong. When TLR4 fires, it starts an inflammatory cascade, **and part of that cascade is the instruction to start building ceramides.**
>
> So the sequence is:
>
> **Inflammatory signal → TLR4 detects it → cascade begins → your cells start converting saturated fat into ceramides → ceramides block insulin.**
>
> Every step in that chain has been mapped. And the experiment above shows that **if you cut the chain at the ceramide step, the saturated fat at the front of it does no harm at all.**
>
> This is why the mainstream advice gets it backwards. **They're blaming the brick instead of the person who told the crew to build the wall.**

# A2 — THE PUZZLE THAT TOOK TEN YEARS TO SOLVE

**Placement: after A1.**

External validation. The field assumed something else, tested it, and the answer was ceramides.

> #### The Puzzle That Took Ten Years to Solve
>
> Here's another piece of evidence, and this one came from researchers who weren't looking for it.
>
> For years it was assumed that fat stored **inside muscle cells** caused insulin resistance. The correlation is strong and consistent: in sedentary people, more fat inside the muscle means worse blood sugar. It looked obvious.
>
> Then somebody measured endurance athletes.
>
> **Athletes have as much fat stored inside their muscles as people with type 2 diabetes.** And they are among the most insulin-sensitive people you can find, with very low diabetes risk.
>
> Same fat. Same place. Same tissue. **Opposite metabolic health.** It became known as the athlete's paradox, and it broke the theory.
>
> It took a decade to resolve. **And when the answer came, it was ceramides.**
>
> When researchers finally measured them, trained muscle turned out to have **fewer ceramides — despite holding more fat.**
>
> **The stored fat was never the problem. It was never the problem in anyone.** What separates a healthy muscle from a sick one isn't how much fat is in it. It's whether that fat is sitting there as fuel or being converted into ceramides.
>
> #### And This Is Where It Gets Useful
>
> There's a second finding that turns this into something you can act on.
>
> Researchers measured how fast people were **packaging fat into storage** inside their muscle cells — literally how quickly they were parking it away as fuel.
>
> **The faster they did it, the more insulin-sensitive they were. And the fewer ceramides they had.**
>
> **Safe storage and ceramide production are competing destinations for the same fatty acid.** Every one that gets parked away as fuel is one that didn't become a ceramide.
>
> So what decides which way a fatty acid goes? **Whether your muscle is equipped to handle it** — enough mitochondria to burn it, enough capacity to store it properly.
>
> Both of which are built by training. Which brings us to something missing from most advice about this.

**Placement: add to your "How to Get Rid of Them" list.**

Your list currently has three items: lower insulin, eliminate seed oils, reduce toxin exposure. **Exercise belongs on it, and arguably at the top.**

> #### Move Your Body — The Most Direct Lever You Have
>
> **This is the one most people leave off the list, and it may be the most effective.**
>

>

>
> And you don't need to be an athlete to get this. **The study that demonstrated it used people who had never trained before.**

> #### Your Body Has a Ceramide Eraser — And Sick Fat Cells Stop Making It

>
> **Adiponectin's main job appears to be destroying ceramides.**
>

>
> **That's the loop that makes insulin resistance so self-reinforcing.** And it's why the answer is never one intervention — you're working on both halves of the same problem.
>
> The good news: **everything that shrinks sick fat cells raises adiponectin.** Lowering insulin, reducing inflammation, losing visceral fat, and exercise. Same list as always, now with one more reason.

# A5 — ONE PRECISION FIX

**Placement: revising your "Eliminate Seed Oils" bullet.**

Your recommendation is right. The reasoning needs adjusting, and the corrected version is more interesting.

**What the research shows:** in the same experiment from A1, the mice were tested on two different fats. Blocking ceramide assembly prevented the saturated-fat damage completely — **but it had no effect at all on the damage from soy oil.** Seed oils caused insulin resistance through a different pathway that doesn't need ceramides.

> **Eliminate Seed Oils.** Industrial seed oils drive insulin resistance too — but through a **different route.**
>
> In that same experiment, blocking ceramide production protected the animals from saturated fat completely. **It did nothing to protect them from soy oil.** The seed oil damaged insulin signalling by another mechanism entirely.
>
> So seed oils aren't a ceramide problem. They're their own problem — and that means the two fats damage you through two separate doors. **Which is worse news, not better.** Removing one pathway doesn't protect you from the other.
>
> Seed oils also feed the general inflammation that turns the ceramide foreman on in the first place, and they drive the fat-cell inflammation from Chapter 9. **So they hurt you in at least three ways.** Remove them.

## B1 — Ceramide synthesis and the rate-limiting step

**[ESTABLISHED]** De novo ceramide synthesis begins with condensation of **L-serine and palmitoyl-CoA** by **serine palmitoyltransferase (SPT)** — the first committed and rate-limiting step. **Palmitoyl-CoA is a highly specific substrate for SPT**, which is why palmitic acid is the relevant saturated fat.

Downstream: 3-ketodihydrosphingosine → dihydrosphingosine → dihydroceramide (ceramide synthases CERS1–6) → ceramide (dihydroceramide desaturase).

**Two other routes exist:** sphingomyelin hydrolysis via sphingomyelinase, and the salvage pathway.

**SPT is upregulated by:**
- **TLR4 activation** (bacterial LPS, saturated fatty acids)
- **TNF-α** and other inflammatory cytokines
- **Oxidative stress**
- **ER stress** — Sptlc1 and Sptlc2 upregulated via spliced XBP1; liver-specific Sptlc2 transgenic mice showed elevated hepatic ceramide, elevated fasting glucose, and **reduced insulin receptor β phosphorylation** (PMC9166747)

**>>> THIS IS THE COURSE'S "FOREMAN" CLAIM WITH A NAMED ENZYME AND NAMED INPUTS.** The lesson's framing is mechanistically correct: palmitate is substrate; the inflammatory signal is what upregulates the assembling enzyme.

## B2 — The SPT inhibition experiment — the key citation

**[ESTABLISHED]** **Holland WL, et al.** Lipid-induced insulin resistance mediated by the proinflammatory receptor TLR4 requires saturated fatty acid–induced ceramide biosynthesis in mice. *J Clin Invest*. 2011;121(5). Open access at jci.org/articles/view/43378.

Design: mice infused with **lard oil** (high saturated fat) or **soy oil**, with or without SPT inhibitors (**myriocin** or **cycloserine**).

Findings:
- **Both inhibitors prevented lard-induced insulin resistance and ceramide accumulation**
- **Neither had any effect on soy-induced insulin resistance**
- **Neither reduced DAG levels**
- Authors' conclusion: ceramide synthesis is **essential** for lard-induced insulin resistance and **dispensable** for soy-oil-induced insulin resistance. **Different fatty acids induce insulin resistance via distinct mechanisms, discernible by their reliance on ceramide.**

**>>> THE STRONGEST SINGLE CITATION FOR THIS CHAPTER.** Same saturated fat, ceramide assembly blocked, insulin resistance prevented. **"Innocent bystanders" demonstrated rather than asserted.**

**>>> AND THE PRECISION POINT THE COURSE CURRENTLY MISSES:** seed oil–induced insulin resistance does **not** run through ceramides. The published Lesson 10 lists "eliminate seed oils" as a ceramide intervention. **The recommendation is right; the mechanism is different.** Seed oils act via other lipid intermediates and via general inflammatory load.

**>>> COACH FRAMING:** two fats, two doors. Removing one pathway doesn't protect against the other. That's a stronger argument for removing seed oils, not a weaker one.

## B3 — Adiponectin as ceramidase activator

**[ESTABLISHED]** **Holland WL, Summers SA, Scherer PE** and colleagues demonstrated that **adiponectin receptors (AdipoR1/2) activate cellular ceramidase activity**, degrading ceramide to sphingosine and subsequently sphingosine-1-phosphate.

Reviewed in **Summers SA, Holland WL.** Ceramides as modulators of cellular and whole-body metabolism. *J Clin Invest*. 2011;121(11). jci.org/articles/view/57144.

Key points from that review:
- The AMPK-centric theory of adiponectin action is challenged — **LKB1, required for AMPK activation, is dispensable for adiponectin's effects**
- Instead, **adiponectin "exerts its broad spectrum of actions by degrading ceramide via ceramidase activation"**
- **Tissue ceramide levels are regulated by hormonal signals modulating both synthesis and degradation** — inflammatory agonists drive production; adiponectin drives clearance
- The same TLR4-activating stimuli also induce **hypothalamic** insulin resistance, with ceramide identified as a likely mediator

**>>> THE LOOP — connects Lesson 9 and Lesson 10:**

Hypertrophied adipocytes secrete **less adiponectin** and **more inflammatory cytokines** (PMC11980453, and the recent *Front Endocrinol* 2026 review confirming decreased adiponectin in adipose dysfunction).

**So sick fat cells simultaneously increase ceramide production and decrease ceramide clearance.** Production up, disposal down. **This is the mechanistic basis for why insulin resistance is self-reinforcing.**

**>>> COACH USE — HIGH VALUE:** explains why single interventions underperform and why the same list of actions (lower insulin, reduce inflammation, reduce visceral fat, exercise) appears in every chapter. They all raise adiponectin.

**>>> Do not recommend adiponectin testing** — not a routine clinical test and no validated action follows.

## B4 — The athlete's paradox as external validation

**[ESTABLISHED]** **Goodpaster BH, He J, Watkins S, Kelley DE.** *J Clin Endocrinol Metab*. 2001;86(12):5755–5761 — endurance-trained athletes have intramuscular triglyceride **equivalent to individuals with type 2 diabetes**, with very low diabetes risk and high insulin sensitivity.

**Amati F, Dubé JJ, et al. (Goodpaster BH).** Skeletal muscle triglycerides, diacylglycerols, and ceramides in insulin resistance: another paradox in endurance-trained athletes? *Diabetes*. 2011;60(10):2588–2597. **PMID 21873552.** — **trained muscle characterized by lower DAGs and ceramides despite higher IMTGs.**

**Bergman BC, et al.** *Am J Physiol Endocrinol Metab*. 2018;314:E152–E164 — IMTG synthesis rate **positively related to insulin sensitivity** and to **decreased accumulation of C18:0 ceramide and glucosylceramide.**

**Dubé JJ, et al.** **PMID 18319352** — moderate exercise training in **previously sedentary, overweight-to-obese, insulin-resistant older adults** increased intramyocellular lipid, oxidative capacity, **and insulin sensitivity together.**

*Science Advances* 2025, doi:10.1126/sciadv.adr8849 — athletes showed lower novel PKC activation despite higher DAG, pointing toward **lipid localization** (plasma membrane vs mitochondria) rather than amount alone.

**>>> COACH USE:** the field assumed intramuscular fat itself caused insulin resistance, tested it, and the explanation turned out to be ceramides and DAGs. **That's external confirmation of the lesson's thesis from researchers who weren't arguing for it.**

**>>> The Bergman finding is the actionable half:** safe triglyceride storage and ceramide formation compete for the same fatty acids. **Exercise lowers ceramides by improving partitioning, not by burning fat off.**

**>>> Use Dubé for the population point** — sedentary, overweight, insulin-resistant, older. That's the reader.

## B5 — Ceramides vs DAGs — the honest position

**[CONTESTED — both arms are real, and the field has not fully settled which dominates]**

Two lipid intermediate families are implicated in lipid-induced insulin resistance:

**Diacylglycerols (DAGs)** — Shulman's group's principal mechanism. DAG activates novel PKC isoforms, impairing insulin receptor signalling and GLUT4 translocation. Central to the revision of the Randle hypothesis *(Lesson 1 and 8 layers)*.

**Ceramides** — Summers, Holland, Scherer, Bikman. Ceramides impair Akt activation, partly via PP2A and PKCζ.

**Notably, in the Holland 2011 experiment, SPT inhibition did not reduce DAG levels** — yet it prevented lard-induced insulin resistance. **So in that model ceramides were the operative mediator independent of DAG.** In the soy oil arm, insulin resistance proceeded without ceramide involvement, implicating other intermediates.

**>>> COACH RULE:** the lesson focuses on ceramides and that focus is defensible and well sourced. **Do not claim ceramides are the sole mechanism of insulin resistance.** Defensible framing: ceramides are one of the principal lipid intermediates, they're the one most clearly tied to saturated fat plus inflammation, and they're the one with a clear clearance mechanism (adiponectin).

**>>> If a user raises DAG or Shulman's work**, affirm both — they're parallel arms of lipotoxicity, not competing theories, and different dietary fats appear to work through different ones.

## B6 — What ceramides do at the cellular level

**[ESTABLISHED]** The lesson's "biological roadblocks" framing is accurate at the reader level. Mechanistically: ceramides impair insulin signalling primarily downstream of the receptor, through **inhibition of Akt/PKB activation** — via protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A) mediated dephosphorylation and via PKCζ. Consequences include impaired GLUT4 translocation and reduced glycogen synthesis.

Ceramides also promote **mitochondrial dysfunction, ER stress, apoptosis** (including pancreatic beta-cell apoptosis), and **hypothalamic insulin resistance** (Summers & Holland 2011).

**>>> COACH USE:** the roadblock metaphor is fine. If a user wants more, the accurate statement is that ceramides block the signal *inside* the cell, downstream of the receptor — so the receptor can be working perfectly and the message still doesn't get through.

**>>> Note the hypothalamic finding** — connects to Lesson 7 (leptin resistance and hypothalamic inflammation). Same inflammatory drivers, same brain region.

## COMMON USER QUESTIONS

**"So should I eat saturated fat or not?"**

**Saturated fat isn't the villain, and it also isn't irrelevant.** It's the building block your cells use when the instruction to build ceramides arrives — but without that instruction it's stable, useful fuel.

The order matters. **Remove the inflammatory drivers first** — processed food, seed oils, excess refined carbohydrate, toxin exposure. Then the fat in your butter and beef is what it was meant to be.

**What I wouldn't do is fear it while leaving the fire burning.** That's the version of this advice that helps nobody.

**"How do I know if I have a ceramide problem?"**

**There's no practical test you should be chasing.** Plasma ceramide panels exist at some centres but they aren't standard, and no specific treatment follows from the result.

**What you can measure is the thing ceramides cause:** insulin resistance. **Fasting insulin** and your **triglyceride-to-HDL ratio** from Chapter 3 tell you what you need to know, and they're cheap.

**>>> Do not recommend ceramide testing.**

**"Does exercise lower ceramides?"**

**Yes, and it's probably the most direct lever you have** — which is why I'd put it at the top of the list.

It doesn't work by burning fat off. **It works by giving fatty acids somewhere better to go.** Trained muscle parks fat as fuel and burns through it, instead of letting it be converted into ceramides.

And this isn't just an athlete thing. **When researchers trained previously sedentary, overweight, insulin-resistant older adults, their insulin sensitivity improved even as the fat inside their muscles went up.** Resistance training and walking after meals are the two highest-yield versions.

**"Is this Bikman's theory or is it accepted science?"**

**It's established science that Bikman is a prominent researcher in.** The ceramide-insulin resistance link is published across major journals and multiple independent groups — Summers, Holland, Scherer, and others.

**The cleanest demonstration:** researchers fed mice saturated fat, blocked the enzyme that assembles ceramides, and **the insulin resistance never developed.** Same fat, no ceramides, no damage.

**What's still debated** is how much of insulin resistance is ceramides versus other related lipid molecules. Both are real. Ceramides are the one most clearly tied to saturated fat plus inflammation, and the one with a known clearance mechanism.

**"If ceramides come from saturated fat, isn't a keto diet dangerous?"**

**Reasonable question, and the answer is in the mechanism.** Ceramide production requires the *instruction*, not just the material. A low-carbohydrate diet lowers insulin, reduces the fat-cell inflammation that generates the signal, and removes the refined carbohydrate and seed oils that drive it.

**So you're reducing the foreman while supplying more bricks.** In practice, well-formulated low-carb diets improve the markers of insulin resistance rather than worsening them.

**Where I'd be more careful:** someone eating high saturated fat *alongside* refined carbohydrate, processed food, and seed oils has both the material and the instruction. **That's the worst configuration, and it's the standard Western diet.**

**"What about DAG / I read it was diacylglycerols, not ceramides?"**

**Both are real and they're not competing theories.** They're two arms of the same problem — lipid intermediates accumulating inside cells and interfering with insulin signalling.

Interestingly, **different dietary fats appear to work through different ones.** In the experiment above, blocking ceramide production protected against saturated fat but did nothing against soy oil — which damaged insulin signalling another way.

**"Do I need supplements to lower ceramides?"**

No, and I'd be skeptical of anything marketed that way. **The levers are the ones in this chapter:** lower insulin, remove seed oils and processed food, reduce toxin exposure, exercise, and reduce visceral fat.

**Your body also makes its own ceramide eraser** — adiponectin — and everything on that list raises it.
