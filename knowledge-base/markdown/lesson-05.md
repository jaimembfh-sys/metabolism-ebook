# Thyroid, Nervous System, and Energy Output

<!-- chunk -->
## Overview
<!-- page: Lesson 5 -->

Stress and nervous system health directly impact thyroid function

While your mitochondria determine how energy is produced, your thyroid and nervous system determine how much of that energy is actually used.

<!-- chunk -->
## The Thyroid: Your Metabolic Accelerator
<!-- page: Lesson 5 -->

The thyroid acts as a kind of metabolic accelerator. It regulates the speed at which energy is burned and how much heat the body produces. When thyroid function is optimal, the body maintains a steady metabolic rate, supports normal body temperature, and uses energy efficiently. When thyroid function is reduced, the entire system slows down to conserve fuel. Body temperature may drop, and fat loss becomes incredibly difficult regardless of your diet or exercise routine.

Thyroid function is heavily influenced by nutrient availability, liver function, gut health, and overall stress levels. The body produces thyroid hormone in an inactive form (T4) that must be converted into its active form (T3) in the liver and gut. If this conversion process is impaired—often due to chronic stress, inflammation, or nutrient deficiencies—your metabolism can slow down even when your baseline thyroid levels appear "normal" on standard blood tests.

<!-- chunk -->
## So What Should You Ask For?
<!-- page: Lesson 5 -->

If the problem can be in the conversion rather than the gland, then a test that only checks the gland won't find it.

Most doctors order TSH alone. TSH is your brain's signal to your thyroid — it tells you whether the gland is being asked to work harder. It tells you nothing about whether the hormone is being activated once it's made.

A full picture needs four things:

TSH — the signal from your brain to the gland.

Free T4 — the inactive hormone your thyroid actually produces.

Free T3 — the active hormone your cells can use. This is the one most often missing, and the one that determines how you feel.

Thyroid antibodies (TPO and thyroglobulin) — these show whether your immune system is attacking the gland, which is the most common cause of low thyroid function by a wide margin.

If you can only get one thing added: free T3.

You can have a perfect TSH, a normal free T4, and a low free T3. That combination is exactly what this chapter describes — a gland working fine and a conversion step that isn't. And on a TSH-only panel, it's invisible.

Expect some pushback, and know how to handle it. Many physicians consider TSH sufficient, and for detecting outright gland failure they're right. You're asking a different question. It helps enormously to ask it that way — "I'd like to know whether I'm converting properly, can we look at free T3 and antibodies" lands very differently than arriving with a printout from the internet.

And if your results come back and you're told they're normal while you still feel unwell — you're probably not imagining it. You may have had the wrong test, or you may be inside a reference range that isn't the same thing as optimal. That's a legitimate conversation to have with a practitioner who works in that gap.

## The Thing Most People Get Wrong — Including a Lot of Doctors
<!-- page: Lesson 5 -->

Hashimoto's isn't really a thyroid disease. It's an immune system problem that happens to be attacking the thyroid.

That distinction changes everything about where you look.

Your thyroid isn't broken. It's under attack. So the question isn't how to fix the gland — it's why your immune system turned on it. And the answers are usually in your gut, your inflammatory load, and whatever chronic burden your body is fighting. Lessons 10, 11, and 13.

This also explains something that frustrates a great many women: why so many people on thyroid medication still don't feel well. The medication replaces the hormone. It does nothing about the attack.

And it's by far the most common cause. In countries with enough iodine in the food supply, most low thyroid function is autoimmune. It's far more common in women than men, and it often shows up in the forties and fifties — exactly when everything else in this book becomes relevant.

One more thing worth knowing: antibodies often appear years before your TSH moves. A woman with real symptoms, a normal TSH, and positive antibodies isn't imagining things. She's early.

One practical note on iodine. Your thyroid needs iodine. It's the raw material — you cannot make thyroid hormone without it, and deficiency is still a genuine problem in much of the world. But if your immune system is already attacking the gland, a large iodine supplement can add fuel to the fire rather than help. Iodine from food is not the concern. Seafood, seaweed in reasonable amounts, iodized salt — eat them. Before taking a high-dose iodine supplement, just get a thyroid antibody test. One blood draw. It tells you whether you're feeding a gland or feeding a fire. This applies to the iodine recommendation in Lesson 15 as well.

## What Happens to Your Thyroid on Low-Carb
<!-- page: Lesson 5 -->

This book spends a lot of time on lowering carbohydrates. So you should get this part from me rather than finding it somewhere else.

When people cut carbohydrates, T3 reliably goes down. This has been known since the 1970s. And here's the striking part: it's specifically about carbohydrate, not about eating less.

Researchers in 1976 showed it clearly. Total fasting dropped T3 by about half. A no-carbohydrate diet at the same calories dropped it almost as much. But the same people eating the same calories with at least 50 grams of carbohydrate showed no significant change at all.

Same calories. Different carbohydrate. Different thyroid.

Now Here's What Changes the Interpretation

In genuine low thyroid function, your brain notices the shortage and shouts louder at the gland. That shout is what TSH measures.

On low-carb, TSH usually stays completely normal. Your brain isn't sounding an alarm. Free T4 often stays normal too — sometimes it even rises.

So one reasonable reading is this: your body isn't struggling to make thyroid hormone. It's choosing to activate less of it — because running on fat is a different arrangement and may genuinely require less.

And I want to be honest that this is an interpretation, not a settled fact. The same pattern — low T3 with normal TSH — shows up in chronic illness, where it is definitely not harmless. What separates the two isn't the number on the page.

The Rule: Symptoms Decide

If your T3 comes back low and you feel well — good energy, warm hands, hair staying put, normal cycle — this is very likely adaptation. Don't chase a number.

If your T3 is low and you feel worse — that needs addressing, not tolerating.

And the timing tells you which one you're in.

The first two weeks of low-carb feel rough for almost everyone. That's the transition, and it resolves. If you still feel bad at week six, or you only started feeling bad at week six, that isn't adaptation.

Don't wait to notice. Some of these show up late or get blamed on something else:

Cold hands and feet, or feeling cold when nobody else does

Hair shedding — which lags about three months behind the cause, so almost nobody connects it

Your cycle changing in length or regularity

Fatigue that isn't improving

If those appear, the answer is usually not more restriction. It's adding safe starches back — the ones from Lesson 9 — and eating more overall.

Which brings us to the reason most women miss this entirely.

## Accidentally Eating Too Little
<!-- page: Lesson 5 -->

Low-carb eating suppresses appetite. That's one of its best features — it's why people stop thinking about food constantly.

It's also the trap.

When you're genuinely not hungry, you don't eat. Not restricting. Not white-knuckling anything. Just... not hungry, so lunch gets skipped, and the day ends with two meals that didn't have much in them.

Do that for a few weeks and your body concludes food is scarce. Thyroid output drops. Your cycle changes. Your hands get cold. Hair starts thinning. Progress stalls — and then stalls harder, because the natural response is to cut further.

There's also a sex difference here that matters. In one study of a single day of fasting, the women's stress hormone rhythm shifted measurably — running higher and peaking earlier. In the men, nothing moved at all.

That's one study and it needs replicating. But it points the same direction as everything else in this chapter: women appear more responsive to the stress of restriction, and most low-carb and fasting protocols were built on research in men.

What to Do About It

For your first month, don't trust your appetite as a guide to how much to eat. Eat your protein on a schedule whether you want it or not.

And if you feel worse rather than better after the first couple of weeks — eat more. That is almost always the correction, and it is almost always the last thing anyone tries.

You'll see this called "adrenal fatigue" in a lot of places. The underlying stress physiology is real, but that particular label doesn't hold up to scrutiny, and I'd rather give you the version that does: chronic stress and under-eating both suppress thyroid output, and both respond to being addressed directly.

When the body shifts into a parasympathetic state, digestion improves, nutrient absorption increases, hormones find their natural balance, and the body becomes willing to burn energy rather than hoarding it.

## The Nervous System: The Safety Switch
<!-- page: Lesson 5 -->

At the same time, the nervous system acts as the ultimate safety switch, determining whether the body feels secure enough to use energy freely.

When the body is in a sympathetic state (often described as fight-or-flight), it prepares for immediate survival. Blood sugar is elevated to provide quick fuel, digestion is dramatically slowed, and the body becomes focused entirely on conserving long-term resources. If you live in a state of chronic stress, you are constantly sending these sympathetic signals. This leads to consistently elevated cortisol levels, increased fat storage, disrupted sleep, and severely reduced metabolic efficiency.

When the body shifts into a parasympathetic state (often referred to as rest-and-digest), the opposite occurs. Digestion improves, nutrient absorption increases, hormones find their natural balance, and the body becomes willing to burn energy rather than hoarding it. This shift is physiological, not just psychological. The state of your nervous system directly influences how your body processes the food on your plate.

## Energy Demand and Heat Production
<!-- page: Lesson 5 -->

Another critical factor that ties the thyroid and nervous system together is energy demand. Muscle tissue increases your baseline energy demand because it requires constant maintenance. Furthermore, daily movement—even outside of structured gym workouts—significantly contributes to your total energy expenditure. Small, consistent movements like walking, standing, and general daily activity can collectively have a greater impact on your metabolism than a single, short period of intense exercise.

This has a name — non-exercise activity thermogenesis, or NEAT. It covers everything you do that isn't deliberate exercise: walking, standing, carrying things, fidgeting, gesturing when you talk.

For most people it's a considerably larger share of daily energy use than their workouts. And here's the part that matters:

It's the first thing your body cuts when it decides resources are short.

You don't decide to move less. You just find yourself sitting more, taking the elevator, driving instead of walking the four blocks — and you don't notice, because the decision was never conscious.

This is one of the main ways restriction backfires without showing up anywhere you'd think to look. You cut your food, your body cut your movement, and the deficit you thought you created never actually existed.

The reverse is also available to you. Building more movement into ordinary life reaches a much larger pool of energy than adding another gym session — which is why Lesson 17 is about more than workouts.

## Brown Fat, Cold Exposure, and Thermogenesis
<!-- page: Lesson 5 -->

The body also has a unique ability to increase energy expenditure through heat production, a process known as thermogenesis. You possess certain types of fat tissue, referred to as "brown fat," which are specialized entirely for this purpose. Unlike regular white fat, which simply stores excess energy, brown fat actively burns energy to generate heat.

Brown fat is packed with mitochondria—the cellular engines we discussed earlier. These mitochondria contain a unique protein called UCP1 (Uncoupling Protein 1), which literally "uncouples" the calorie-burning process from creating usable energy (ATP) and instead forces the cell to just generate pure heat. When your brown fat is activated, your metabolic rate increases significantly without you having to move a muscle.

The most potent natural stimulator of brown fat is cold exposure. When the skin senses a drop in temperature, the brain sends a signal via the sympathetic nervous system to activate brown fat to keep your core temperature stable. Over time, regular cold exposure can actually increase the amount of brown fat your body has, progressively raising your resting metabolic rate.

<!-- chunk -->
## Practical Methods
<!-- page: Lesson 5 -->

The Cold Shower Finish: The simplest way to start is by turning the water to cold at the very end of your normal shower. Start with 15 to 30 seconds and gradually work your way up to 1 to 2 minutes as you build resilience.

Cold Plunges: For a more intense stimulus, full-body immersion in cold water (typically between 50°F and 60°F) for 2 to 5 minutes is highly effective.

Winter Walks: Going for a brisk 10-to-15-minute walk outside in colder weather wearing only a t-shirt (ensuring you are safe, of course) provides a strong, natural environmental stimulus.

<!-- chunk -->
## A Note for Women
<!-- page: Lesson 5 -->

While cold exposure is a powerful metabolic tool, it must be applied with nuance. Because the female body is uniquely designed to protect reproduction and hormonal balance, it is often more sensitive to physical stressors than the male body.

Intense, prolonged cold exposure (like daily, extended ice baths) acts as a significant acute stressor. If your nervous system is already heavily burdened by a busy lifestyle, poor sleep, or intense exercise, extreme cold can drive cortisol too high and potentially disrupt thyroid function or the menstrual cycle.

The key is "minimum effective dose." You often only need a brief, mild cold stimulus. Pay close attention: cold exposure should leave you feeling energized and warm within a few minutes. If it leaves you feeling exhausted, deeply chilled for hours, or overly stressed, you are doing too much.
