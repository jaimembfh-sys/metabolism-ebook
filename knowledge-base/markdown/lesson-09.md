# Balancing Your Plate

<!-- chunk -->
## Overview
<!-- page: Lesson 9 -->

Ancestral whole foods form the foundation of a balanced plate

When you understand that your body operates on hormonal signals rather than just mathematical equations of energy, the way you look at a plate of food fundamentally changes. You are no longer asking, "How much energy does this contain?" You are asking, "What signal is this sending to my body?"

To create an environment where your metabolism thrives, your blood sugar remains stable, and your body can effortlessly burn stored energy, you must structure your meals strategically.

<!-- chunk -->
## The Foundation: Protein
<!-- page: Lesson 9 -->

Protein should be the undisputed foundation of every meal you eat. It is the most essential macronutrient for human survival and metabolic health. When you consume high-quality protein (like beef, eggs, poultry, and fish), it provides the amino acids necessary to maintain and build muscle tissue. Because muscle is the primary engine of your metabolism, protecting it is your highest priority.

Furthermore, protein is highly satiating. It triggers the release of fullness hormones (like GLP-1 and PYY) while simultaneously requiring a significant amount of internal energy just to digest and process it. This is known as the thermic effect of food.

## How Much Protein? Let's Be Specific
<!-- page: Lesson 9 -->

I've told you protein is the foundation of every meal. That's useless without a number.

The number

1.2 to 1.6 grams per kilogram per day. For most women, toward the upper end.

And here's something that will surprise you: that's the current federal recommendation.

In January 2026, the Dietary Guidelines for Americans were updated — raising protein from the 0.8 grams per kilogram that had stood for eighty-four years to 1.2–1.6. For a 150-pound woman that's roughly 82 to 109 grams a day. Roughly double.

Where the old number came from

The 0.8 figure was never meant to be a target.

The researcher whose work produced it wrote, plainly, what it was: "intended as a minimum value... the lowest level that will maintain nitrogen balance in nearly all healthy individuals."

The floor below which you start visibly breaking down. Not the amount to build muscle, or support a working thyroid, or keep a metabolism running for another fifty years.

And it sat there for eighty-four years. Three generations of women were handed the floor and told it was the goal.

What makes that stranger: in the 1890s the USDA recommended over 110 grams a day. Atwater — the same man whose calorie formula is still on your food labels — set his standard at 125. It took until 2026 to get back to a number we already had in 1900.

Calculate it from your goal weight

This matters and almost nobody gets it right.

If you're carrying extra weight, your protein need is set by the tissue that's metabolically active — the muscle and organs you have and want to keep. Fat tissue barely uses protein. So using your current weight gives you a number that's too high.

Use the weight you're aiming for. If you're already at a healthy weight with decent muscle, use your actual weight. If you lift seriously, go higher.

Per meal, not just per day

Muscle building isn't triggered by protein trickling in across the day. It needs a large enough dose in one sitting to flip the switch.

Spread 60 grams thinly across three meals and you may miss the threshold at all three. Concentrate 90 grams into three real servings and you hit it three times.

And the number goes up with age

This is the most important thing in this section and the least known.

As you get older, your muscles become less responsive to the same amount of protein. A portion that built muscle at 30 may not maintain it at 60.

Your protein requirement rises at exactly the age most women start eating less of it. Appetite drops. Cooking for fewer people. Meat feels heavy. And the requirement goes the other way.

If you take one number from this entire book, take this one.

Can you eat too much?

Yes, but it takes more than you'd think — and I'm raising it because I'm watching a lot of people overshoot.

Above roughly 2 grams per kilogram, more protein stops doing much for anyone who isn't a competitive athlete. Beyond that you're just eating more protein.

Some people eating all-meat diets are at triple the target. Two things happen. Protein crowds out fat, so total energy can quietly fall short. And fasting blood sugar can rise — very high protein with essentially no carbohydrate raises the hormone that tells your liver to release glucose. Readings of 100–110 are common in long-term carnivore eaters.

That's usually adaptive rather than diabetic — your muscles are deliberately sparing glucose for your brain. But read it alongside fasting insulin and HbA1c, never alone. Lesson 3.

So what does "moderate protein" mean? You'll hear keto described that way, and most people hear it as not much. It means 1.2 to 1.6 grams per kilogram of your goal weight — considerably more than you're eating now, and nowhere near 300 grams.

One note: if you have reduced kidney function, protein intake is a genuine medical question. Higher protein hasn't been shown to harm healthy kidneys — but don't raise it substantially without talking to your doctor.

## Protein Was Never About Muscle
<!-- page: Lesson 9 -->

Now here's why protein matters far more than muscle.

When you eat protein, your body takes it apart into amino acids — about twenty building blocks. Nine of them your body cannot make. They have to come from food, every day, or you start taking them out of yourself.

Your thyroid hormone is made from protein

Thyroid hormone — the thing that sets your metabolic rate, the subject of an entire chapter of this book — is built from an amino acid called tyrosine, with iodine attached.

Not supported by protein. Made of it.

So is the molecule that carries fat into your cells

Remember the doorway from Lesson 8 — the one fat has to be carried through to be burned?

The carrier is called carnitine, and your body builds it from two amino acids — using vitamin C to assemble it.

No carnitine, no fat through the door. Your ability to burn fat depends on a molecule made from protein.

And your master antioxidant

Your cells' most important protective compound, glutathione, is built from three amino acids.

This one matters more than it sounds. In Lesson 5 you learned that inflammation blocks your body from activating thyroid hormone. The way it does that is by depleting glutathione. So the raw materials for glutathione are also raw materials for a working thyroid.

The short version

Amino acids also build your neurotransmitters — tryptophan becomes serotonin, tyrosine becomes dopamine. They build every enzyme in your body, your immune cells, your collagen, and the protein part of every hormone you have.

Protein isn't the muscle macronutrient. It's the raw material for the machinery.

Which is why eating too little doesn't just cost you muscle. It quietly limits your thyroid, your fat burning, your antioxidant defenses, and your ability to repair anything.

## Not All Protein Contains the Same Things
<!-- page: Lesson 9 -->

Different foods contain different amino acids, in different amounts. Twenty grams of protein from two sources is not the same twenty grams.

Animal proteins — meat, fish, eggs, dairy — contain all nine essential amino acids in roughly the proportions your body needs. That's why they're called complete.

Plant proteins are usually short on at least one. Grains tend to be low in lysine; beans tend to be low in methionine. Not a problem if you eat a variety — that's the reasoning behind rice and beans, and behind every traditional cuisine that pairs a grain with a legume. But it means you need more total plant protein to get the same usable amount.

And one amino acid does the triggering

Of the nine, one is the signal that tells your muscle to start building: leucine.

This is why the per meal amount matters. You need enough leucine in one sitting to flip the switch — and leucine is concentrated in animal protein, whey, and eggs.

Another reason 30 grams at each of three meals beats 90 at dinner.

## The One Almost Everyone Is Short On
<!-- page: Lesson 9 -->

This is the part I most want you to know, because the fix is simple.

Glycine.

It's technically non-essential — your body can make it. The problem is that it can't make enough.

In 2009, biochemists did the arithmetic on how much glycine the human body requires versus how much it can produce. They found a shortfall of roughly 10 grams a day — and showed it's structural. Not about eating badly or having a slow metabolism. The pathway itself has a ceiling, and our needs exceed it.

They called it "a weak link in metabolism" and argued glycine should be reclassified as essential.

Why you need so much

Glycine is the main ingredient in collagen — it occupies every third position in the collagen molecule. And collagen is the most abundant protein in your body: roughly eight pounds of it, in your bones, cartilage, tendons, skin, and the framework holding every organ together.

It's also required for glutathione (your master antioxidant — glycine appears to be the rate-limiting ingredient), creatine, heme (the iron-carrying part of your blood and the iron built into your energy chain from Lesson 4), your liver's detoxification pathways, and sleep and temperature regulation.

And why modern diets run short

We stopped eating the parts of the animal that contain it.

Glycine is concentrated in skin, connective tissue, cartilage, tendon, and bone — the gelatinous parts. Muscle meat, which is nearly all we eat now, is comparatively low in it.

Your great-grandmother made stock from bones. She ate the skin, the tougher cuts, the parts that turn gelatinous when they cook. We eat chicken breast.

So we get plenty of the amino acids concentrated in muscle and very little of the one concentrated in everything else.

There's also a balance problem

Muscle meat is high in methionine, another essential amino acid. You need it.

But in animal research, a high-methionine, low-glycine pattern accelerates aging, while glycine appears to counterbalance it.

Modern eating produces exactly the wrong ratio: plenty of methionine from muscle meat and eggs, very little glycine because we skip the rest.

The answer isn't less meat. It's the rest of the animal.

What to do

One of the easiest fixes in this book:

Bone broth or stock. Real stock that gels when it cools — that gelling is the collagen.

Eat the gelatinous cuts. Oxtail, shanks, short ribs, chuck, chicken thighs with the skin, anything slow-cooked with the bone in. These cost less than the lean cuts — a rare case where the better choice is cheaper.

Eat the skin. On chicken, on fish.

Collagen or gelatin powder if the cooking isn't realistic. It isn't a magic supplement — it's the part of the animal you stopped eating, in a jar.

A note on the research: the 10-gram shortfall is calculated rather than measured directly, and supplementation trials in healthy people are limited. But the calculation is sound, the biochemistry behind the ceiling is well understood, and the fix is food your ancestors ate routinely.

## Putting Protein Together
<!-- page: Lesson 9 -->

Eat enough — the amount from earlier in this chapter.

Mostly from animal sources, because they contain all nine essentials in usable proportions and the most leucine.

Spread across meals, so you hit the trigger more than once a day.

And eat more of the animal than the muscle. Bones, skin, connective tissue, the slow-cooked cuts. Whole animal, not just the lean parts — same principle as whole food. The parts we discarded turned out to matter.

## The Fuel: Healthy Fats and the Fat Cell Dilemma
<!-- page: Lesson 9 -->

For decades, we were falsely taught that eating fat makes you fat. Biologically, this makes no sense. Fat is the only macronutrient that has essentially zero impact on insulin. When you eat fat, your body receives a signal of abundant fuel without the storage-triggering spike of insulin.

However, the type of fat you eat dictates how your fat cells behave. To understand this, you must understand why some heavy people are metabolically healthy, while some thin people develop type 2 diabetes. It all comes down to how your body stores energy:

Hyperplasia (Healthy Storage)

When energy is abundant, some bodies naturally multiply their fat cells, creating brand new, small, healthy fat cells to store the energy. This keeps the person highly insulin sensitive, even if they carry a lot of extra weight.

Hypertrophy (Sick Storage)

Other people have a genetic limit on the number of fat cells they can make. Instead of multiplying, their existing fat cells simply inflate like balloons. When a single fat cell gets too big (hypertrophied), it becomes sick, heavily inflamed, and highly insulin resistant. It begins leaking fatty acids into the bloodstream. This is why a physically thin person can suddenly develop severe metabolic disease—their limited fat cells reached maximum capacity and became sick.

<!-- chunk -->
## The Hormone That Goes the Other Way
<!-- page: Lesson 9 -->

There's a second thing that happens when fat cells inflate, and it's easy to miss.

Healthy fat tissue produces a hormone called adiponectin — one of the genuinely helpful ones. It improves insulin sensitivity, lowers inflammation, and encourages your cells to burn fat rather than store it.

Hypertrophied fat cells make less of it. As they inflate, they produce less adiponectin and more inflammatory signals — and more leptin, the one your brain has already stopped hearing.

So sick fat storage isn't passive. The unhelpful signal goes up and the helpful one goes down, at the same time.

Deep abdominal fat produces even less adiponectin than fat under the skin — which is part of why your waist measurement tells you more than the scale. Worth remembering when the number won't move: a tape measure can see progress a scale can't, because the scale can't tell the difference between losing fat and losing muscle.

Advanced metabolic research highlights a massive problem in the modern diet: the overconsumption of industrial seed oils (like soybean, canola, corn, and sunflower oils). These oils are highly unstable and packed with linoleic acid. When consumed in excess, linoleic acid forces your body into hypertrophy. Instead of allowing your fat cells to multiply safely, seed oils force your existing fat cells to rapidly inflate, become inflamed, and drive systemic insulin resistance.

To support your metabolism, completely remove industrial seed oils from your diet. Replace them with ancestral, stable fats: butter, ghee, tallow, coconut oil, olive oil, and the natural fats found in whole cuts of meat and eggs. These fats support hormone production, keep your brain healthy, and provide deep, long-lasting energy.

## How Exactly Does This Happen?
<!-- page: Lesson 9 -->

Two ways, and the second is the one operating in you right now.

Linoleic acid converts in your body to arachidonic acid, which directly drives fat tissue development through a specific signalling pathway. Researchers demonstrated it cleanly: mice whose mothers ate a linoleic-acid-rich diet were 40% heavier shortly after weaning — and the effect disappeared completely in mice bred without the receptor that pathway uses.

Same calories. Different fat. Different bodies.

The second route is the one that matters for you now. Omega-6 excess drives inflammation, and inflammation blocks the healthy kind of fat storage.

When researchers examined insulin-resistant people, their fat tissue had lost much of its ability to make new fat cells — with the specific genetic switches for that process turned down. The culprit was an inflammatory signal produced by their own fat tissue.

So the sequence is: seed oils feed inflammation. Inflammation shuts down your ability to make new small fat cells. And the energy has nowhere to go except into the cells you already have. They inflate, become inflamed themselves, and the cycle tightens.

Remember this in Lesson 10. The same inflammation doing this is what instructs your body to build insulin-blocking ceramides. One problem wearing two costumes.

And the Encouraging Half

Omega-3 fats appear to do the reverse. Studies show fish oil reducing fat cell size while increasing the formation of new small ones.

This axis moves in both directions depending on what you eat.

It's Not the Fat. It's What They Do to It.

Let me be precise about what I'm asking you to avoid.

Your body needs omega-6. You'd get sick without it. A handful of walnuts, some sunflower seeds, an avocado — that's omega-6 in the form nature made it: wrapped inside the food, protected from air and light by the vitamin E that came with it.

What happens in an industrial refinery is a different thing entirely.

The oil gets extracted. It's heated past 400 degrees to strip out the smell — which also strips out the vitamin E protecting it. Then it sits on a shelf under fluorescent lights for months. Then it goes into a restaurant fryer and is heated over and over for days.

Fragile fat, stripped of its protection, heated repeatedly. What comes out is not what went in. It's full of broken-down fragments your body has to deal with — and those fragments drive exactly the inflammation we've been discussing.

So the rule isn't "avoid omega-6." It's this:

Eat fat in the form it came in. Avoid fat that's been refined, bottled, and reheated.

Olive oil, butter, avocado, coconut, tallow, whole nuts and seeds — fine.

Canola, soybean, corn, cottonseed, safflower, sunflower in a clear plastic bottle — and especially anything fried in a restaurant — not fine.

By applying the Fiber First rule — eating your fibrous vegetables and high-quality protein before your carbohydrates — you completely change the biological response to that exact same meal.

## The Lever: Carbohydrates and "Metabolic Safety"
<!-- page: Lesson 9 -->

If protein is the foundation and fat is the primary fuel, carbohydrates should be viewed as a lever that you pull based on your activity level and metabolic health. For a healthy individual looking to burn fat, pulling the carbohydrate lever low is highly effective.

However, for individuals with stressed adrenals, chronic illness, or those who feel constantly "wired but tired," dropping carbohydrates too low sends a massive famine signal to the brain, spiking adrenaline and cortisol. In these cases, we must use the Carbohydrate Safety Principle.

Instead of strict elimination, the goal is to incorporate "safe starches"—like sweet potatoes, plantains, winter squash, and whole fruits. These easy-to-digest carbohydrates provide a steady supply of glucose, which actively inhibits the breakdown of muscle tissue, lowers stress hormones like adrenaline, and supports healthy thyroid function.

Carb Back-Loading for Sleep and Stress

If you struggle with high stress and poor sleep, strategic carbohydrate timing is vital. Cortisol should naturally be highest in the morning and lowest at night. Eating a high-protein, low-carb breakfast supports this natural morning energy. However, incorporating a moderate portion of safe starches with your dinner helps actively lower evening cortisol and supports the production of serotonin and melatonin, easing the nervous system into deep, restorative sleep.

This isn't just theory. In a controlled study, people who ate a carbohydrate meal four hours before bed fell asleep measurably faster than those eating a lower-glycemic version. A later review found carbohydrate quantity and quality significantly affected how fast people fell asleep, how efficiently they slept, and how much deep and REM sleep they got.

Two details make the difference:

Timing. Four hours before bed worked. One hour before bed did not. So this means dinner — a real dinner, finished with time to spare. Not a bedtime snack.

Quality. Use your safe starches: sweet potato, winter squash, plantain. Not dessert. High-sugar food late at night suppresses melatonin and fragments sleep, which is the opposite of what you're after.

You'll sometimes see this explained as carbohydrate boosting serotonin. That mechanism barely works once there's protein in the meal — and your dinner will have protein. The effect is real; that explanation isn't the reason.

## What About Artificial Sweeteners?
<!-- page: Lesson 9 -->

It is theorized that some sweeteners may trigger an insulin response in some people, just from the taste. The research isn't settled — it seems to depend on the sweetener, the form, and the individual. Worth keeping in mind, because someone who responds strongly would be quietly keeping her fat-burning gates shut with something she believed was free.

But there's a second effect that's better documented and may matter more.

A 2025 study scanned the brains of 75 people after they drank sucralose, real sugar, or water. Sucralose increased activity in the hunger center of the brain and increased their actual hunger by about 17%. And it produced no fullness hormones at all. As the lead researcher put it: there's a sweetness signal, but no hormone signal telling you you're full.

Effects were strongest in people already carrying extra weight. And women showed bigger changes in that brain region than men.

So the real problem isn't insulin. It's that you get the promise without the payoff, and your body responds by asking for more.

Which points at the actual answer, and it isn't a product. The goal isn't finding a sweet thing that's safe. It's no longer needing the sweet thing. Sweet cravings are largely learned and maintained by exposure — and they fade faster than most people expect. Try two weeks without and watch.

## Alcohol: The One Nobody Wants to Talk About
<!-- page: Lesson 9 -->

This deserves its own section, because for a lot of women it's the single biggest obstacle and they have no idea why.

Your body treats alcohol as something to get rid of, not something to use. It goes to the front of the queue — and while your liver is clearing it, fat burning essentially stops. Not slows. Pauses.

So the problem isn't mainly the calories in the drink. It's that two glasses of wine can shut down fat burning for hours — during a window when everything else you did that day had you well positioned.

Three more things stack on top:

It fragments your sleep even when it helps you fall asleep — which feeds into the hunger hormones from Lesson 7 and the stress hormones in Lesson 13.

It reliably lowers restraint around food, usually at night, when the pull is already strongest.

And the liver clearing it is the same liver doing everything in Lesson 11 — including converting your thyroid hormone into its active form.

You don't have to quit. But if your progress has stalled and there's a nightly drink in the picture, that's the first thing I'd test — and the test is two weeks without, not one. One week is mostly water weight.

## The "Fiber First" Rule: Food Sequencing
<!-- page: Lesson 9 -->

It is not just what you eat that determines your metabolic response; the order in which you eat your food plays a massive role in how your body handles blood sugar. This is known as food sequencing.

If you eat carbohydrates on an empty stomach, they are rapidly broken down and absorbed into the bloodstream, causing a sharp spike in glucose and a massive surge of insulin. However, if you apply the "Fiber First" rule—eating your fibrous vegetables and high-quality protein before you eat your carbohydrates—you completely change the biological response.

The fiber and protein create a physical matrix in your digestive tract, naturally slowing down gastric emptying. When the carbohydrates finally arrive, their absorption is drastically delayed. This simple shift in sequencing can significantly blunt your blood sugar and insulin spike, keeping your body in a stable, fat-burning state for much longer after a meal—without changing a single ingredient on your plate.

## Why Pizza Is Different
<!-- page: Lesson 9 -->

There's a reason certain combinations feel impossible to stop eating, and it isn't a character weakness.

When both fuels arrive at once, in quantity, your cells congest. Not because they can't use either one — because they can't commit to one while the other keeps arriving. The result is half-finished byproducts and cellular exhaust, and that exhaust interferes with insulin's signal directly. Lesson 4 covers what's happening inside the cell.

And nature almost never packages the two together. Nothing that grows is both high in fat and high in refined starch at high density. Bread and butter. Cheese and crackers. Pastries. Chips. Ice cream. That combination has to be manufactured.

There's a second reason those foods are hard to stop eating, and it has nothing to do with your cells. When researchers put people in a scanner, foods combining fat and refined carbohydrate were valued more than the sum of their parts — people worked harder and paid more for them, and it wasn't explained by calories or even by how much they said they liked the food.

So it's engineered on two levels. The combination overwhelms your cells, and it also overrides the signal that tells you to stop.

## Why Your Body Might Burn Fuel Differently Than Your Friend's
<!-- page: Lesson 9 -->

The Randle Cycle explains more than just what happens inside a single meal. It also explains why the exact same diet can produce wildly different results in two different people. When you eat fat, your body has a choice: burn that fat you just ate for immediate energy, or reach into your own stored body fat instead. Research shows your body actually prioritizes the fat sitting in your bloodstream from your last meal before it will touch what's stored on your hips, waist, or thighs.

This is why some people can eat generously on a ketogenic diet—plenty of fat, plenty of food—and still lose weight steadily. Their bodies are efficient at reaching past the dietary fat and pulling from stored reserves. Other people can eat the exact same way, stay in ketosis, avoid gaining weight, and still find their stored fat barely budges. Their bodies are simply better at burning what's freshly eaten than what's already stored.1

## Your Fat Cells Are Not a Storage Unit
<!-- page: Lesson 9 -->

You probably picture fat storage like a warehouse. Energy comes in, gets locked away, stays there until you burn it off. That isn't how it works at all.

Your fat cells are breaking down and rebuilding fat constantly — right now, and while you sleep.

How stored fat is built

Stored fat is called a triglyceride, and the name describes it: three fatty acids on one glycerol backbone. Think of the glycerol as a rack holding three items.

To store fat, your body needs both the fatty acids and the rack.

And here's the surprising part: when a fat cell breaks a triglyceride apart, it cannot reuse its own rack. Fat cells lack the enzyme needed to recycle glycerol. The rack gets released into your bloodstream and floats away.

So every time a fat cell stores fat again, it has to build a brand new rack from scratch.

Where the rack comes from

When you eat carbohydrate, your fat cells build racks easily — glucose is the raw material and it's right there. Carbohydrate makes storage easy.

When you don't, your fat cells build racks anyway — from lactate, from amino acids, from other available materials. The pathway was discovered in 1967.

And here's what should catch your attention: your body turns that pathway UP during fasting. The gene for the key enzyme is actively switched on when food is scarce.

Your body has a dedicated system for rebuilding fat, and it runs hardest when you're not eating.

Which explains the most frustrating thing about fat loss

In fasting humans, researchers have measured as much as 60% of the fatty acids released from fat cells being put straight back into storage.

Released. Not burned. Repackaged.

Scientists call this a futile cycle, and it literally is — it costs your body six units of energy currency to reattach three fatty acids. Your body spends energy to store fat it just released.

This is what's actually happening when people say they're in fat-burning mode and not losing fat.

You are releasing fat. Most of it is going back in. Whether your fat stores shrink depends on whether, across days and weeks, more comes out than goes back — not on whether the door is open.

So what determines the direction?

Two things, and they're both familiar.

How much fat is released — mostly insulin. Lower insulin, more release. Lessons 2 and 8.

How much gets burned versus repackaged — whether your tissues actually need the energy. If you've eaten enough fat to meet your needs, your body has no reason to burn what it just released. So it pays the cost and puts it back.

Which is the honest answer to a question that confuses a lot of people. If low-carb means low insulin, and low insulin means fat release, why am I not losing fat?

Because release isn't loss. You've opened the door, and your body is walking the fat right back through it — because you gave it enough dietary fat that it doesn't need yours.

So once you're fat-adapted, the lever is usually the added fats. The butter in the coffee, the extra oil, the fat bombs. Useful during the transition. Frequently the reason nothing moves afterward.

And one genuinely beautiful thing

Remember the rack has to leave the fat cell because it can't be reused there?

It goes to your liver — and your liver turns it into glucose.

So there's a complete loop running in your body right now:

Stored fat breaks apart → fatty acids and glycerolFatty acids → burned for energy, or converted to ketones for your brainGlycerol → to the liver → becomes the small amount of glucose your body still needs

That's how you maintain normal blood sugar on almost no dietary carbohydrate. Part of it is being made from the backbone of your own fat.

One correction worth making

You may have heard that you can't store fat without carbohydrate. Partly right mechanically, wrong as an absolute.

Carbohydrate makes building the rack much easier, so storage is easier when it's around. But your body can build the rack without it, and it will.

I'm telling you because I'd rather you hear it from me. There are no loopholes in your metabolism — only conditions that make one direction easier than the other. Lowering insulin makes release easier. That's real and valuable. It isn't a door that locks behind you.

## Fat Isn't All Stored in One Place
<!-- page: Lesson 9 -->

When we say "body fat," most people picture one thing. Your body keeps it in four places, and they behave completely differently.

In your fat cells — what you see in the mirror.

Inside your muscle cells — small droplets stored right next to the machinery that burns them. This one is a fuel tank, not a storage unit.

In your liver — where it causes problems if it piles up. Lesson 11.

In your blood — travelling to where it's needed. This is what a blood test measures.

Four separate compartments. More in one doesn't mean more in another. And one of them is actually a sign of fitness.

The Finding That Surprised Everyone

Fat inside muscle was assumed to be bad. In sedentary people it tracks reliably with insulin resistance.

Then researchers measured endurance athletes.

Athletes have as much fat inside their muscles as people with type 2 diabetes. And they're among the most insulin-sensitive people you can find.

Same fat. Same place. Opposite health. Scientists called it the athlete's paradox, and it took ten years to solve.

And the Answer Changes How You Should Think About This

Stored fat inside a muscle cell is harmless. It's a fuel tank.

The damage happens when fatty acids get turned into other compounds instead — and trained muscle has far fewer of those. Lesson 10.

Then researchers found something that flips the intuition. The faster someone was packaging fat into storage inside her muscle, the healthier her blood sugar was.

Storing fat safely is how your muscle protects itself. A fatty acid parked as storage can't be turned into something harmful.

So the Goal Was Never "Less Fat"

Stored fat isn't your enemy. It's your energy system. What you want isn't less of it — it's fat that moves. In and out, stored where it belongs, used when needed.

A trained woman has more fat inside her muscles than an untrained one and is metabolically healthier. Because hers is cycling through instead of sitting there.

It isn't how much you store. It's whether it moves.

One thing so this doesn't confuse you: your blood triglycerides are a different pool, and lower really is better there. Exercise lowers those, because trained muscle gets much better at pulling fat out of your bloodstream and using it. Two pools, opposite directions, both good.

Efficient Stored-Fat Burners

Some bodies are highly efficient at reaching past recently eaten fat to tap into stored reserves. These individuals often see steady fat loss on a well-formulated low-carb or ketogenic diet, even with generous fat intake.

Dietary-Fat Preferential Burners

Other bodies preferentially burn whatever fat was just eaten before ever reaching stored fat. These individuals may stay weight-stable on a high-fat diet without much fat loss, even without gaining weight.

This isn't a character flaw or a sign you're doing something wrong. Genuine genetic differences exist in the enzymes and transporters responsible for fat-burning capacity,2 and a history of past weight loss and regain can also reduce how efficiently your body burns fat, even at the very same body weight as someone who has never struggled with their weight. This is one of the many reasons a truly personalized approach matters more than a one-size-fits-all rule.

Fat burning and fat loss are not automatically the same thing. Your body can be actively burning fat all day and still not be touching your stored reserves, if it keeps reaching for what you just ate instead.

## Exercise and the Randle Cycle
<!-- page: Lesson 9 -->

Exercise adds another layer to this same competition. When you exercise, especially with resistance training or higher-intensity movement, you deplete the glycogen (stored carbohydrate) in your muscles. This temporarily makes your muscles unusually receptive to absorbing carbohydrates without the same insulin spike they'd normally trigger. In practical terms, the day you exercise hard is often the day your body is best equipped to handle the carbohydrates in your meal.3

Beyond meal composition and exercise timing, several other factors can prevent your body from reaching a fat-burning state, even on a well-formulated low-carb plan. We cover these in depth in Lesson 13, including chronic stress and elevated cortisol, poor sleep, a history of chronic dieting, systemic inflammation, and certain medications.

- Eating fiber and protein before carbohydrates physically blunts post-meal blood sugar and insulin spikes by slowing gastric emptying.
- The combination overwhelms your cells, and it also overrides the signal that tells you to stop.
