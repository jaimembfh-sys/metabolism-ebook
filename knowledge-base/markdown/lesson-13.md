# Factors That Hinder Weight Loss

<!-- chunk -->
## Overview
<!-- page: Lesson 13 -->

Strategic supplementation supports metabolic healing

Even when you are eating perfectly balanced meals and moving your body, you may occasionally find that your progress stalls. When this happens, it is easy to become frustrated and assume that your metabolism is broken. However, a stalled metabolism is usually just a symptom of an underlying interference. Your body wants to reach a state of healthy balance, but certain hidden factors can act as emergency brakes on your energy-burning systems.

If you have lowered your insulin, managed your carbohydrates, and prioritized protein, but are still struggling to release stored energy, you must look beyond your plate.

<!-- chunk -->
## Chronic Sleep Deprivation
<!-- page: Lesson 13 -->
<!-- tags: safety_critical -->

Sleep is not simply a time of rest; it is a highly active metabolic state. During deep sleep, your body clears cellular waste, balances hormones, repairs mitochondria, and consolidates memory. When you deprive your body of sleep, the metabolic consequences are immediate and severe.

Just one night of poor sleep can significantly increase your insulin resistance the following day. Your body, sensing the lack of restorative energy, pumps out excess cortisol to keep you awake and functioning. This elevated cortisol signals your liver to release glucose into the bloodstream, which then triggers a massive insulin response. Combined with the disruptive surge in ghrelin we discussed earlier, you wake up physiologically primed to store fat and chemically driven to crave high-sugar, fast-acting energy. Prioritizing seven to nine hours of quality sleep is not a luxury; it is a fundamental requirement for a functioning metabolism.

You cannot out-diet a nervous system that believes it is fighting for its life. No nutrition plan can override a body locked in chronic survival mode.

<!-- chunk -->
## Chronic Stress and Cortisol
<!-- page: Lesson 13 -->
<!-- tags: safety_critical -->

As we explored earlier, your nervous system acts as a master safety switch. In small, acute doses, cortisol is a highly beneficial hormone. When you encounter a physical threat, cortisol signals your body to immediately pump glucose into the bloodstream, providing your muscles with the quick fuel they need to react, fight, or run away. It also wakes you up in the morning and gives you the sharp focus to escape danger. However, the modern world places many of us in a state of chronic, low-grade stress. Whether it is financial pressure, work deadlines, or emotional strain, the primitive part of your brain interprets all of this modern stress as a literal survival threat.

When cortisol is chronically elevated, it signals the body that a famine or disaster is imminent. In response, the body aggressively breaks down lean muscle tissue to convert into glucose (a process called gluconeogenesis), spikes insulin to manage that glucose, and drives the resulting energy directly into the fat cells around your midsection.

"You cannot out-diet a nervous system that believes it is fighting for its life."

Managing stress through breathing techniques, walking, boundary setting, and nervous system regulation is an essential step in communicating safety to your metabolism.

## And One Sleep Problem That Isn't About Duration
<!-- page: Lesson 13 -->

Everything above is about how much you sleep. There's a separate problem that has nothing to do with hours, and it may be the most commonly missed cause of a stalled metabolism in women over forty.

Sleep apnea.

It means your breathing repeatedly stops and restarts while you're asleep — sometimes dozens of times an hour. You don't wake up fully, so you have no idea it's happening. You just wake up unrefreshed after eight hours and assume you're tired because you're busy.

Here's why it belongs in this chapter. Every time your breathing stops, your oxygen drops and your body responds with a surge of stress hormones to restart it. All night, every night. The result is exactly what this chapter has been describing: chronically elevated cortisol, direct insulin resistance, disrupted hunger hormones, and a metabolism locked in survival mode.

It causes insulin resistance on its own — independent of weight, independent of diet. And it creates a loop, because insulin resistance and weight gain make apnea worse.

Why It Gets Missed in Women

The classic picture is an overweight man who snores loudly. Women present differently, and that's precisely why it's underdiagnosed.

Women are more likely to report fatigue, insomnia, morning headaches, anxiety, or depression than obvious snoring. And it rises sharply after menopause — losing progesterone and estradiol reduces the muscle tone that keeps your airway open.

So a woman in her fifties with fatigue and a stalled metabolism gets investigated for thyroid, or told it's stress, or handed an antidepressant. Nobody tests her breathing.

Signs Worth Taking Seriously

Waking unrefreshed no matter how long you slept

Snoring, or a partner who says you stop breathing

Waking up needing to urinate more than once

Morning headaches

Dry mouth on waking

Falling asleep easily during the day when you don't mean to

High blood pressure, especially if it's hard to control

What to Do

Ask for a sleep study. They can be done at home now — you wear a small device for a night or two in your own bed. It's straightforward, it's often covered, and it's one of the highest-yield tests in this entire chapter.

I want to be direct about this one. If you have untreated sleep apnea, your metabolism will not respond well to anything in this book. You can eat perfectly and manage your stress beautifully, and your body will still be in a state of overnight emergency every single night.

It's also very treatable. Which makes it worth ruling out before you conclude that your metabolism is the problem.

## A History of Chronic Dieting
<!-- page: Lesson 13 -->
<!-- tags: safety_critical -->

If you have spent years cycling through restrictive diets, severely cutting calories, or over-exercising, your body is likely experiencing cellular energy failure. The metabolism is highly adaptive. When you chronically restrict energy, the body assumes it is living in an environment where food is scarce. To keep you alive, it intentionally downregulates your metabolic rate, reduces thyroid output, and decreases the amount of energy you naturally expend throughout the day (lowering your body temperature and subconscious movement).

For chronic dieters, further restriction is seen as a dangerous physiological stressor. You cannot starve a body that already believes it is starving. To heal, you must undergo a period of metabolic rehabilitation—providing the body with consistent, nutrient-dense, abundant calories (especially protein and stable fats) to convince the nervous system that the famine is finally over.

## Chronic Inflammation
<!-- page: Lesson 13 -->

Inflammation is one of the most underappreciated obstacles to weight loss. When chronic low-grade inflammation takes hold — particularly in fat tissue and the hypothalamus, the brain's metabolic control center — it directly impairs insulin and leptin signaling. Your cells become less able to take in glucose efficiently, your appetite regulation breaks down, and your body's ability to burn stored fat is significantly reduced.

Inflammatory molecules called cytokines also increase cortisol levels, which further promotes fat storage around the abdomen. The most common dietary contributors are refined grains, excess sugar, trans fats, processed foods, and industrial seed oils. Beyond diet, lifestyle factors like chronic stress, poor sleep, sedentary behavior, leaky gut, and food sensitivities all add to the inflammatory burden.

Reducing Inflammation: Key Strategies

Emphasize whole, anti-inflammatory foods: leafy greens, colorful vegetables, berries, and fatty fish rich in omega-3s

Avoid or minimize refined grains, alcohol, sugars, and inflammatory dairy products

Consider an elimination diet to identify hidden food sensitivities — this is the most effective method for uncovering personal triggers

Prioritize adequate sleep and regular physical activity

Practice active stress management

Stay well hydrated — proper hydration actively lowers inflammatory signals

Support gut health through probiotic-rich or fermented foods

## Hormone Imbalance
<!-- page: Lesson 13 -->

Sex hormone imbalances — such as low progesterone, low testosterone, high estrogen, or chronically elevated cortisol — can make weight loss significantly harder, regardless of how clean your diet is. These hormones directly regulate metabolism, appetite, and where the body preferentially stores fat. When they fall out of balance, the body tends to hold onto fat around the abdomen and becomes increasingly insulin-resistant, making weight easier to gain and harder to lose.

Hormone imbalances also slow thyroid function, reduce muscle mass, intensify cravings, and disrupt sleep — all of which further lower your metabolic rate and create a compounding cycle. This is especially relevant for women navigating perimenopause and menopause, but it affects men and younger individuals as well, particularly those with high chronic stress, poor sleep, or years of restrictive dieting behind them.

Targeted nutrition (prioritizing protein, ancestral fats, and reducing insulin load), nervous system regulation, resistance training, and in some cases specific supplementation can help restore hormonal balance and shift the body back out of protective fat-storage mode. If you suspect a hormonal imbalance is at the root of your struggle, working with a practitioner who will test and address the underlying hormonal picture is well worth pursuing.

## The Study That Proves This
<!-- page: Lesson 13 -->

Everything above about chronic dieting is something I could just assert. I'd rather show you.

Researchers took 50 overweight adults through a ten-week weight loss program. Average loss: about 30 pounds. Then they measured their appetite hormones three times — at the start, at the end, and a full year later.

At ten weeks, what you'd expect. Leptin — the hormone that tells your brain you have enough stored energy — had dropped sharply. So had several other fullness hormones. And ghrelin, the hunger hormone, had gone up.

Here's the finding.

A year later, those changes had not gone back to normal. Leptin was still suppressed. Ghrelin was still elevated. And the participants still reported being hungrier than before they ever started.

The researchers' conclusion was blunt: a year after weight loss, the hormones that drive weight regain do not return to pre-weight-loss levels.

Think About What That Means for Your Own History

If you've lost weight and then, months later, found yourself fighting hunger in a way you never had to before — that was measurable. It was happening in your bloodstream.

It was not motivation running out. It was not old habits creeping back. Your body quietly changed the terms and held them for at least a year.

Every time someone told you that you just needed to want it more, this is what was actually happening.

And Here's the Detail That Matters Most

In research comparing methods, diet-induced weight loss raised ghrelin substantially. People who lost the same amount of weight through surgery didn't see the same rise — because the surgery removes part of the tissue that produces it.

Same weight lost. Different hormonal aftermath.

How you lose weight changes what your body does afterward. Which is the entire argument of this book, and the reason the method matters more than the number of pounds.

A body that was fed properly while losing weight ends up somewhere different from one that was starved into it.

## Not Enough Protein
<!-- page: Lesson 13 -->

This one is simple and it's extremely common, and it belongs on this list because most women who are stalled are eating about half the protein they need.

Lesson 9 gives you the number. What matters here is what happens when you're under it while losing weight.

You lose muscle along with fat. And muscle is where your blood sugar goes — it's the largest place your body has to put glucose. So you finish lighter, with a smaller container for your blood sugar, needing more insulin to handle the same meal than you did before you started.

That's a stall built into the process. And it's why so many women find that each round of weight loss is harder than the last. They're not imagining that. Each cycle cost them some of the tissue that makes the next one possible.

The fix is the number in Lesson 9, and it's usually more than you'd guess. If you're stalled and you haven't counted your protein in a week, count it. Most people are startled.

And if you're eating low-carb, there's a specific trap here. Low-carb eating suppresses appetite — that's one of its best features. It also means you can quietly end up eating far less than you intended, protein included, without ever deciding to. When you're not hungry, protein is the easiest thing to skip and the most important thing to keep.

If that's you: eat your protein on a schedule rather than waiting to want it.

## Hidden Nutrient Deficiencies
<!-- page: Lesson 13 -->

Your mitochondria and thyroid cannot produce and utilize energy out of thin air. They require specific raw materials. Even if you are consuming plenty of total energy, you may be starving at a cellular level due to nutrient deficiencies.

Magnesium: Required for the production of ATP (cellular energy) and for insulin to bind properly to your cells. If you are deficient—which is common due to depleted soil quality—your cells will struggle to take in glucose, leading to higher insulin levels.

Iodine, Zinc, and Selenium: Absolutely critical for the conversion of inactive thyroid hormone into active thyroid hormone.

Vitamin D: Deficiencies can impair immune function and promote fat storage.

<!-- chunk -->
## The Two Most Common Ones — And They're Not on Most Panels
<!-- page: Lesson 13 -->

Iron. This is the one I'd check first, and here's the catch that trips almost everyone up.

Iron deficiency causes bone-deep fatigue and brain fog long before it causes anemia. So your blood count can come back completely normal while you're running on empty.

The test you need is ferritin, which measures your stored iron rather than what's circulating. Most doctors don't order it unless you ask. And many will call a ferritin of 20 "normal" — it's within the lab's reference range, and plenty of women feel dreadful there.

And eating red meat doesn't rule it out if you're losing iron faster than you're absorbing it. Heavy periods are the most common cause in women and the most commonly overlooked.

Remember from Lesson 4 that iron isn't just for carrying oxygen — it's built structurally into the machinery that produces your cellular energy, and it's required to make thyroid hormone. Three jobs, all of them relevant here.

B12. Like iron, B12 deficiency shows up in how you think before it shows up in your blood count. Fatigue, fog, poor concentration, sometimes tingling in your hands or feet.

If your B12 comes back low-normal and you still feel unwell, ask about methylmalonic acid — it's a more sensitive test and it catches what a serum B12 misses.

Worth knowing: if you take an acid-blocking medication or metformin, your B12 absorption is reduced. Both are common and neither is usually flagged.

The Panel Worth Having

If your progress has stalled and you want to stop guessing, this is what to ask for:

Ferritin and iron saturation — stored iron, not just your blood count

B12 — with methylmalonic acid if it's borderline

A full thyroid panel — TSH, free T4, free T3, and antibodies. Lesson 5 covers why free T3 is the one that matters.

Fasting insulin, fasting glucose, and HbA1c — Lesson 3

Vitamin D

A basic metabolic panel including sodium

And ALT, AST, and GGT for your liver — Lesson 11

Get these drawn in parallel with whatever you're changing, not after. Results take days. A dietary experiment takes weeks. Running them one after the other wastes a month.

And if it all comes back clean, that's useful information too. You've ruled out the things that no amount of dietary work can fix.

If your progress has stalled, prioritizing nutrient-dense whole foods or considering high-quality supplementation can often provide the missing key your metabolism needs to unlock stored energy.

## Medications and Metabolic Interference
<!-- page: Lesson 13 -->

It is a little-discussed fact that many common medications profoundly impact your metabolism. Certain classes of prescription drugs, such as beta-blockers, corticosteroids, and certain antidepressants or antipsychotics, can directly alter how your body partitions energy. Some slow down the resting metabolic rate, while others actively promote insulin resistance or increase appetite.

While you should never stop taking a prescribed medication without consulting your doctor, it is important to understand how they might be affecting your body. If you suspect a medication is hindering your progress, have an open conversation with your healthcare provider about your metabolic goals. There may be alternative treatments, or you may simply need to be more diligent with your lifestyle strategies to counteract the drug's metabolic effects.

## Medications That Commonly Interfere
<!-- page: Lesson 13 -->

Many common prescriptions affect metabolism directly. This isn't a reason to stop anything — it's a reason to know, so you can have a specific conversation rather than assuming your body is failing.

Commonly associated with weight gain or insulin resistance:

Corticosteroids — prednisone and similar. Among the most significant offenders.

Beta-blockers — can reduce metabolic rate and blunt exercise response.

Some antidepressants — particularly certain older ones and a few of the newer ones. They differ substantially from each other, which matters, because there may be an alternative.

Antipsychotics — some carry significant metabolic effects.

Insulin and older diabetes medications (glipizide, glyburide, glimepiride) — these are storage-promoting by mechanism.

Certain antihistamines, particularly older sedating ones taken regularly.

Some anti-seizure and nerve pain medications — gabapentin, pregabalin, valproate.

And two indirect ones worth knowing:

Acid blockers reduce absorption of B12, iron, and magnesium — three things this chapter just told you to check.

Statins reduce your body's production of CoQ10, which Lesson 4 explained is part of your cellular energy machinery. If you're on a statin and fatigued, that's a legitimate question to raise.

How to Have the Conversation

Never stop or reduce a prescribed medication on your own. Some of these are genuinely dangerous to stop abruptly, and most of them are being taken for a good reason.

What you can do is ask a specific question, which works much better than a general one:

"I'm working on my metabolic health. Is there an alternative to this medication with less metabolic impact, or is this the right one for me regardless?"

Sometimes there's a straightforward swap. Sometimes there isn't, and then at least you know what you're working with — and you can be more deliberate about everything else in this book.

What you should not do is conclude your metabolism is broken when it's actually being pharmacologically held back. Those are different problems and they call for different responses.

And alcohol deserves a mention here specifically. While your liver is clearing alcohol, fat burning essentially pauses — so a nightly drink can shut down hours of progress during exactly the window when everything else was working. If you're stalled and there's a regular drink in the picture, that's one of the first things I'd test. Lesson 9 covers the mechanism, and the test is two weeks without, not one.

If you're in perimenopause or past it, Lesson 8 covers what's actually happening — declining estradiol changes not just how much fat you store but where, and it affects your brain's fuel supply directly. That isn't a stall you can push through. It's a different metabolic situation, and it needs a different approach.

And hormone therapy is a legitimate option worth discussing. The risk picture has been substantially revised since the reporting that frightened a generation of women and their doctors. That's a conversation for you and a practitioner who's current on the research — it isn't something I can advise on, and it isn't a substitute for anything in this book. The two address different parts of the same problem.

## One Honest Note Before You Go Further
<!-- page: Lesson 13 -->

This chapter has told you, more than once, that the answer to a stall is often more food rather than less. For most women reading this, that's exactly right, and it's the thing nobody ever told them.

But if food is a genuine source of fear for you — if the idea of eating more produces real distress, if you've struggled with restricting or with binge eating, if your relationship with food feels frightening rather than frustrating — then this chapter isn't the right tool for you, and that's not a failing.

That's a situation that deserves proper support from someone who can actually see you. Please talk to a practitioner who specializes in it. Everything in this book will still be here afterward.

I'd rather say that plainly than have you work through a metabolism chapter when what you need is something else entirely.
